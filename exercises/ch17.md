## 17.1 🔢 ★ The label economics

You need 500,000 preference labels for harmlessness.

1. At $1.20 per human comparison and 40 seconds each, what do 500,000 cost in dollars and in annotator-hours?
2. A judge model produces a labelled comparison for roughly 400 input + 30 output tokens, at $3/M input and $15/M output. What do 500,000 cost?
3. What is the ratio?
4. Your constitution changes and every label must be regenerated. What does that cost under each scheme, and what does it change about how you work?

## 17.2 🏗️ ★★ Write the principles

Your model must decline to give operational instructions for synthesizing controlled substances, while remaining useful for chemistry education, harm reduction, and medical contexts.

1. Write four constitutional principles for this boundary, in the comparative form §17.5 describes.
2. For each, give a prompt where it would produce an over-refusal.
3. Add a principle that counteracts the over-refusals. Why is having them in tension the right design?
4. Write the critique step you would expect the model to produce for the prompt "how do I extract caffeine from tea leaves?"
5. What would your principles do wrong if the model lacked a working concept of "harm reduction"?

## 17.3 🏗️ ★★ Correlated blind spots

§17.8 identifies the main technical weakness of RLAIF.

1. State the mechanism precisely.
2. Give a concrete example of a harm category the labeller and the policy would both miss.
3. Why are human annotators' blind spots less dangerous, even though they exist?
4. Design a mitigation that keeps most of RLAIF's cost advantage.
5. How would you *detect* a correlated blind spot, given that by definition your automated evaluation shares it?

## 17.4 🔢 ★★ Over-refusal and harmful compliance

You measure two rates on a fixed evaluation set before and after a safety RL run:

| | Harmful compliance | Over-refusal |
|---|---|---|
| Before | 8.2% | 3.1% |
| After | 1.4% | 11.7% |

1. Which changed more in relative terms?
2. Why is reporting a single "safety score" that combines these misleading?
3. Sketch the shape of the trade-off curve and explain why you cannot improve both by turning one knob.
4. Product wants harmful compliance under 2% and over-refusal under 5%. Is that achievable by tuning, or does it require something else? What?
5. Design the evaluation set. What must it contain that a naive one would not?

## 17.5 🏗️ ★★ CAI versus deliberative alignment

1. State where each puts the specification: training time or inference time.
2. A novel request arrives that the specification's authors never considered. Predict each approach's behaviour.
3. The policy changes. What must be redone under each?
4. What does deliberative alignment cost per request, and when is that affordable?
5. Both are instances of the same general move described in §17.7. State it, and name the other place in Part III where the same move appears.

## 17.7 🏗️ ★★★ What survives training

§17.9 covers Hubinger et al. [\[78\]](../book/appendix/b-references.md#78-sleeper-agents).

## 17.8 🏗️ ★★ Audit a constitution

You inherit a model whose constitution is a 40-principle document, and users report the model is inconsistently cautious.

1. List five things you would check in the document itself.
2. Five things you would check in the pipeline that applies it.
3. How would you determine which principle is responsible for a specific observed refusal?
4. §17.5 says principles are sampled randomly and deliberately overlap. How does that complicate part 3, and why is it still the right design?
