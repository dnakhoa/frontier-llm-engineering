## 11.1 🔢 The packing win

**1. Padded token count.**

$$800{,}000 \times 8{,}192 = 6.55 \times 10^9 \text{ tokens}$$

**2. Real content.**

$$800{,}000 \times 720 = 5.76 \times 10^8 \text{ tokens}$$

**3. Packed.**

Perfect packing processes only real content, so **5.76 × 10⁸ tokens**, in $5.76\times10^8 / 8{,}192 \approx 70{,}300$ sequences instead of 800,000.

**4. Speedup, and the effect of dropping long examples.**

$$\frac{6.55 \times 10^9}{5.76 \times 10^8} = 11.4\times$$

Now drop the ~1% of examples longer than 8,192 tokens. The instinct is that this hurts the packing advantage. It does the opposite.

The padded baseline pads to the *context length*, not to the longest example — so removing 8,000 examples reduces the baseline by exactly $8{,}000 \times 8{,}192 = 6.55\times10^7$ tokens, or 1%. But those 8,000 examples are the longest ones in the corpus. If the top 1% averages ~12,000 tokens, they carry $9.6\times10^7$ tokens — **16.7% of all real content**.

$$\text{new speedup} = \frac{792{,}000 \times 8{,}192}{5.76\times10^8 - 9.6\times10^7} = \frac{6.49\times10^9}{4.80\times10^8} = 13.5\times$$

The speedup goes **up**, because you removed exactly the examples that were closest to filling a context on their own — the ones for which padding wasted the least.

The trap this exercise sets: the packing multiplier is a property of your *length distribution*, not of packing as a technique. Quoting "packing gives 3×" or "packing gives 11×" without the distribution is meaningless. Measure yours.

And note what dropping those examples actually costs: 16.7% of your content, concentrated in the long-context examples that §11.7 warns you not to lose. Truncation or context-parallel training is usually the better answer than dropping.

## 11.2 🔢 Epoch budget

**1. Tokens.** With packing there is essentially no padding waste:

$$1.2\times10^6 \text{ examples} \times 720 \text{ tokens} \times 3 \text{ epochs} = 2.59\times10^9 \text{ tokens}$$

**2. FLOPs.**

$$6ND = 6 \times 70\times10^9 \times 2.59\times10^9 = 1.09\times10^{21} \text{ FLOPs}$$

**3. Wall clock.**

Aggregate throughput: $64 \times 990\times10^{12} \times 0.40 = 2.53\times10^{16}$ FLOP/s.

$$\frac{1.09\times10^{21}}{2.53\times10^{16}} = 4.30\times10^4 \text{ s} \approx 12 \text{ hours}$$

**4. Fraction of pre-training compute.**

Pre-training: $6 \times 70\times10^9 \times 15\times10^{12} = 6.3\times10^{24}$ FLOPs.

$$\frac{1.09\times10^{21}}{6.3\times10^{24}} = 1.7\times10^{-4} \approx 0.017\%$$

**SFT is roughly 1/5,800th of the pre-training compute.** Twelve hours on 64 GPUs against a run that consumed months on thousands.

This is the number the exercise exists for. It reframes everything in Chapter 11:

- **Cost is not the constraint.** You can run 40 SFT ablations for a rounding error against the pre-training bill. This is why post-training teams iterate on *mixtures* rather than on efficiency — the experiment is cheap, so the bottleneck is deciding what to test and evaluating the result.
- **The leverage is enormous.** 0.017% of the compute determines whether the model is usable at all.
- **It explains where the money actually goes in post-training**, which is not the SFT training step. It is data generation (Exercise 11.6) and evaluation. A single rejection-sampling round can cost more GPU-hours than the SFT run it feeds.

## 11.3 🔢 Where did the supervision go?

Per-turn token totals, including markers and `<|end|>`:

| Turn | Role | Content | Marker | End | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | user | 40 | 1 | — | 41 |
| 2 | assistant | 120 | 1 | 1 | 122 |
| 3 | user | 25 | 1 | — | 26 |
| 4 | assistant | 200 | 1 | 1 | 202 |
| 5 | user | 60 | 1 | — | 61 |
| 6 | assistant | 150 | 1 | 1 | 152 |
| | | | | | **604** |

**1. Correct.** Assistant content plus each `<|end|>`; role markers masked (§11.4 — the marker is emitted by the serving harness, so learning it produces a spurious duplicate):

$$(120+1) + (200+1) + (150+1) = \mathbf{473}$$

**2. `no_mask`.** Everything: **604**.

**3. `last_turn`.** Turn 6 only: $150 + 1 = \mathbf{151}$.

**4. `no_eos`.** Correct minus the three stop tokens: $473 - 3 = \mathbf{470}$.

**Fraction surviving under `last_turn`:**

$$\frac{151}{473} = 31.9\%$$

**You wasted 68% of your annotation budget.** If those conversations cost \$4 each to produce, you paid for 1.2M dollars of data and trained on 380K dollars of it.

And the loss curve looks completely normal. That is the entire lesson of §11.4: the bugs that announce themselves are cheap; this one is not.

Note also that `no_eos` looks almost harmless by this metric — 470 vs 473 tokens, a 0.6% difference — and yet it produces a model that never stops generating. **Supervision volume and supervision correctness are different things.** Three tokens out of 473 carried the entire stopping behaviour.

## 11.4 🏗️ Design an SFT mixture

There is no single right answer. Here is a defensible one, with the reasoning that matters more than the numbers.

**1. Mixture**

| Category | Examples | Share |
|---|---|---|
| General chat / instruction-following (EN) | 150,000 | 25% |
| Code — generation, editing, explanation, debugging | 132,000 | 22% |
| Math and quantitative reasoning | 78,000 | 13% |
| Vietnamese (chat + task, native-authored where possible) | 60,000 | 10% |
| Japanese (chat + task) | 54,000 | 9% |
| Long-context (summarization, multi-doc QA, repo-scale code) | 48,000 | 8% |
| Tool / function calling (incl. multi-step and error recovery) | 42,000 | 7% |
| Safety — refusals **paired with** benign near-misses | 24,000 | 4% |
| Structured output (JSON, schema adherence) | 12,000 | 2% |
| **Total** | **600,000** | |

**2. Sources**

- **Code (132K):** predominantly rejection sampling with *execution* as the verifier — generate $k$, run the tests, keep what passes (§11.10). This is the category where verification is cheapest and therefore where synthetic data is most trustworthy. Supplement with permissively-licensed repository data converted to instruction shape.
- **Math (78K):** rejection sampling against an answer key, plus Evol-Instruct-style difficulty escalation ([\[55\]](../../book/appendix/b-references.md#55-evol-instruct)) on seed problems. Keep the reasoning traces, not only final answers.
- **General chat (150K):** model-generated and filtered, seeded from real user-prompt distributions. This is the category where filter quality matters most and where you have the least verification signal.
- **Vietnamese / Japanese (114K):** the hard one. Translated English data produces *translationese* — grammatically fine, culturally wrong, and it teaches the model that these languages are English in costume. Budget at least a third of each language for natively-authored data, which means human annotators. This is where your human-annotation money should go.
- **Long-context (48K):** converted from existing corpora (documents, repositories, meeting transcripts) with synthetic questions over them. Cheap to construct, and §11.7 says you cannot skip it without losing the capability Chapter 9 paid for.
- **Tools (42K):** synthetic against real API schemas, including deliberate failure paths — malformed arguments, tool errors, results that do not answer the question. A model that has only seen tools succeed handles failure by hallucinating.
- **Safety (24K):** human-written for the four required categories. Critically, **paired**: for every refusal, a benign near-miss with a helpful answer. Unpaired refusal data is how you get a model that will not explain how to kill a process (§11.9).

**3. Three trade-offs made explicit**

- **114K multilingual examples bought at the cost of English quality.** At 30B parameters capacity is finite, and Chapter 11 §11.9 is direct about this. The choice was made because shipping in three markets is a product requirement, not because it is free.
- **8% long-context bought at the cost of general chat volume.** Long examples are also token-expensive — 48K long-context examples may consume as many tokens as 150K chat examples, so the *token* share of this category is far above its example share. Budget in tokens, not examples.
- **Code weighted above math (22% vs 13%).** Both are verifiable and both benefit from rejection sampling, but the brief says "strong at code, competent at math." This is the brief translated into a number, which is the actual job.

**4. The first ablation**

**The multilingual share**, run as three SFT runs at 0%, 10%, and 19% multilingual, evaluated on English capability *and* on native-speaker judgement in each target language.

Why this one first: it is the largest single trade-off in the mixture, the cost side is well-measured by existing English benchmarks, and the benefit side requires evaluation infrastructure you should build before you need it rather than after. Every other category's ablation is cheaper to run later; this one gates a hiring and annotation decision with a long lead time.

**5. The length problem**

In SFT, response length is controlled almost entirely by *what is in the data*. Three levers:

- Filter the mixture on length, conditioned on task type. A short answer to "what is the capital of France" and a long answer to "explain this codebase" are both correct; a global length cap is the wrong instrument.
- Include explicit length-instruction data ("in one sentence…", "briefly…") so the model learns length is controllable rather than fixed.
- During rejection sampling, break ties toward the shorter response. This is nearly free and it is the single highest-leverage intervention.

**Why SFT is the better place than RL for this:** length bias in RL comes from the reward model preferring long responses (§12.6), and fixing it there means fixing your reward model, which is harder and riskier. In SFT you are directly choosing what the model imitates. It is a data filter, not an objective change.

**Why SFT is the worse place:** SFT can only shorten responses *on distribution*. It cannot teach the model to reason about when brevity is appropriate for a prompt type it has not seen. RL with a length-aware reward generalizes better — which is why in practice you do both, and why Chapter 15 shows RL pushing length back *up* when you let it.

## 11.5 🏗️ Diagnose eight failures

| # | Symptom | Most likely cause | Five-minute check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Benchmarks up, humans say worse | Format overfitting — too many epochs (§11.7, §11.11) | Read 20 responses side by side with the previous generation. Look for stereotyped openers, forced bullet lists, uniform paragraph counts. Then check the epoch count. |
| 2 | Fine in eval, worse in product | Chat-template mismatch (§11.6) | Dump the exact token ids the eval harness sends and the exact ids the product sends for the same conversation. Diff them. It will be whitespace. |
| 3 | Stray `<\|assistant\|>` in output | Role marker included in the loss (§11.4) | Run the bracketed label printout on ten examples. The marker must be bracketed. |
| 4 | MMLU 79.1 → 74.3 | LR too high, or mixture lacks knowledge-preserving data (§11.7) | Check the LR against 1e-5–2e-5. Then re-run at half the LR for one epoch and see if the drop shrinks — that isolates LR from mixture in one run. |
| 5 | Refuses "kill a zombie process" | Unpaired safety data (§11.9) | Grep the safety subset for benign near-misses of each refusal category. If refusals outnumber paired benign examples heavily, that is the cause. |
| 6 | Long-document quality below base | No long examples in the mixture (§11.7) | Histogram the SFT set's sequence lengths. If nothing exceeds 4K and the model claims 128K, that is it. |
| 7 | 22K tokens/sec vs a colleague's 140K | No packing (§11.5) | Compute the fraction of tokens in a batch that are padding. 6.4× is suspiciously close to a typical padding ratio. |
| 8 | Multi-turn degrades after turn 3 | `last_turn` masking bug (§11.4, Ex 11.3) | Bracketed printout on a **multi-turn** example specifically. Earlier assistant turns will be bracketed. |

The meta-lesson: **seven of these eight are checkable in minutes by looking directly at the data or the tokens**, and none require retraining. The instinct to reach for a hyperparameter sweep before decoding a batch is the most expensive habit in post-training.

## 11.6 🔢 Rejection sampling economics

**1. $k = 8$.**

$$200{,}000 \times 8 \times 600 = 9.6\times10^8 \text{ generated tokens}$$
$$\frac{9.6\times10^8}{2{,}400 \text{ tok/s/GPU}} = 4.0\times10^5 \text{ GPU-s} = 111 \text{ GPU-hours}$$

Scoring adds 1/20th: **≈ 117 GPU-hours**, which on 128 GPUs is about **55 minutes** of wall clock.

**2. $k = 64$.** Eight times the generation: **≈ 933 GPU-hours**, about **7.3 hours** on 128 GPUs.

**3. Coverage at 2% per-sample success.**

$$P(\text{at least one correct}) = 1 - (1-p)^k$$

- $k=8$: $1 - 0.98^{8} = 1 - 0.851 = \mathbf{14.9\%}$
- $k=64$: $1 - 0.98^{64} = 1 - 0.274 = \mathbf{72.6\%}$

**4. Deep or wide?**

The two options at equal budget:

- **$k=64$ on 200K prompts:** $200{,}000 \times 0.726 = 145{,}200$ prompts solved.
- **$k=8$ on 1.6M prompts:** $1{,}600{,}000 \times 0.149 = 238{,}400$ prompts solved.

Going wide yields **64% more training examples for the same compute**. If your only metric is volume, wide wins, and it is not close.

But volume is the wrong metric, for a reason worth being precise about. The 2% figure is a *per-prompt* property, and prompts are not homogeneous. Across a realistic prompt set, per-prompt success probability is bimodal: most prompts are near 90%, a minority near 0%. Under $k=8$ you harvest almost exclusively from the easy mode — and you are training the model on problems it *already solves*, which teaches it very little.

The prompts where $k=64$ finds a solution and $k=8$ does not are precisely the ones at the frontier of the model's ability. Those are the training examples that move capability.

So the real answer depends on a fact the problem does not give you:

- **Prompt supply is abundant** (you can source 1.6M quality prompts): go wide, then run a *second* deep pass at high $k$ on only the prompts the wide pass failed. This is the standard two-stage approach and it dominates both pure strategies.
- **Prompt supply is scarce** (200K is what you have, as in a specialist domain): go deep. You have no alternative use for the compute.
- **You care about a specific hard capability**: go deep on that slice regardless of overall supply.

The two-stage version is what makes this worth working through: you spend $k=8$ to *identify* the hard prompts cheaply, then spend $k=64$ only on those. Total cost is far below uniform $k=64$ and coverage of hard prompts is far above uniform $k=8$.

## 11.7 📄 DeepSeek-V3 post-training

Read the SFT section of arXiv:2412.19437 before checking these.

**1.** The report describes a dataset of roughly **1.5 million instances** spanning multiple domains, with distinct treatment for reasoning-oriented data (math, code, logic) versus non-reasoning data (creative writing, role-play, simple QA).

**2. The R1-generates-data-for-V3 problem.** R1-style reasoning output is long, contains extensive deliberation, and is often formatted for a reasoning-model output convention rather than for a general assistant. Training V3 directly on raw R1 output would give V3 R1's verbosity and its reasoning-trace formatting on prompts that do not need either.

Their approach is to generate with R1 and then apply filtering and rewriting so the data carries R1's *correctness* without R1's *presentation*, with expert models per domain and rejection sampling against verifiers where verification is possible.

The general pattern to extract: **a specialist model generates training data for a generalist model, with a filtering stage that strips the specialist's idiosyncrasies.** This is now standard practice across the field, and Chapter 19 returns to it.

**3. Response length.** The report is explicit that reasoning-heavy generation produces long outputs and that this is managed deliberately rather than accepted. This is §11.9's reasoning-length-versus-latency trade-off appearing in a real recipe: length is a *product* decision expressed as a data decision, and it is made at the SFT stage rather than left to RL.

**4. What is not published** — the valuable half of this exercise. At minimum:

- **The exact mixture proportions.** "Multiple domains" and a total count; no per-domain breakdown. Without this you cannot reproduce the capability balance.
- **The filtering criteria.** The specific thresholds, classifiers, and rejection rules that decide which generated sample survives. Chapter 11 §11.3 is blunt that this is the intellectual property, and its absence is the single biggest barrier to reproduction.
- Also unpublished: the prompt set's provenance, the rejection-sampling $k$, the annotation guidelines behind the human-written portion, and the ablations that produced these choices rather than others.

Reading for what a report *omits* is a distinct skill from reading what it says. The omissions are rarely accidental, and their pattern tells you what a lab considers its moat.

## 11.8 📄 LIMA versus FLAN

**1. Core claims.**

- **LIMA:** 1,000 carefully curated examples suffice to align a strong base model, because alignment surfaces capabilities pre-training already installed rather than teaching new ones (the "superficial alignment hypothesis").
- **FLAN:** instruction-tuning on a large, diverse mixture of many tasks produces broad zero-shot instruction-following, and both task count and model scale improve it.

**2. The reconciliation** (§11.2).

They are measuring different things and both are right about the thing they measured.

LIMA measures **format acquisition** — learning that a prompt is a request and a response should answer it. This is one behaviour, it is already latent in the base model, and 1,000 examples is plenty to elicit it.

FLAN measures **capability coverage** — being good at summarization *and* translation *and* extraction *and* classification *and* multi-step reasoning. That is not one behaviour; it is hundreds, and each needs its own examples.

Frontier SFT sets are large not because format-learning needs a million examples, but because a modern assistant is expected to be competent at a hundred distinct things and each needs a few thousand.

A useful test of whether you have internalized this: predict what happens when you evaluate a LIMA-style 1,000-example model on a *narrow, unusual* task format — say, structured JSON extraction against a schema. Prediction: it produces a fluent, well-formatted, conversational response that does not conform to the schema. Format acquired; capability absent.

**3. Would LIMA replicate on a 2025 base model?**

**Stronger, argued:** modern base models are trained on far more instruction-shaped data — technical documentation, Q&A sites, and (increasingly) synthetic instruction data that leaks into web crawls. The assistant behaviour is closer to the surface, so less is needed to elicit it. Some 2025 base models will follow instructions zero-shot with a well-chosen prompt, which is close to LIMA's claim taken to its limit.

**Weaker, argued:** the *bar* moved. LIMA was evaluated against 2023 expectations — coherent, helpful, on-topic. A 2025 assistant is expected to call tools with correct JSON, handle 128K contexts, refuse precisely without over-refusing, and produce calibrated multi-step reasoning. None of those are latent behaviours you can surface with 1,000 examples; they are specific capabilities requiring specific data. Relative to what a model must now do, 1,000 examples covers *less* than it did in 2023.

Both are true, which is the resolution: **the superficial alignment hypothesis holds for the behaviour LIMA measured and has been steadily overtaken by growth in what "aligned" means.**

**4. An experiment that distinguishes format from capability.**

Take one base model. Produce two SFT sets of identical size (say 5,000 examples):

- **Set A (format-only):** broad coverage of conversational format across many topics, but every example is a task the base model can already do with few-shot prompting.
- **Set B (capability):** same format, same size, but every example is a task the base model *fails* at few-shot — a specific structured output format, a tool-calling convention, a domain requiring multi-step reasoning it does not do spontaneously.

Evaluate both on: (i) held-out instances of Set A's tasks, (ii) held-out instances of Set B's tasks, (iii) few-shot base-model performance on both as the floor.

- **If SFT only teaches format:** A and B perform equally on (i), and *both* fail (ii) — because if capability cannot be taught, Set B's training did not help.
- **If SFT teaches capability:** B substantially outperforms A on (ii) and roughly matches on (i).

The falsifier for the superficial alignment hypothesis is a large B-over-A gap on (ii). The falsifier for "SFT teaches capability" is B failing to beat A on the very tasks it was trained on.

The reason to design the experiment this way: the base-model few-shot floor in (iii) is what makes it interpretable. Without it you cannot distinguish "SFT taught this" from "the base model could always do this."

## 11.9 🧪 Break the packing lab

**1. `MAX_LEN` 128 → 512.**

*Common prediction:* packing gets more efficient, so the speedup rises modestly.

*What happens:* the speedup rises **sharply** — roughly 4× the previous ratio. But packing did not get better. Packed token count is nearly unchanged (it is bounded below by real content, which did not change). What changed is the *baseline*: padding to 512 wastes four times as much as padding to 128.

The lesson: **the packing multiplier is a statement about your padding baseline, not about packing.** A team quoting "11× from packing" and a team quoting "2.9×" may be doing identical work on different length distributions and context lengths.

**2. `no_mask` trained 5× longer.**

*Common prediction:* with enough training it figures out that user turns are not its job.

*What happens:* it gets *better* at generating user turns. Nothing in the objective ever penalized this — you explicitly trained it to maximize the likelihood of `<|user|>echo dog`, and more optimization means more of what you optimized for.

This is the single most transferable lesson in Part III, and it recurs in reward hacking (§12.6), KL collapse (§13.6), and length exploitation (§15.7): **more compute on a wrong objective makes the wrong thing better, never right.** Wrong objectives do not self-correct.

**3. Block-diagonal mask, positions running across segments.**

*Common prediction:* KL returns to exactly zero, because attention already cannot cross segments.

*What happens:* it does **not** reach zero. Example B's tokens are at absolute positions 20–39 in one pack and 22–41 in the other, because the preceding example had a different length. Different positional embeddings, different logits — even though attention never crossed the boundary.

The magnitude is small with learned positional embeddings and a short context. With RoPE at long context it is not small, which is why §11.5 lists position reset as a *separate* fix from the attention mask rather than a redundant one. You need both.

**4. `"cat"` ten times more frequent, `no_eos` retrained.**

*Common prediction:* only `"cat"`'s generations get longer.

*What happens:* every runaway generation becomes dominated by `"cat"` fragments — the babble is drawn from the training frequency distribution, whatever the prompt was.

This is a small, direct window into what a base model does before SFT, and a decent intuition for why SFT is needed at all. Without a learned stopping behaviour, the model falls back to sampling from its marginal distribution over text. `no_eos` does not produce a broken assistant; it produces a *base model wearing an assistant's clothes*.

## 11.10 🏗️ The template migration

**1. Old client, new model.** The old template never emits `<|tool_call|>` or `<|tool_result|>`, so nothing is malformed on the way in. The failure is on the way *out*: the new model has been trained to emit `<|tool_call|>` when a tool would help. The old client does not know that token, so it either renders it as garbage, drops it, or — worst — the model emits a tool call, receives no result, and continues as if it had one. The user sees a confidently hallucinated tool output.

**2. New client, old model.** The client sends `<|tool_result|>`, which is not in the old model's vocabulary. Depending on the tokenizer it becomes either an `<unk>` token or a sequence of literal bytes. Either way the old model has never seen it and is now generating from a context outside its training distribution. Output quality degrades in a way that will be blamed on the prompt.

**3. A migration that does not break existing clients.**

Version the template and negotiate it, rather than switching it:

- Keep the old template *exactly* byte-identical as `v1`, and serve it to any request that does not explicitly ask for `v2`. Never "improve" a shipped template.
- Add `v2` as opt-in — by API parameter, by model name suffix (`model-2` vs `model-2-tools`), or by endpoint.
- **Train the new model on both templates.** This is the part people skip. Include `v1`-formatted examples in the SFT mixture so the new model is genuinely competent under the old template rather than merely tolerant of it.
- Add a server-side guard: when serving `v1`, suppress the tool-call tokens in the logits entirely. A model cannot emit a token you have masked to $-\infty$, which removes failure mode 1 by construction.
- Publish a deprecation timeline and instrument which clients use which version so the timeline is based on data.

**What you give up:** training on both templates costs mixture capacity (§11.9) — those `v1` examples displace something else. You also carry two serving code paths, two test matrices, and a permanent constraint on ever reusing those token ids. This cost is real and recurring, which is why part 5 matters.

**4. New special-token embeddings.**

Fresh rows in the embedding matrix, randomly initialized, adjacent to rows that have absorbed trillions of tokens of training. Two problems:

- Random initialization at the *default* scale is typically far larger than the norm of converged embeddings. The new token acts as a large perturbation to the residual stream and destabilizes early training.
- The output projection has a corresponding untrained row, so the model's initial probability of emitting the token is arbitrary rather than near-zero.

The standard fixes, in ascending order of care:

- Initialize to the **mean of existing embeddings**, which puts the new row in the right region of the space at the right scale. Cheap, and much better than random.
- Initialize from a **semantically related existing token or phrase** — for `<|tool_call|>`, the mean of the embeddings for `"function"`, `"call"`. Better still.
- Train **only the new embedding rows** for a few hundred steps with everything else frozen, then unfreeze. This lets the new tokens find their place without perturbing the rest of the model.

Whichever you choose, verify it: compare the new rows' norms to the distribution of existing embedding norms before you start the real run. An order-of-magnitude mismatch is the visible symptom.

**5. The version you could have avoided.**

Yes, and it is cheap: **reserve unused special tokens in the vocabulary at pre-training time.** Allocate 64 or 256 slots as `<|reserved_0|>` … `<|reserved_63|>`, include them in the vocabulary from the start, and give them a small amount of training signal so their embeddings are not pathological.

Then adding tool calling later means *renaming* a reserved token, not resizing the vocabulary. No embedding-matrix surgery, no untrained rows, no tensor-parallel resharding because the vocabulary dimension changed, no risk that the new vocabulary size breaks a padding-to-multiple-of-128 alignment your kernels assumed.

Llama, Qwen, and most modern releases ship reserved tokens for exactly this reason. It costs a few thousand unused embedding parameters and it buys you the ability to extend the protocol without retraining. A decision made once at pre-training time that pays out repeatedly for the life of the model family.

## 11.11 🔢 Learning-rate sanity check

**1. Ratio.** $3\times10^{-4} / 1\times10^{-5} = \mathbf{30\times}$.

**2. Why.**

Pre-training builds representations from random initialization; the updates must be large because there is nothing there yet. SFT adjusts a model that already works — it is repurposing existing representations toward a behaviour, not constructing them.

The mechanism behind the failure is worth being concrete about. The knowledge in the model lives in weights that took trillions of tokens to shape. Your SFT set is ~2.6B tokens (Ex 11.2), three orders of magnitude smaller, and *not representative of the pre-training distribution* — it is heavily skewed toward conversational shapes. Large updates on that narrow distribution move weights far enough that the representations serving broad knowledge get overwritten. That is catastrophic forgetting, and it is a direct consequence of step size against distribution narrowness.

**3. The colleague's 1e-4 proposal.**

*Predicted failure:* capability benchmarks drop several points while conversational quality looks fine or even improves. The model becomes a better-sounding, less knowledgeable assistant. Expect MMLU down 3–8 points, GSM8K down more (multi-step reasoning is more fragile than recall), HumanEval down substantially.

There is a second, subtler failure: at 1e-4 on a small dataset the model overfits within a single epoch, so the "fewer epochs" premise partly comes true — but what it converged to is the *format*, memorized, not the task.

*The evaluation that catches it:* capability benchmarks run against the **base model as the reference point**, not against the previous SFT model. This is the check §11.11 puts first in the evaluation stack, and it is the one people skip because they are comparing to last week's SFT run instead of to the model they started from.

The general principle, and the reason this is the last exercise in the set: **in SFT, "learns faster" and "forgets faster" are the same knob.** There is no setting that gives one without the other.
