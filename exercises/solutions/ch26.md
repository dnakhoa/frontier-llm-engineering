## 26.1 🏗️ Calibrate the chapter

**1. Three predictions, with falsification conditions.**

Worked examples of the *format* — write your own for whichever three you pick.

| Prediction | Right looks like | Wrong looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Environments become the contested resource** (§26.3) | Environment-engineering job postings multiply; labs publish agentic *methods* while withholding environments; an "environments" section appears in technical reports the way "data" does now | Agentic RL stalls for a different reason; or general-purpose environments turn out to be cheap to generate, and the bottleneck moves to reward design |
| **Reliability, not capability, is the agentic bottleneck** (§26.3) | pass^k becomes a standard reported metric; the gap between pass@1 and pass^8 narrows slowly and visibly; products ship with narrow scopes and heavy verification | pass@1 improvements simply carry pass^k along with them, and the gap closes as a side effect of capability |
| **Inference compute substitutes for training compute** (§26.5) | Model sizes plateau or shrink while inference budgets grow; pricing shifts toward per-query with variable compute; test-time-compute curves become standard reporting | Training scale resumes growing and the reasoning-model architecture turns out to be a transitional form |

**2. Most likely to be wrong.**

A defensible pick: **"reliability, not capability."** The argument for it being wrong is that pass^k is a deterministic function of pass@1 under independence, so a capability improvement mechanically improves reliability — $0.987^8 = 0.90$ requires only per-attempt 98.7%, which is a capability claim ([Exercise 18.2](ch18.md) part 4). If capability keeps improving, reliability may not need to be a separate research programme at all.

The counter, and why the chapter says it anyway: agent failures are **correlated**, not independent ([Exercise 16.3](ch16.md) part 4). A model that fails a task fails it the same way on retry, so pass^k is worse than independence predicts and does not improve as fast as pass@1. Whether that correlation weakens with capability is genuinely unknown.

**3. The two patterns, from the last three years.**

**Boring engineering solved faster than expected:**

- **Long context.** In 2022, 4K was standard and 100K seemed distant. FlashAttention plus RoPE extension plus paged KV cache made 128K routine within about two years — and none of it required a conceptual advance, just careful systems work.
- **Open-model inference efficiency.** vLLM's continuous batching and paged attention took serving throughput up several-fold in under a year, on unchanged models.
- **FP8 training.** From "numerically dangerous" to production in roughly two years, solved by fine-grained scaling rather than by any new theory ([Exercise 8.8](ch08.md)).

**Conceptual problems solved slower than expected:**

- **Hallucination.** Widely expected to be substantially solved by better training or retrieval; it is better and it is not solved, and the reason it is hard has not changed.
- **Evaluation.** Everyone has agreed since 2022 that benchmarks are inadequate. The situation has arguably worsened as contamination spread ([Chapter 23 §23.5](../../book/part-4-infra/23-evaluation.md)).
- **Reward specification.** [Chapter 12 §12.6](../../book/part-3-post-training/12-reward-modeling.md)'s overoptimization was characterized in 2022 and the field's answer is still "use a KL budget and measure independently." The workaround — verifiable rewards — sidesteps the problem rather than solving it.

The asymmetry is real and it is worth carrying as a prior: **when someone says "this is just an engineering problem," they are usually right about the difficulty and wrong about the timeline in the optimistic direction; when someone says "we need a conceptual breakthrough," they are usually right.**

**4.** Date it and keep it. This is the only exercise in the book whose value is entirely in revisiting.

## 26.2 🏗️ Convert judgement into verification

A worked example on **explanation clarity** — "is this a clear explanation of a technical concept?"

**1. What you would need to verify.**

That a reader who did not previously understand the concept understands it after reading. That is the actual criterion, and it is about a *reader*, not about the text — which is why it resists verification.

**2. A partial verifier.**

A program checking necessary-but-not-sufficient conditions:

```python
def clarity_checks(explanation, concept_spec):
    """Necessary conditions for a clear explanation. None are sufficient."""
    results = {}

    # 1. Coverage: does it mention every element the concept requires?
    results["covers_required_elements"] = all(
        mentioned(explanation, e) for e in concept_spec.required_elements
    )

    # 2. Prerequisite discipline: does it use terms it never defines,
    #    that are not in the assumed-known set?
    undefined = undefined_technical_terms(explanation, concept_spec.assumed_known)
    results["no_undefined_jargon"] = len(undefined) == 0

    # 3. Factual consistency: do the checkable claims hold?
    results["claims_verified"] = verify_claims(
        extract_factual_claims(explanation), concept_spec.knowledge_base
    )

    # 4. Internal consistency: does it contradict itself?
    results["self_consistent"] = not has_contradictions(explanation)

    # 5. Downstream utility -- the strongest signal here:
    #    can a *weaker* model answer questions about the concept
    #    correctly when given only this explanation as context?
    results["teaches"] = weak_model_accuracy(
        explanation, concept_spec.comprehension_questions
    )

    return results
```

Check 5 is the interesting one. It converts "is this clear to a reader" into "does a weaker model, given only this text, answer comprehension questions correctly" — a **measurable proxy for the thing you actually care about**, and it is the same Monte Carlo trick as [Chapter 16 §16.4](../../book/part-3-post-training/16-process-reward-models.md)'s step labelling: *a thing is good if you can succeed from it.*

**3. What it fails to capture, and what gaming produces.**

Fails to capture: whether the explanation is *pleasant* to read, whether it is appropriately concise, whether its analogies are apt or merely present, and whether it is clear to a *human* rather than to a weak model.

**A policy gaming it** would produce something like: an exhaustive explanation that mentions every required element, defines every term including obvious ones, states every fact in an easily-extractable declarative form, and ends with a summary optimized for the comprehension questions. Technically complete, exhausting to read, and it would score well on all five checks.

Recognizably: it would read like a badly-written textbook, which is what optimizing coverage without optimizing readability produces.

**4. Objective, ranker, or filter?**

**Data filter first, inference-time ranker second, RL objective last.**

Per [Exercise 16.5](ch16.md)'s distinction: check 5 is a *learned* component (a weak model's accuracy), so it is hackable. As a filter over a fixed candidate set generated for other reasons, there is no adversary and it is safe. As a ranker at inference, there is a weak adversary (best-of-$n$) and it is mostly safe. As an RL objective, gradient descent is an efficient adversary and part 3's failure mode arrives within a few hundred steps.

The productive use: **filter your SFT data with it**, so the model imitates explanations that demonstrably teach. That is a real improvement and it does not put the verifier in the optimization loop.

**5. What would have to be true for a full verifier.**

You would need a **reliable model of the reader** — one that responds to an explanation the way a human with a specified background would, including being confused by the same things.

That is close to requiring a solution to the general evaluation problem (§26.9's last open question: how do you evaluate a system you cannot out-think?). If you had it, you would use it for much more than explanation clarity.

The honest conclusion, and it generalizes: **the domains without verifiers are the ones where the criterion is about a human's internal state.** Those are unlikely to become fully verifiable, and the productive move is partial verifiers used as filters — which is where a lot of the field's practical progress on "unverifiable" domains is actually coming from.

## 26.3 🔢 The environment economy

**1. Cost of 10,000 reproducible task instances.**

Per-instance engineering, in a *new* domain (no existing mining pipeline):

| Component | Effort |
|---|---|
| Domain analysis, task schema design | 3–6 engineer-weeks, one-time |
| Sandbox: container, isolation, reset ([Exercise 18.4](ch18.md)) | 4–8 engineer-weeks, one-time |
| Task mining or generation pipeline | 6–12 engineer-weeks, one-time |
| Verifier per task type | 2–4 engineer-weeks per type, ~5 types |
| Validation pass over instances | 0.2–1 engineer-hour per instance |

The last row dominates at scale. At 0.3 hours per instance, 10,000 instances is **3,000 engineer-hours ≈ 1.5 engineer-years**, on top of roughly 25–40 engineer-weeks of one-time infrastructure.

**Total: roughly 2–2.5 engineer-years**, or on the order of **$400k–600k** fully loaded.

(SWE-bench Verified's existence is the evidence for the validation cost: 500 instances required a human annotation pass because automatic construction produced unusable tasks, [Exercise 18.7](ch18.md) part 4.)

**2. Versus the RL run.**

From [Exercise 18.1](ch18.md): one iteration is ~70 minutes on 400 environment workers plus 64 GPUs. A 200-iteration run is roughly 230 hours ≈ 10 days.

- GPUs: $64 \times 230 = 14{,}720$ GPU-hours ≈ **$37k** at $2.50/hour.
- Environment workers: 400 CPU workers × 230 h ≈ 92,000 CPU-hours ≈ **$5k**.

**Total compute: ~$42k.**

**The environment costs roughly 10–15× the RL run it supports.**

**3. Why the cost structure differs from pre-training data.**

| | Pre-training data | Agentic environments |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Exists already (the web) | Must be **constructed** |
| Marginal cost per unit | Near zero — filtering, not creation | High — each instance needs a verifier and validation |
| Reusable across tasks | Yes, entirely | Only within a domain |
| Improves with scale of effort | Yes, sublinearly | Yes, roughly linearly |
| Goes stale | Slowly | **Quickly** — dependencies drift, APIs change, and instances silently break |

The decisive difference: **pre-training data is found and filtered; environments are built and maintained.** [Exercise 3.1](ch03.md) established that a filtered crawl has ~10× more tokens than a 15T run needs — supply is not the constraint. For environments, supply is entirely the constraint, and it is a linear-cost engineering problem rather than a filtering problem.

Maintenance is the underrated part: an environment whose pinned dependencies drift produces silently noisy rewards ([Exercise 18.4](ch18.md) part 1), so it is an ongoing cost, not a capital one.

**4. Shared or hoarded?**

**Hoarded, and the cost structure predicts it.**

The reasoning: a resource that is expensive to build, cheap to copy, durable, and directly convertible into capability is exactly the profile of a trade secret. Compare against algorithms, which are also valuable but whose advantage is *temporary* once published ([Exercise 19.8](ch19.md) part 4) — publishing MLA cost DeepSeek little because everyone would have got there. Publishing 10,000 validated task instances is a permanent transfer.

Expect the same disclosure boundary as [Exercise 19.6](ch19.md)'s: **methods published, environments withheld.** Papers describing agentic RL techniques in detail, with a sentence about "an internal suite of training environments."

The partial counter: **benchmark** environments will be shared, because a benchmark is only useful if others run it. So expect a growing gap between public evaluation environments (SWE-bench, τ-bench, WebArena) and private *training* environments — which is itself a contamination problem, since the public ones become training targets ([Exercise 23.7](ch23.md) part 4).

**5. What an "open environments" project would need.**

Modelling on what made FineWeb useful for data:

- **Scale.** Tens of thousands of instances, not hundreds. Below that it is a benchmark, not a training resource.
- **Reproducibility guarantees.** Pinned container images, locked dependencies, deterministic execution ([Exercise 18.4](ch18.md) part 1). Without this the reward is noisy and the resource is worthless for RL.
- **Verifiers included**, not just tasks. A task without a programmatic success criterion is a prompt.
- **A maintenance commitment.** This is the hard part and it is what most such projects lack. Environments rot; a repository of task instances that nobody re-validates is unusable within a year.
- **A held-out split with a governance policy**, so the evaluation portion does not immediately become a training target.
- **Domain diversity.** Software engineering is over-represented because it is easiest to construct. The valuable contribution is a *second* domain done properly.
- **Permissive licensing on both the tasks and the harness.**

The realistic assessment: the maintenance requirement makes this much harder than a data release, and it is why nothing yet plays FineWeb's role here. It is also, for that reason, one of the highest-value open contributions available ([Exercise 19.8](ch19.md) part 3).

## 26.4 🏗️ The ceiling question

**1. Both hypotheses, falsifiably.**

**H1 — Amplification.** RL increases the probability of behaviours the base model already assigns non-negligible probability to. Formally: for any capability $C$ that RL produces in policy $\pi_\theta$, the base model $\pi_0$ assigns non-negligible probability to outputs exhibiting $C$ under some feasible sampling budget. RL is an efficient *extraction* procedure and its ceiling is set by pre-training.

**H2 — Composition.** RL can produce capabilities whose constituent behaviours the base model exhibits, combined in ways the base model does not produce at any feasible sampling budget. The ceiling is set by the RL procedure and its compute, not by the base.

The operative distinction is **at any feasible sampling budget**. H1 says: sample the base model enough and you find the behaviour. H2 says: no amount of sampling the base model produces it, but RL does.

**2. Evidence in this book.**

**For H1:**

- **R1-Zero** (§19.4). A base model plus a one-bit correctness signal produced extended reasoning with no demonstrations. The most natural reading is that the traces were latent and RL amplified them.
- **The zero-variance-group problem** ([Exercise 15.1](ch15.md)). If all $G$ samples fail, the group contributes no gradient. RL structurally *cannot* learn from a prompt the policy never solves — which is the amplification hypothesis stated as a mechanism.
- **Distillation beats RL on small models** ([Exercise 19.5](ch19.md)). If a 7B base lacks the latent capability, RL has nothing to amplify, while SFT on a stronger model's traces installs it. This is close to a direct test and it favours H1.
- **§19.9's reproductions:** the same recipe on a weaker base produces much less.

**For H2:**

- **Length growth** (§15.6). Traces reach lengths the base model essentially never produces spontaneously. Whether "a 10,000-token trace" counts as a new capability or as an amplified rare behaviour is exactly the ambiguity.
- **Self-verification and backtracking** emerging without demonstrations. Same ambiguity: latent-but-rare, or composed?
- The general observation that RL policies reach states far from the base distribution, measured in KL.

**Honest summary: the evidence leans toward H1, and none of it is decisive**, because "non-negligible probability at a feasible budget" is doing a lot of work and nobody has measured it directly.

**3. An experiment that distinguishes them.**

The design that actually tests it:

- Take a base model $\pi_0$ and a task set where RL produces a large gain.
- **Measure the base model's pass@$n$ for very large $n$** — $10^4$, $10^5$, as far as compute allows — using high-temperature sampling and diverse prompting. This estimates whether the capability is latent.
- Run RL to get $\pi_\theta$. Measure its pass@1.
- **Compare $\pi_\theta$'s pass@1 against $\pi_0$'s pass@$n$ curve.**

**Falsifies H1** if $\pi_\theta$ solves problems that $\pi_0$ fails at every feasible $n$ — the capability was not there to extract.

**Falsifies H2** if $\pi_0$'s pass@$n$ curve, extrapolated, covers everything $\pi_\theta$ solves — RL merely concentrated probability mass that already existed.

The confound to control, and it is the hard part: **RL also changes the output distribution's shape, not just its mass.** A base model at high temperature produces incoherent long traces; the RL policy produces coherent ones. Distinguishing "the coherent trace was latent at $10^{-9}$ probability" from "RL constructed it" may not be empirically separable, which is a reason this question has stayed open.

**4. If the base model is the ceiling.**

- **Compute should go to pre-training.** RL is an extraction procedure with a bounded return, so the marginal FLOP is worth more upstream.
- **§26.9's compute-optimal-split question has an answer skewed toward pre-training**, and finding it becomes very valuable.
- **Distillation is the right way to build small models** ([Exercise 19.5](ch19.md)), because RL on a weak base cannot help.
- **Data quality in pre-training becomes even more load-bearing** — you are setting the ceiling for everything downstream.
- Reasoning capability is, in effect, purchased at pre-training time and merely *unlocked* later.

**5. If it is not.**

- **RL compute becomes a first-class scaling axis**, with its own scaling laws to discover.
- **Exploration matters enormously.** If RL can compose, then how it explores determines what it composes, and the field's current exploration machinery (temperature sampling, group size) is primitive.
- **Environments and reward design become the binding constraint** rather than base-model quality — which is §26.3's bet.
- **Small models are not capped by their base**, so RL on small models is worth revisiting.
- The compute-optimal split shifts substantially toward post-training.

The reason §26.9 calls this the most important open question: **almost every allocation decision in the field depends on the answer, and nobody has one.**

## 26.5 🔢 Inference compute as a substitute

**1. The total-cost objective.**

$$\min_{N,\,D,\,k} \; \mathcal{L}(N, D, k) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \underbrace{6ND}_{\text{training}} + \underbrace{2N \cdot k \cdot T_{\text{out}} \cdot Q}_{\text{inference}} \le C_{\text{total}}$$

where $k$ is samples per query, $T_{\text{out}}$ is output tokens per sample, and $Q$ is lifetime queries. The inference term is linear in $N$, $k$, and $Q$, and independent of $D$ — which is what drives the whole result.

**2. The comparison.**

$Q = 10^{10}$ queries, $T_{\text{out}} = 500$ tokens.

**Option A — 91B at pass@1** (Chinchilla-optimal for $10^{24}$ FLOPs, [Exercise 8.1](ch08.md)):

- Training: $6 \times 91\times10^9 \times 1.82\times10^{12} = 9.94\times10^{23}$ FLOPs
- Inference: $2 \times 91\times10^9 \times 1 \times 500 \times 10^{10} = 9.1\times10^{23}$ FLOPs
- **Total: $1.90\times10^{24}$**

**Option B — 30B at maj@8**, trained on more tokens (say 3T to compensate):

- Training: $6 \times 30\times10^9 \times 3\times10^{12} = 5.4\times10^{23}$ FLOPs
- Inference: $2 \times 30\times10^9 \times 8 \times 500 \times 10^{10} = 2.4\times10^{24}$ FLOPs
- **Total: $2.94\times10^{24}$**

**Option A is cheaper**, by 55%. The 8× sampling overhead more than cancels the 3× smaller model.

**3. Where the answer flips.**

Setting the totals equal and solving for $Q$:

$$9.94\times10^{23} + 9.1\times10^{13} Q = 5.4\times10^{23} + 2.4\times10^{14} Q$$
$$4.54\times10^{23} = 1.49\times10^{14} Q \;\Rightarrow\; Q = 3.05\times10^{9}$$

At **$Q \approx 3$ billion queries**, the two are equal. **Below** that, Option B (small + voting) wins because the training saving dominates. **Above** it, Option A wins because inference dominates and the small model's 8× sampling is a permanent tax.

This inverts the naive intuition, and the reason is worth stating: **maj@8 is an 8× inference multiplier applied to every query forever.** A smaller model only wins if its *per-sample* cost advantage exceeds the sampling multiplier — here $91/30 = 3\times$ against an $8\times$ multiplier, so it does not.

**4. Implication for compute-optimal size.**

Not "always go smaller." The correct statement is:

- **A heavily-served model at pass@1 should be smaller than Chinchilla and trained on far more tokens**, because inference cost scales with $N$ and not $D$. This is the Llama-3-8B logic ([Exercise 8.1](ch08.md) part 5) and it holds.
- **A model relying on test-time sampling should be *larger* than the naive small-model instinct suggests**, because the sampling multiplier compounds with the per-token cost.

Combined: **test-time compute and small models are substitutes, not complements.** You spend your inference budget on *either* more samples *or* more parameters per sample, and at high query volume the multiplier-free option is usually better.

**5. Two things this ignores.**

- **Accuracy is not actually equal.** The premise assumes 30B+maj@8 matches 91B pass@1, and [Exercise 23.6](ch23.md)'s curve shows voting saturates — maj@8 buys most of what maj@256 buys, and neither reaches a genuinely stronger model's ceiling on hard problems. If the equivalence fails on the queries that matter, the comparison collapses.
- **Latency and memory, which the FLOP accounting ignores entirely.** maj@8 has 8× the token cost and roughly the *same* wall-clock latency if run in parallel — which may make Option B better for an interactive product despite costing more. Conversely, the 91B model needs more HBM per replica and a higher TP degree ([Chapter 22 §22.8](../../book/part-4-infra/22-inference-serving.md)), so its *deployment* cost is not proportional to its FLOPs.

A third worth noting: **serving efficiency differs by more than the FLOP ratio.** A 30B model may fit on one GPU where a 91B needs two with tensor parallelism, and TP adds an all-reduce per token. Real cost ratios diverge from FLOP ratios by factors that this analysis cannot see.

## 26.6 🏗️ What you would bet on

Mostly yours. Some reasoning to argue against.

**1. Betting against §26.8's durable list.**

The most vulnerable item is **"evaluation is the bottleneck."**

The case against: it is a statement about the current ratio between generating candidates and assessing them. If verification keeps expanding into domains that currently require judgement (§26.2), evaluation becomes progressively cheaper and more automatic, and the bottleneck moves — probably to environments (§26.3) or to data.

The others are more robust because they are statements about physics or about optimization, not about a ratio. "Communication is expensive" and "RL optimizes exactly what you specify" are not the kind of claim that a technique supersedes.

A second candidate: **"data is the product."** It is an observation about a *disclosure pattern* across three reports ([Exercise 19.8](ch19.md)), and [Exercise 19.8](ch19.md) part 4's counterargument — that V3's 10× cost advantage was algorithmic — shows the pattern is contingent on the field's current state rather than permanent.

**2, 3, 4.** Yours. Two observations to inform them.

**On over-hyped:** the honest candidate is **multi-agent structure** (§26.3). It is currently more prompting pattern than trained capability, the published gains are frequently attributable to more inference compute rather than to the structure, and the ablation "one agent with the same total token budget" is often not run.

**On under-invested:** **environment engineering** and **evaluation infrastructure**, both by the same argument — they are the bottlenecks (§26.3, §23.1), they are unglamorous, and §25.1's scarcity column shows labs cannot fill the roles. Under-investment relative to impact is close to the definition of those two rows.

**5. What to learn to still be useful in five years.**

A candidate paragraph:

> Learn the arithmetic — bytes, FLOPs, bandwidth, memory hierarchies — until you can size any system on a whiteboard, because that skill predates this field and will outlast it. Learn the failure modes structurally rather than as a list: reward hacking, overoptimization, distribution shift between training and serving, and the fact that more compute on a wrong objective makes the wrong thing better. Learn to debug by forming hypotheses and ordering them by cost to test, and to look at the data before the hyperparameters. Learn to read a primary source for what it does not say. Then pick one area — data, systems, kernels, environments, evaluation — and go deep enough that you can be wrong about it in public and correct yourself. The specific algorithms in this book will be replaced; every one of those habits will still be load-bearing, and the people who have them will be the ones who learn whatever replaces the algorithms fastest.

Write your own. It is the last exercise in the book and it is the one you will actually use.
