## 7.1 🔢 The bandwidth hierarchy

**1. Bandwidths.**

- **Intra-node (NVLink, H100/NVSwitch):** ~900 GB/s bidirectional per GPU. For collectives, achieved all-reduce bandwidth is commonly ~400–450 GB/s per GPU.
- **Inter-node (400 Gb/s InfiniBand):** $400/8 = 50$ GB/s per NIC. With one NIC per GPU (typical on HGX platforms) and ~80% achieved, roughly **40 GB/s per GPU**.

**2. Ratio.** $450 / 40 \approx \mathbf{11\times}$. On clusters with fewer NICs per node, or on Ethernet, the ratio is worse — 20–40× is common.

**3. A 140 GB all-reduce.**

Ring all-reduce moves $2(N-1)/N \times$ payload per rank, approaching $2\times$:

- **Within a node** (8 GPUs): $140 \times 1.75 = 245$ GB at 450 GB/s = **0.54 s**
- **Across 128 nodes** (1,024 GPUs): $140 \times 2.0 = 280$ GB at 40 GB/s = **7.0 s**

**4. The placement rule.**

**Put the highest-volume, most-frequent, least-overlappable collective on the fastest link.**

In practice that orders the parallelism axes: **TP** (two blocking all-reduces per layer, no overlap possible) gets NVLink; **EP** (all-to-all twice per layer, on the critical path) gets NVLink; **PP** (small activation transfers at stage boundaries) tolerates the slow network; **DP** (one all-reduce per step, overlappable with the backward pass) tolerates the slow network.

This ordering is why §6.7's configuration put TP and EP inside the node and PP and DP across nodes, and it is the single most transferable fact in Chapter 7.

## 7.2 🔢 Topology and the rack boundary

**1. Cost to a data-parallel all-reduce.**

2:1 oversubscription at the spine means the aggregate uplink bandwidth is half the aggregate downlink. A ring all-reduce whose ring crosses the spine repeatedly is throttled to roughly **half the effective inter-node bandwidth** — so ~20 GB/s instead of 40, and the §7.1 all-reduce goes from 7.0 s to ~14 s.

Mitigated substantially by two things: the all-reduce overlaps with the backward pass, and a topology-aware ring can be ordered so that most hops stay within a rack-group and only a few cross the spine.

**2. Cost to an expert-parallel all-to-all.**

Much worse, for a structural reason: **an all-to-all is inherently all-pairs.** Every rank sends to every other rank, so a fixed fraction of the traffic *must* cross the spine — there is no ordering that avoids it, unlike a ring.

Worse still, all-to-all is a scatter of many smaller messages, which achieves lower link utilization than a ring's large contiguous transfers. And per §6.6, it is on the critical path with nothing to overlap against unless you have built DualPipe-style scheduling.

Expect the all-to-all to run at **well under half** the non-oversubscribed rate.

**3. What you can change.**

- **Confine EP within a node.** The most important move, and it makes the spine oversubscription irrelevant to the all-to-all. This is why a common default is EP = 8 (one node). It is a rule of thumb, not a law: DeepSeek-V3 ran EP = 64 across 8 nodes and paid for it with overlap and node-limited routing.
- **Increase gradient accumulation**, so the DP all-reduce happens less often relative to compute.
- **Increase PP degree** and decrease DP, shifting traffic from the all-reduce onto the much cheaper pipeline transfers.
- **Use hierarchical all-reduce** — reduce-scatter within a node over NVLink, all-reduce the smaller per-node partials across the spine, all-gather back. This reduces the inter-node volume by the intra-node group size.

**4. Estimated throughput difference for the §6.7 config.**

That configuration had EP=8 intra-node, so the all-to-all is unaffected. The exposed cost is the DP=32 all-reduce, which goes from ~7 s to ~14 s per step against ~8 s of compute — but it overlaps with the backward pass, so the *exposed* cost is roughly $\max(0, 14 - 8) = 6$ s of stall instead of 0.

Step time goes from ~8 s to ~14 s: a **~40% throughput loss**, turning a 39-day run into ~55 days.

Which is the practical lesson: the same 1,024 GPUs, the same model, the same code, and a 40% difference from an allocation decision made by a scheduler. This is why §7.4 topology-aware placement is a real job.

## 7.3 🔢 MTBF, properly

**1. GPU-only.** $40{,}000 / 4{,}096 = \mathbf{9.77 \text{ hours}}$.

**2. With non-GPU components.** Roughly doubling the failure rate halves the interval: **~4.9 hours**.

**3. Over 45 days.** $45 \times 24 / 4.9 = \mathbf{\approx 220 \text{ interruptions}}$.

For calibration, Llama 3 recorded 419 unexpected interruptions over 54 days on 16,384 GPUs. Scaling that rate down to 4,096 GPUs and 45 days gives $419 \times (4096/16384) \times (45/54) \approx 87$. So this model **over-predicts by ~2.5×** — the real per-GPU MTBF under Meta's conditions was better than 40,000 hours, or their non-GPU contribution was smaller than assumed. Either way, treat 220 as a pessimistic bound and 87 as the observed-rate estimate.

**4. Correlated failures and the distribution.**

A rack power event taking out 32 nodes at once does not change the expected number of *GPU-failures*, but it changes everything about how they arrive.

Instead of ~220 independent single-GPU events, you get many single events plus a handful of large correlated ones. The **mean** downtime may be similar; the **variance** is enormous. Specifically:

- **Recovery is not proportional.** Losing 8 GPUs means grabbing 8 hot spares. Losing 256 means you may not have 256 spares, so the job waits for hardware.
- **The tail lengthens dramatically.** A single correlated event can cost hours rather than minutes.
- **Poisson planning is wrong.** If you sized your spare pool from the mean failure rate, a correlated event exhausts it.

**5. Mean or tail?**

**Both, with different mechanisms, and conflating them is the common planning error.**

*Against the mean:* frequent checkpointing (§21.7) and fast automatic restart. This makes each of the ~220 small events cost minutes. It is an optimization problem with a known answer.

*Against the tail:* a hot spare pool sized for the largest correlated failure domain, elastic reconfiguration so the job can continue degraded (§21.9), and multi-rack placement so one power domain cannot take the whole job. It is a capacity and topology problem, not an optimization.

The failure mode to avoid: engineering excellent checkpointing, achieving 95% effective training time in the steady state, and then losing three days to a cooling event because there were no spare nodes in a different rack-group.

## 7.4 🏗️ Diagnose eight cluster symptoms

| # | Symptom | Two likely causes | First diagnostic |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | +15% step time, persistent | (a) A GPU throttling on temperature or power; (b) a degraded NIC or optical link forcing retransmits | Per-rank step-time histogram. One slow rank makes every synchronous collective wait — find it, then check `nvidia-smi -q` clocks and throttle reasons on that host. |
| 2 | Periodic 40 s spikes | (a) Checkpoint write blocking the training step; (b) evaluation or logging running inline | Correlate spike timestamps with checkpoint step numbers. If they align, you have a synchronous checkpoint that should be asynchronous (§21.5). |
| 3 | One rank `NaN`, others finite | (a) Hardware fault on that GPU — bad memory, a failing SM; (b) a genuine numerical issue that happened to surface on the rank holding a particular shard | Move the job to different hardware and rerun from the same checkpoint. If the `NaN` follows the *rank*, it is the data or shard; if it follows the *node*, it is hardware. This bisection is the whole diagnostic. |
| 4 | Hang, 100% utilization, no error | (a) A collective mismatch — one rank called a different collective, or a different size; (b) a deadlock between compute and communication streams | `py-spy dump` or `gdb` on every rank, then look at which collective each rank is blocked in. Ranks blocked in *different* collectives is the signature of a mismatch. |
| 5 | Fine for 20 min after restart, then degrades | (a) Memory fragmentation in the caching allocator; (b) host memory or page-cache pressure from a leaking data loader | Plot allocator reserved-vs-allocated over time. Growing reserved with flat allocated is fragmentation. Also check host RSS. |
| 6 | NCCL timeout on a proven collective | (a) A transient network fault; (b) one rank fell behind for an unrelated reason — a slow data loader, a stalled disk read — so the others timed out waiting | Check whether the timing-out rank is the *late* one or an *early* one. Timeouts report the waiter, not the culprit. Look at per-rank data-loader latency. |
| 7 | Identical configs and seeds diverge | (a) Nondeterministic kernels — atomics, cuDNN autotuning (§20.8); (b) different collective reduction order between runs | `torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)` on a small config and see if the divergence disappears. If it does, it was floating-point associativity, not a bug. |
| 8 | Slightly worse loss, nothing errors | (a) **Data-loader resumption bug** (§21.6) — training on repeated data; (b) silent data corruption (§7.5) | Log the sample IDs consumed per step and diff against the previous run. This is the one symptom on the list with no error message and no metric, and it is the most expensive. |

**Why item 8 is the hardest:** every other symptom announces itself. This one looks like normal run-to-run variation, and the two candidate causes both silently degrade the model over the entire remaining run. The only defence is *instrumentation you built before you needed it* — logging consumed sample IDs, and periodic verification of a known-good computation.

## 7.5 🔢 Silent data corruption

**1. Expected incidents.**

$$4{,}096 \text{ GPUs} \times \frac{60}{365} \text{ years} = 673 \text{ GPU-years}$$
$$\frac{673}{2{,}000} = \mathbf{0.34 \text{ incidents expected}}$$

About a one-in-three chance over one run — which sounds tolerable until you consider that a lab runs many jobs continuously, so over a year of cluster operation you should expect several.

**2. How a corrupted gradient propagates.**

One corrupted value in the all-reduce output is applied to one parameter by the optimizer. From there:

- The corrupted parameter affects that layer's output for **every subsequent token**.
- Adam's $m$ and $v$ for that parameter now carry the corruption forward, with $v$ in particular being slow to forget (typically $\beta_2 = 0.95$–$0.999$).
- If the corrupted value is large, gradient clipping (§8.6) may mask it by scaling the whole gradient down — which corrupts *every* parameter slightly rather than one badly.

**3. Why not a `NaN`.**

Because SDC typically flips a bit in the mantissa or a low exponent bit, producing a **plausible finite number**. A flipped high exponent bit would give a huge value that clipping or a `NaN` check would catch. The dangerous case is the quiet one: a weight that should be 0.0031 is now 0.0047, and nothing anywhere is checking.

This is the defining property of SDC and the reason it is called silent.

**4. Three detection strategies, ranked by cost.**

1. **Periodic idempotency check (cheap).** Every $N$ steps, run the same micro-batch forward twice and compare outputs bitwise. Catches deterministic per-device corruption. Costs one extra forward pass every few thousand steps — essentially free.
2. **Gradient-norm anomaly detection (cheap).** Track per-rank gradient norms; a rank whose norm departs from the distribution is suspect. Catches large corruptions, misses small ones.
3. **Checksumming collectives (expensive).** Compute a checksum before and after every collective. Catches transport corruption reliably; adds a reduction over every tensor in every collective.

**5. Checksumming every collective: for and against.**

*For:* it is the only method that catches small corruptions in the communication path deterministically. On a run where an undetected corruption costs weeks of training, the expected value is easy to justify.

*Against:* the overhead is not small — a checksum is itself a reduction over the full tensor, and doing it before and after every all-reduce meaningfully increases the time in collectives, which §6.4 showed is already the tight budget. It also does not catch corruption that happens in *compute* rather than in transport, which is the more common source.

**The usual resolution:** checksums on the checkpoint write/read path (where they are cheap relative to the I/O and where a corruption is permanent), plus periodic idempotency checks during training, and no per-collective checksums. That combination catches the two highest-consequence cases at negligible cost.

## 7.6 🏗️ Design the on-call

**1. Alerts and thresholds.**

| Alert | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Job not running | Any process exit |
| Step time above baseline | >20% for 5 consecutive minutes |
| No step completed | >10× median step time (hang detection) |
| Loss `NaN` or `Inf` | Immediate |
| Loss spike | >3σ above the trailing 100-step mean |
| Gradient norm anomaly | >5σ, or a per-rank outlier |
| Checkpoint write failed | Any failure |
| Checkpoint age | >3× the configured interval |
| Free space on checkpoint filesystem | <15% |
| GPU ECC uncorrectable errors | Any |
| GPU throttling | Any rank throttled >2 minutes |
| Data-loader stall | Any rank waiting >30 s for a batch |

**2. What pages at 3am.**

**Pages:** job not running, hang detected, `NaN`, checkpoint filesystem nearly full, checkpoint writes failing.

**Waits until morning:** a single throttling GPU, a modest step-time regression, a single loss spike that recovered, one ECC error.

**The line:** *page if the job is currently not making progress, or if it will stop making progress within the next few hours and automation cannot fix it.* Everything else is a morning problem. A 15% throughput regression costs $22,000 a day and is not worth destroying an engineer's sleep and judgement over — and a tired engineer makes worse decisions about a $150,000/day job than a rested one.

**3. What is automated with no human in the loop.**

- **Restart on crash**, from the last checkpoint, with a bounded retry count.
- **Drain and replace a node** that reports uncorrectable ECC or fails a health check, pulling from the hot spare pool.
- **Health-check every node before it joins** a restarted job — a short NCCL bandwidth test and a matmul correctness check. This catches the node that is broken *now* rather than the one that was broken an hour ago.
- **Escalate to a page** after N failed automatic restarts.

**4. Runbook for "job died, cause unknown."**

1. Do not restart yet. Capture the state: last 500 lines of every rank's log, `dmesg` on every host, the last checkpoint's step number, and the scheduler's exit reason.
2. Check whether any rank logged something the others did not. Sort by timestamp across ranks — the *first* anomalous message usually identifies the culprit rank, and the loudest messages are usually downstream victims.
3. Check the hardware layer: ECC counters, link flap counters, node health-check results.
4. If a specific node is implicated, drain it, then restart.
5. If nothing is implicated, restart with health checks enabled and mark the job for closer watching.
6. If it dies again in the same way within an hour, stop restarting and escalate. Repeated automatic restart of a deterministically failing job burns money and destroys the evidence.

**5. Avoiding the restart-a-broken-job failure mode.**

This is the important part of the question. Automation that restarts on crash will happily restart into a state that is running and wrong — the §7.4 item-8 case.

Three defences:

- **Validate after every restart.** Run a fixed evaluation batch and compare the loss against the value recorded at the checkpoint. A mismatch means the resume did not restore state correctly. This is cheap and it catches the data-loader bug directly.
- **Log and diff consumed sample IDs** across the restart boundary.
- **Bound the automation.** After 3 automatic restarts in an hour, stop and page. Automation should handle the routine case and refuse the unusual one, rather than confidently doing the wrong thing 40 times overnight.

## 7.7 🔢 The cost of a hang

**1. Detection time.** $10 \times 10 \text{ s} = \mathbf{100 \text{ seconds}}$, plus whatever margin the watchdog requires before firing — call it ~2 minutes.

**2. Total cost per hang.**

$$2 \text{ min (detect)} + 4 \text{ (reschedule)} + 6 \text{ (reload)} + \text{lost work}$$

Lost work is on average half the checkpoint interval. At §21.7's ~8-minute optimum, that is 4 minutes.

$$2 + 4 + 6 + 4 = \mathbf{16 \text{ minutes}}$$

**3. Cost in dollars.**

$$\frac{16}{60 \times 24} \times \$150{,}000 = \mathbf{\approx \$1{,}670 \text{ per hang}}$$

**4. Watchdog at 3× instead of 10×.**

*The benefit:* detection in ~30 s instead of ~100 s, saving ~1 minute per hang — about $105.

*The risk:* **false positives.** Step time is not constant. A checkpoint write, an evaluation pass, a data-loader hiccup, or a transient network stall can all produce a step 3× the median without anything being wrong. A false positive kills a healthy job and costs the full 16 minutes plus the lost work — so a false positive is 10× more expensive than the true positive is cheap.

*How to decide:* **measure your step-time distribution.** Plot the histogram over a few hours of healthy running and find the actual 99.9th percentile. If p99.9 is 1.8× the median, then 3× is safe and 10× is wasteful. If p99.9 is 4×, then 3× will fire constantly.

This is the general form of every threshold decision: **set it from the measured tail of the healthy distribution, not from a round number.** And note the asymmetry — because a false positive costs far more than a slow detection, err high.

## 7.8 📄 Read the Llama 3 infrastructure section

**1. Failure categories.** They report 466 interruptions over 54 days: 47 planned, 419 unexpected. Of the unexpected, roughly **78% were confirmed hardware issues**, with GPU-related problems the largest single category (well over half), followed by network, host, and other components. A meaningful remainder was software and unknown.

**2. Effective training time and implied cost per interruption.**

They report **over 90% effective training time.** With 419 interruptions over 54 days:

$$\text{lost time} \le 10\% \times 54 \times 24 \times 60 = 7{,}776 \text{ minutes}$$
$$\frac{7{,}776}{419} \approx \mathbf{19 \text{ minutes per interruption}}$$

Which is remarkably close to the §7.7 estimate of 16 minutes — a good sign that the model in that exercise reflects reality.

**3. Automation described.** Automated detection, diagnosis, and remediation; health checks; a system for identifying and draining problematic hosts; and tooling to make restart fast. The theme is that failure handling is a *system*, not a procedure someone follows.

**4. Something you would not have predicted.**

Several candidates, and the one most people find surprising: **environmental and power effects at cluster scale.** They describe throughput variation correlated with time of day from temperature effects, and — the memorable one — the power-grid implications of tens of thousands of GPUs synchronously starting and stopping compute between training steps, which produces large, fast swings in datacenter power draw.

That is a failure mode that exists only at this scale and that no amount of distributed-systems background prepares you for. It is a good illustration of Chapter 7's premise: **at 16,000 GPUs, physics is part of your stack.**

## 7.9 🧪 Break the collective model

**1. Ring all-reduce at 8 versus 128 ranks.**

*Prediction people make:* per-rank traffic grows with rank count.

*What happens:* per-rank traffic is $2(N-1)/N \times$ payload, which **approaches $2\times$ and never exceeds it.** At $N=8$ it is $1.75\times$; at $N=128$ it is $1.98\times$. Essentially flat.

What *does* grow is **latency**: a ring all-reduce takes $2(N-1)$ sequential steps, so at 128 ranks you pay 254 hops of link latency. For large tensors this is negligible against transfer time; for small tensors it dominates completely. That is why gradient bucketing (§6.4) exists — it converts many latency-bound small collectives into few bandwidth-bound large ones.

**2. Oversubscription: all-reduce versus all-to-all.**

*Prediction:* both degrade by the oversubscription factor.

*What happens:* the all-reduce degrades **less than proportionally** if the ring is topology-aware, because you can order the ring so most hops stay under a leaf switch and only a few cross the oversubscribed tier. The all-to-all degrades **fully**, because all-pairs communication has no ordering that avoids the spine (§7.2 part 2).

**3. One rank 20% slower.**

*Prediction:* aggregate throughput drops ~20%/N, since one rank of many.

*What happens:* **aggregate throughput drops ~20%, full stop.** Every synchronous collective completes at the pace of the slowest participant, so one straggler taxes all $N$ ranks equally.

This is why §7.4 item 1's first diagnostic is a per-rank step-time histogram: a 15% cluster-wide slowdown is far more likely to be *one* rank at 85% than all ranks at 85%.

**4. Hierarchical versus flat all-reduce.**

*What you find:* hierarchical wins as soon as the intra-node bandwidth advantage exceeds the cost of the extra stages — in practice, at **any node count above 1**, on a cluster with the §7.1 11× ratio.

The mechanism: flat ring moves $\sim2\times$ payload per rank over the *slow* link. Hierarchical does reduce-scatter within the node over NVLink (fast), then all-reduces only $1/8$ of the payload across nodes, then all-gathers back over NVLink. Inter-node volume drops by the intra-node group size.

The crossover is nearly immediate, which is why every production NCCL configuration uses hierarchical algorithms and why the flat ring is mostly a pedagogical object.
