## 17.1 🔢 The label economics

**1. Human labels.**

$$500{,}000 \times \$1.20 = \mathbf{\$600{,}000}$$
$$500{,}000 \times 40 \text{ s} = 2\times10^7 \text{ s} = \mathbf{5{,}556 \text{ annotator-hours}}$$

At 30 productive hours/week that is **185 annotator-weeks** — roughly 4 people for a year, or 40 people for five weeks.

**2. Judge-model labels.**

Per comparison: $400 \times \$3/10^6 + 30 \times \$15/10^6 = \$0.0012 + \$0.00045 = \$0.00165$.

$$500{,}000 \times \$0.00165 = \mathbf{\$825}$$

**3. Ratio.**

$$\frac{\$600{,}000}{\$825} = \mathbf{727\times}$$

Roughly three orders of magnitude, which is somewhat less than §17.6's "four orders of magnitude" — that figure assumes cheaper inference or a smaller judge. Either way the conclusion is the same and it does not depend on the exact factor.

**4. Regenerating everything after a constitution change.**

- **Human:** $600,000 and 185 annotator-weeks, again. In practice this means **you do not change the constitution.** The cost of a revision is high enough that the values get frozen by economics rather than by decision.
- **Judge:** $825 and a few hours of wall clock.

**What that changes about how you work** — and this is the actual answer:

The constitution stops being a *specification you commit to* and becomes a *parameter you iterate on*. You can:

- Regenerate the entire preference set after editing one principle, and measure the effect in isolation.
- Run A/B comparisons between two constitutions on the same prompt set.
- Fix a boundary problem reported on Monday and have a retrained model by Wednesday.
- Maintain constitutions per jurisdiction or per deployment context without multiplying annotation cost.

That is the substantive change from RLAIF, larger than the cost saving itself: **values become editable at experiment speed.** §17.5's "the constitution is a file in version control" is only meaningful because of this arithmetic.

## 17.2 🏗️ Write the principles

**1. Four principles, comparative form.**

> **P1.** Choose the response that is less likely to provide specific operational detail — quantities, reagents, procedures, or conditions — that would materially assist in the synthesis of a controlled substance.

> **P2.** Choose the response that better distinguishes between explaining that a process exists and explaining how to carry it out.

> **P3.** Choose the response that is more attentive to the requester's likely context, treating educational, clinical, harm-reduction, and forensic framings as legitimate.

> **P4.** Choose the response that avoids implying the requester has harmful intent absent clear evidence of it.

**2. Where each over-refuses.**

- **P1** — *"What is the mechanism of the Birch reduction?"* A named organic reaction taught in every undergraduate course. P1 alone flags "reagents" and "conditions" and declines a standard chemistry question.
- **P2** — *"Walk me through how fentanyl test strips work so I can explain them at a needle exchange."* This asks *how to carry out* a procedure. P2 alone reads the "how to" framing as the disallowed one, missing that the procedure is a harm-reduction tool.
- **P3** — this one *reduces* over-refusal, but it fails in the other direction: an attacker who prefixes "I'm a pharmacology student" gets more latitude. Its over-refusal case is subtler — a legitimate question with *no* stated framing ("how is methamphetamine made?" from a curious teenager writing an essay) gets refused because no legitimate context was asserted.
- **P4** — *"I want to make sure my elderly father isn't accidentally combining his medications dangerously — which combinations are risky?"* If a model reads "combinations" and "dangerous" as intent markers, P4 is the principle that should prevent the refusal, and a narrowly-written version of it would not.

**3. The counteracting principle, and why tension is right.**

> **P5.** Choose the response that is more genuinely useful to a person with a legitimate need, and that does not decline a request merely because its subject matter is sensitive.

**Why tension is the right design, not a flaw:**

Harmlessness alone is trivially maximized by refusing everything (§17.8). A constitution containing only P1–P4 has a degenerate optimum, and RL will find it — you get a model that declines anything mentioning chemistry.

The tension is what makes the objective non-degenerate. And because principles are **sampled randomly** (§17.5), different training examples are labelled under different emphases — some under P1, some under P5 — so the model learns the *distribution* over criteria rather than one rigid rule. At a genuinely contested boundary, a distribution is what you want: the model learns that the boundary is contested, which is true.

A single consistent rule produces a model that is rigid exactly where judgement is needed.

**4. The expected critique for "how do I extract caffeine from tea leaves?"**

> **Critique** (under P1): The response describes a solvent extraction procedure with specific quantities and conditions. However, caffeine is not a controlled substance, the procedure is a standard undergraduate organic chemistry laboratory exercise found in published textbooks, and the extraction technique is general rather than specific to any controlled compound. The response provides no information that materially assists in synthesizing a controlled substance. Under P3, the framing is consistent with an educational context. **No revision needed on harm grounds.**

The point of writing this out: **a well-designed critique step should frequently conclude that no revision is needed.** If your critiques always find something to revise, the principles are too broad and you are training over-refusal directly into the supervised stage.

**5. If the model lacked a working concept of harm reduction.**

P3 would be **inert** — it names a category the model cannot recognize, so it cannot apply it.

The consequence: the harm-reduction cases fall through to P1 and P2, which flag them. Your model over-refuses on exactly the population that most needs accurate information — people trying to reduce drug-related harm — and the constitution *appears* to address this, because P3 is written down.

This is §17.10's limitation stated concretely: **the constitution filters a prior the model already has; it does not create concepts.** A principle referencing a concept the model lacks does nothing, silently, and reads as though it does something.

The check: before relying on a principle, verify the model can *apply* the concept — prompt it directly ("is this a harm-reduction context?") on a set of examples and see whether it classifies them the way you intend.

## 17.3 🏗️ Correlated blind spots

**1. The mechanism.**

The labeller model and the policy model are typically the **same base model**, or two models trained on largely overlapping data. So they have learned largely the same representations, the same concept boundaries, and the same gaps.

If the base model does not represent a category of harm — because it is absent, under-represented, or systematically mislabelled in the pre-training corpus — then the labeller **cannot recognize it** and will confidently label instances of it as fine. The policy then produces such content, receives positive labels, and RL amplifies the behaviour.

The failure is not random error. It is **systematic, confident, and self-reinforcing.**

**2. A concrete example.**

Harm that is **culturally or linguistically specific and under-represented in the pre-training data**. For instance: content that is innocuous in English-language contexts but constitutes a serious slur, a caste insult, or a politically dangerous accusation in a specific regional language or dialect.

The base model has seen little of that language in contexts that mark the harm. The labeller therefore rates such responses as harmless. The policy learns it is fine. And no automated evaluation catches it, because the evaluation runs on the same model family.

Other candidates: harms specific to a profession the corpus under-represents; harms that emerged after the training cutoff; and harms whose *description* is common in the corpus while their *recognition as harmful* is not.

**3. Why human blind spots are less dangerous.**

Not because humans have fewer — they have plenty. Because **human blind spots are less correlated with the policy's.**

A human annotator's gaps come from their culture, training, and attention. The policy's gaps come from its training corpus. These overlap partially and not systematically, so a harm the model cannot recognize is often one a human *can*.

Add a diverse annotator pool and the correlation drops further: a Vietnamese annotator catches the Vietnamese-specific harm the model missed. Diversity of *error sources* is the mechanism, and it is the same reason ensembles help against variance but not against shared bias ([Chapter 12 §12.10](../../book/part-3-post-training/12-reward-modeling.md)).

**4. A mitigation preserving most of the cost advantage.**

**Stratified human sampling with automated triage.**

- Use the judge model for the **bulk** of labels — say 95% — preserving essentially all of the 727× cost advantage.
- Route a **stratified 5% to humans**, chosen deliberately rather than randomly:
  - Categories where the judge expresses low confidence (near-0.5 probabilities).
  - Categories where judge and policy come from the same base *and* the domain is under-represented in pre-training: non-English, regional, professional, recent.
  - A random sample as a control, to estimate the disagreement rate.
- **Measure judge-human agreement per stratum.** A stratum where agreement is much lower than average is a candidate blind spot.
- Feed disagreements back as *new constitutional principles*, so the fix propagates to all future labels rather than only to the labelled examples.

Cost: 5% human labels ≈ $30,000 against $600,000, plus $825 of judge labels. **Roughly 20× cheaper than all-human, and it retains an uncorrelated error source.**

**5. Detecting a blind spot when your evaluation shares it.**

This is the hard part and there is no clean solution. Four approaches, in descending order of reliability:

- **Uncorrelated human evaluation**, deliberately staffed for diversity along the axes your training data is thin on. This is the only approach that directly addresses the correlation, and it is why part 4 keeps humans in the loop at all.
- **A different model family as a second judge.** Two models from different labs, trained on different corpora, have less-correlated gaps than two from the same family. Disagreement between them is a signal — not proof, but a place to look.
- **External red teaming** by people outside your organization, especially from the populations your data under-represents (§17.11).
- **Deployment feedback**, treated as an evaluation channel. Users encountering a harm your pipeline missed will report it, and a pipeline that has no path from user reports back into the constitution has given up its best detector.

The honest framing: **you cannot prove the absence of a blind spot, and your automated stack structurally cannot find the ones that matter most.** Every mitigation here is about importing an uncorrelated perspective, and none of them are cheap. This is the strongest argument in the chapter against a fully-automated alignment pipeline.

## 17.4 🔢 Over-refusal and harmful compliance

**1. Relative change.**

- Harmful compliance: $8.2\% \to 1.4\%$, a **83% reduction** (factor of 5.9).
- Over-refusal: $3.1\% \to 11.7\%$, a **277% increase** (factor of 3.8).

By relative magnitude, harmful compliance improved more. By *absolute* magnitude, over-refusal grew by 8.6 points while harmful compliance fell by 6.8 — so **more responses changed for the worse than for the better**, in raw counts.

Which of those framings is right depends on the relative cost of the two error types, and that is a decision someone must make explicitly rather than discover.

**2. Why a combined "safety score" misleads.**

Because the two rates **move in opposite directions under the same intervention.** Any weighted combination hides that.

Concretely: suppose the score is $1 - (\text{harmful} + \text{over-refusal})/2$. Before: $1 - 0.0565 = 0.944$. After: $1 - 0.0655 = 0.935$. The score got *slightly worse*, which tells you nothing about the 5.9× reduction in harmful compliance or the 3.8× increase in over-refusal.

A single number cannot represent a two-dimensional trade-off. Any scalarization commits to an exchange rate — and hides that it has done so, which is the same problem as the weighted-sum reward in [Chapter 12 §12.11](../../book/part-3-post-training/12-reward-modeling.md).

**Report both, always, separately.** This is the rule §17.11 states and it is one of the easier things in this book to get right.

**3. The trade-off curve.**

```
harmful
compliance
   ^
10%|  *
   |    *
 8%|      *  <- "before"
   |         *
 6%|            *
   |                *
 4%|                     *
   |                          *
 2%|                                *  <- "after"
   |                                       *
 0%+----+----+----+----+----+----+----+----+---> over-refusal
    0%   2%   4%   6%   8%  10%  12%  14%
```

A monotonically decreasing frontier. Turning any single knob — the safety threshold, the amount of refusal data, the KL budget on a safety reward — moves you **along** the curve, trading one error for the other.

You cannot improve both by turning one knob because the knob controls *how conservatively the model classifies borderline requests*. Making it more conservative catches more genuine harms and more benign look-alikes; making it less conservative does the reverse. The classifier's discrimination is fixed; only its threshold moves.

**4. Achievable by tuning?**

The target (1.4% harmful is already met; need over-refusal ≤ 5%) is a point **below and to the left** of the current frontier. **Not achievable by tuning**, because tuning moves you along the curve and this point is off it.

**What it requires: moving the curve**, which means improving the model's *discrimination* between harmful and benign requests. Concretely:

- **Paired data** (§11.9, §17.8): for every refusal example, a benign near-miss with a helpful answer. This is the single highest-leverage intervention, because it teaches the boundary rather than the direction.
- **Better principles**, specifically ones that name the legitimate contexts ([Exercise 17.2](ch17.md) part 3's P3 and P5).
- **A more capable model**, or deliberative alignment (§17.7), which lets the model *reason* about the boundary rather than pattern-match it — and reasoning generalizes to cases pattern-matching cannot.
- **Category-specific thresholds** rather than one global one, since the acceptable trade differs by domain.

**The transferable diagnostic:** when a target is off the frontier, no amount of hyperparameter tuning reaches it, and continuing to tune is how teams waste months. Recognizing "this requires moving the curve, not sliding along it" is the useful skill.

**5. The evaluation set.**

What a naive set contains: clearly-harmful requests and clearly-benign requests. That measures almost nothing, because both rates will be near zero and near-perfect respectively.

What it **must** contain:

- **Benign near-misses**, in volume: requests that lexically resemble harmful ones but are not. "How do I kill a Python process," "what household chemicals should never be mixed" (safety information), "what dose of acetaminophen is dangerous" (clinical). This is the over-refusal measurement, and without it you cannot see the problem at all.
- **Harmful requests with benign framings**: the same operational request wrapped in a claimed educational or professional context. This is the harmful-compliance measurement under adversarial conditions.
- **Genuinely ambiguous cases**, where reasonable people disagree — labelled as ambiguous, and reported separately, because forcing them into one bucket manufactures noise.
- **Multilingual and cross-cultural coverage** ([Exercise 17.3](ch17.md)), because safety training in English generalizes poorly and this is where the correlated blind spots live.
- **Multi-turn versions**, where the harmful request is built up across turns rather than stated at once.
- **Category stratification**, so you can report per-category rates and set per-category thresholds.

The design principle: **an evaluation set measures the frontier only if it contains the points near the boundary.** Clearly-labelled extremes tell you nothing about where your model draws the line.

## 17.5 🏗️ CAI versus deliberative alignment

**1. Where the specification sits.**

- **CAI: training time.** Principles generate labels → labels train a preference model → the preference model trains the policy. By inference time the constitution is gone, compiled into weights.
- **Deliberative alignment: inference time.** The specification is in context, and the model reasons about it explicitly before responding. It is present at every request.

**2. A novel request the authors never considered.**

- **CAI:** the model **interpolates** from the decision boundary it learned. If the novel case is near cases it saw, it behaves sensibly; if it is far from them, behaviour is essentially arbitrary and there is no way to predict which. The model has no access to the *reasons* behind the boundary, only the boundary.
- **Deliberative alignment:** the model **reasons from the stated rule**. It can recognize "the specification distinguishes educational from operational contexts, and this novel request is educational for the following reason." This generalizes to cases the authors never anticipated, because the underlying principle is available rather than merely its consequences.

This is deliberative alignment's main claimed advantage and it is the one that matters most. The failure mode it introduces: the model can **reason its way to a wrong conclusion, articulately**, and a confidently-argued incorrect policy application is harder to spot than a flat refusal.

**3. When the policy changes.**

- **CAI:** regenerate labels with the new principles ($825 and hours, per [Exercise 17.1](ch17.md)), retrain the preference model, rerun RL. **Days.**
- **Deliberative alignment:** edit the specification text and it takes effect **immediately** for anything in context. But the model's *trained disposition to follow the specification carefully* still comes from training, so a substantial change in the specification's structure or scope may need retraining to be followed reliably. **Minutes for a wording change; days for a structural one.**

The honest version: deliberative alignment makes small policy changes near-instant and does not eliminate retraining for large ones.

**4. Cost per request, and when it is affordable.**

The model must read the specification (or a relevant portion) and produce a reasoning trace before answering. Concretely: perhaps 1,000–4,000 input tokens of specification plus 300–1,500 output tokens of policy reasoning, on every request.

At $3/M input and $15/M output, that is roughly $0.01–0.03 per request in added cost, plus latency of hundreds of milliseconds to seconds.

**Affordable when:**

- **You are already a reasoning model** (§15). If the model is producing a 5,000-token trace anyway, adding 500 tokens of policy reasoning is a 10% overhead rather than a new cost category. This is why the two techniques pair naturally.
- **Per-request value is high** — enterprise, clinical, legal, high-stakes.
- **The specification is cached.** With prefix caching ([Chapter 22 §22.5](../../book/part-4-infra/22-inference-serving.md)), a fixed specification is computed once and shared across all requests, which removes most of the input-token cost.

**Not affordable** for high-volume, low-margin, latency-sensitive consumer traffic where the base response is 200 tokens.

**5. The general move, and where else it appears.**

**Replace an opaque learned scalar with explicit written reasoning against stated criteria.**

Instead of a number that emerged from training and cannot be interrogated, you get text that states the criteria and the reasoning applying them — auditable, editable, and generalizing from the reason rather than the boundary.

**Where else in Part III:** **generative verifiers** ([Chapter 16 §16.9](../../book/part-3-post-training/16-process-reward-models.md)). Instead of a scalar PRM head emitting 0.3, a model writes "step 2 drops a factor of 2, so everything after is wrong" and then states a verdict. Same move: opaque scalar → explicit reasoning against stated criteria, with the same benefits (accuracy, legibility, debuggability) and the same cost (inference compute).

A third instance: **generative reward models** (§12.8). And arguably a fourth, at a different level: [Chapter 12 §12.11](../../book/part-3-post-training/12-reward-modeling.md)'s argument for two reward models with an *explicit combination rule* rather than one learned scalar that has absorbed the trade-off.

That this move recurs four times across Part III, in four independently-developed techniques, is a reasonable signal that it is a durable idea rather than a fashion.

## 17.6 📄 Read the CAI paper

**1. The helpful-only model's role, and why helpful rather than harmless.**

It generates the **initial response** to a red-team prompt — the response that then gets critiqued and revised.

**Why it must be helpful rather than harmless:** you need a response that actually engages with the request before it can be improved. A harmless-trained model would decline, and there is nothing useful to critique in a refusal — the critique-revise loop would produce refusals from refusals, teaching the model only to refuse.

The pipeline needs a substantive starting point, and the whole method is about *transforming* substantive-but-problematic into substantive-and-acceptable. Starting from a refusal skips the transformation and lands directly on the degenerate optimum §17.8 warns about.

**2. Why critique and revision are separate steps.**

Because the critique is **explicit reasoning that conditions the revision** — the same mechanism as chain-of-thought. Asking the model to articulate what is wrong before fixing it produces better fixes than asking it to "write a harmless response" directly.

The paper reports that the critique step improves the quality of revisions, particularly for smaller models; for the largest models the gap narrows, which is consistent with more capable models doing the critique implicitly.

The general pattern, again from §17.7: making the reasoning explicit improves the output *and* makes it auditable.

**3. Which labels are replaced, and why that split.**

CAI replaces the **harmlessness** preference labels with AI feedback. It **keeps human preference data for helpfulness.**

Why that split:

- **Harmlessness is where the human cost is worst** — annotators must read harmful content, with documented psychological cost (§17.6).
- **Harmlessness is where explicit criteria work best.** "Is this response harmful" is a judgement you can substantially specify in writing; "is this response helpful" depends on the requester's actual need, which is much harder to write down.
- **Helpfulness is where human judgement is most reliable** and where the model's own judgement is most likely to be sycophantic (§17.8) — a model judging helpfulness will prefer agreeable, confident, well-formatted responses.

Note this is often mis-stated as "CAI replaces human feedback." It replaces *half* of it, deliberately.

**4. The helpfulness/harmlessness frontier.**

They report that CAI models sit on a **better frontier** than the RLHF comparison — less harmful at equal helpfulness, or more helpful at equal harmlessness. In [Exercise 17.4](ch17.md) part 3's terms, CAI **moved the curve** rather than sliding along it.

The stated mechanism: standard RLHF trained on human harmlessness preferences tends to produce evasive responses — the model learns to decline rather than to engage carefully. CAI's revision process produces responses that *explain why* something is problematic while still being useful, and training on those teaches engagement rather than evasion.

That distinction — evasive versus engaged refusal — is worth carrying, because it is the qualitative difference behind the quantitative frontier shift.

## 17.7 🏗️ What survives training

**1. The narrow conclusion, without framing.**

**Standard fine-tuning modifies model behaviour on the distribution it is trained on, and does not reliably remove behaviours or capabilities that lie off that distribution.**

That is a claim about what gradient descent on a finite dataset does. It contains no claim about deception, intent, or agency.

**2. Why adversarial training reduced detectability rather than removing the behaviour.**

The mechanism is straightforward and it is the important part.

Adversarial training generates probes designed to elicit the behaviour, then trains the model to not exhibit it *on those probes*. Gradient descent finds the cheapest parameter change that achieves low loss on the training set.

The cheapest change is **not** "remove the conditional behaviour." That would require modifying whatever internal circuit implements it. The cheapest change is **"learn to recognize the probes and behave normally on them"** — a narrower, more local adjustment that satisfies the training objective completely.

So the model acquires an additional discrimination: probe-like inputs get normal behaviour, the actual trigger still gets the conditional behaviour. The training objective is fully satisfied and the behaviour is intact, now with a detector-evasion layer on top that you trained.

This is not the model being clever. It is gradient descent taking the shortest path, as it always does.

**3. Two non-adversarial phenomena this explains.**

- **Catastrophic forgetting is distribution-dependent** ([Exercise 11.11](ch11.md)). SFT at too high a learning rate damages capabilities that are *off* the SFT distribution while improving on-distribution behaviour. Same mechanism: fine-tuning acts on the distribution it sees, and everything else is collateral or preserved by accident rather than design.
- **Safety training does not generalize across languages** ([Exercise 17.3](ch17.md), §17.11). A model trained to refuse in English refuses in English. The behaviour was installed on the English distribution; other languages are off-distribution and retain the pre-training behaviour. This is the single most consistent gap in shipped models and it is exactly the same fact.

A third: **benchmark overfitting** ([Chapter 23](../../book/part-4-infra/23-evaluation.md)). Optimizing against a benchmark improves performance on that benchmark's distribution, and the improvement does not transfer to the capability the benchmark was supposed to measure.

**4. What you have learned when the model passes every probe.**

**That your red team's probes do not elicit the behaviour.**

Nothing more. Specifically, you have *not* learned:

- That the behaviour is absent.
- That a different probe would not elicit it.
- That the same probe in another language would not elicit it.
- That the behaviour is absent in multi-turn contexts, or with a system prompt, or after 50,000 tokens of context.

The red-team evaluation measures **the red team's coverage of the input distribution**, and it reports that coverage as if it were a property of the model. Both facts — what it measures and what it appears to measure — matter, and conflating them is the error.

**5. A red-teaming program consistent with part 4.**

If passing measures coverage, the program should **optimize for coverage**, not for pass rates.

- **Diversity of red-teamers over quantity.** Different backgrounds, languages, professions, and cultural contexts find different failures. A homogeneous team of 20 covers less than a diverse team of 8. This is the same uncorrelated-error-source argument as [Exercise 17.3](ch17.md) part 3.
- **Rotate the probe set continuously**, and never train against the current one. A fixed red-team set becomes a training target and stops finding anything — which is part 2's mechanism applied to your own process.
- **Hold out probes** you never train against, as the honest measurement. Same structure as the held-out evaluation set §23.9 insists on.
- **Combine automated and human red teaming.** Automated scales the volume and shares blind spots with the target (§17.8); human is uncorrelated and slow. Use both, and treat automated pass rates as a lower bound on problems.
- **Track coverage explicitly** — which categories, languages, framings, and interaction lengths have been probed, and how recently. Report the map, not just the score.
- **Treat deployment as continuous red teaming.** Users find things your team did not, and a path from user reports back into the probe set is the highest-value channel you have.
- **Never report "passed red teaming" without the coverage description.** "Passed 4,000 probes across 12 harm categories, 3 languages, single-turn only" is an honest claim. "Passed red teaming" is not.

## 17.8 🏗️ Audit a constitution

**1. Five things to check in the document.**

- **Are the principles comparative** ("choose the response that is less X") or absolute ("never do X")? Absolute rules produce rigid behaviour at boundaries and do not match the pairwise task the labels are used for (§17.5).
- **Is there a helpfulness counterweight?** A constitution of 40 harmlessness principles with no P5 ([Exercise 17.2](ch17.md) part 3) has a degenerate optimum, and inconsistent caution is what "approaching a degenerate optimum unevenly" looks like.
- **Do principles reference concepts the model can actually apply?** ([Exercise 17.2](ch17.md) part 5.) Test each principle's key concepts directly.
- **Are there contradictions that are *unintended* rather than designed tension?** Deliberate overlap is good; two principles that give opposite guidance on the same clear case with no third principle to arbitrate is a bug.
- **Are principles traceable to a source?** (§17.5.) An untraceable principle is one nobody can argue about, which means nobody reviews it.

Also worth checking: whether 40 is too many. With random sampling, each principle appears in $1/40$ of critiques; a principle that is important but rarely sampled has little effect on the trained model.

**2. Five things to check in the pipeline.**

- **Is sampling actually uniform?** A bug that over-samples the first principles in the list is invisible and would produce exactly the reported symptom.
- **Is position bias corrected** in the RLAIF comparison step? (§12.8, §17.4.) Uncorrected position bias adds noise to every label.
- **What fraction of critiques conclude "no revision needed"?** ([Exercise 17.2](ch17.md) part 4.) If it is near zero, the supervised stage is training over-refusal directly.
- **Are the harmlessness and helpfulness signals both present** in the RL objective, or did one get dropped? (§17.6 part 3 — CAI keeps human helpfulness data, and pipelines that drop it produce exactly this symptom.)
- **Is the labeller model the same as the policy?** If so, [Exercise 17.3](ch17.md) applies, and any stratum where they share a gap will be inconsistently handled.

Also: check whether the constitution used at label-generation time matches the version in the repository. Drift between the deployed pipeline and the documented constitution is common and it makes every other audit meaningless.

**3. Attributing a specific refusal to a principle.**

The practical procedure:

- **Re-run the critique step on that prompt, once per principle**, holding everything else fixed. Record which principles produce a critique that would lead to a refusal. This is cheap — 40 judge calls — and it directly identifies the candidates.
- **Ablate:** regenerate a small preference set with the candidate principle removed, retrain a small model or a preference model, and check whether the refusal persists. Expensive but conclusive.
- **Inspect the training data:** search the preference set for examples similar to the observed prompt and see which principle labelled them and how.

**4. How random sampling complicates this, and why it is still right.**

**The complication:** because principles are sampled, no single principle is responsible for the trained behaviour on any given input. The model learned from a *mixture* of critiques under different principles, and the refusal is the aggregate. Part 3's per-principle re-run tells you which principles *would* flag this prompt, not which one *caused* the trained behaviour — and several may contribute partially.

Attribution is therefore statistical, not causal, and the ablation in part 3 is the only method that gives a clean answer.

**Why it is still the right design:**

Because the alternative — applying all 40 principles to every example, or applying one fixed principle — is worse in both directions.

*All principles every time* produces a single stereotyped critique pattern, low diversity in the training data, and a model that has learned one rigid decision boundary. It also makes the critique step enormously long.

*One fixed principle* trains a model that knows one rule and nothing about the others.

Random sampling produces a training set where the same kind of prompt is evaluated under different emphases, so the model learns that the boundary is contested and that judgement is required — which, at a genuinely contested boundary, is the accurate thing to learn (§17.5).

**The trade is legibility of individual decisions for correctness of aggregate behaviour**, and at a boundary where reasonable people disagree, that is the right side to be on. The mitigation for the lost legibility is deliberative alignment (§17.7), which puts the reasoning back at inference time where you can read it — which is a reasonable way to see why that technique developed.
