## 2.1 🏗️ Route the incident

| # | Incident | Diagnoses | Fixes | Why they differ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Throughput −30%, no code change | Large-Scale Training Engineer | Cluster/Infra Engineer | The training engineer sees the symptom in their metrics and localizes it to a slow rank; the cause is almost always a degraded node, a bad link, or a filesystem problem, and replacing hardware is Infra's authority ([Ch 7](../../book/part-2-pretraining/07-cluster-reality.md)). |
| 2 | Loss spike, rollback did not help | Pre-training Researcher | Pre-training Researcher | Rare case where one role owns both. A rollback that fails means the problem is in the recipe or the data, not a transient — so it is an LR, precision, or data-mixture question (§8.13). |
| 3 | MMLU −4 after post-training | Evaluation Engineer | Post-training Researcher | Evaluation surfaces it and confirms it is real rather than harness noise; the fix is LR or mixture ([Ch 11 §11.7](../../book/part-3-post-training/11-sft-frontier-style.md)). |
| 4 | Benchmark found in the corpus | Evaluation Engineer | Pre-training Data Engineer | Detection is an evaluation-integrity function (§23.5); decontamination lives in the data pipeline. Note that the *fix* may be "use a different benchmark," which is again Evaluation's call. |
| 5 | p99 tripled, p50 unchanged | Inference Engineer | Inference Engineer | A p99-only regression is a scheduling or admission-control problem — long requests starving short ones (§22.11) — not a model problem. |
| 6 | MoE all-to-all is 40% of step time | Large-Scale Training Engineer | Kernel Engineer | The training engineer's profiling localizes it; making the all-to-all overlap with compute is kernel and schedule work (§6.8, §20.11). |
| 7 | Refuses in Vietnamese, answers in English | Alignment/Safety Researcher | Post-training Data Engineer | Safety identifies it as an over-refusal with a cross-lingual gap (§17.8); the fix is paired multilingual safety data (§11.9). |
| 8 | Resumed fine, loss suspiciously low | Large-Scale Training Engineer | Large-Scale Training Engineer | The data-loader resumption bug (§21.6). Worth noting: nobody would file this as an incident, because it looks like good news — which is why it is on the list. |

**The pattern worth extracting:** in six of eight cases the person who *notices* is not the person who *fixes*. That is why §2.5's handoffs matter, and why the most useful thing an engineer at a frontier lab can develop is an accurate map of who to hand a symptom to.

## 2.2 🏗️ Staff the run

**1. An 18-person allocation.**

| Role | Count | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-training Data Engineer | 3 | The data *is* the model (§3.1). At 30B you cannot outspend anyone on compute, so quality is your only lever. Three is the minimum for a real pipeline: crawl and extraction, dedup and filtering, mixture and tooling. |
| Pre-training Researcher | 2 | Architecture, recipe, scaling ablations. Two so decisions get argued rather than asserted. |
| Large-Scale Training Engineer | 3 | Owns the runs and the on-call. Three is the floor for a rotation that does not destroy people over 12 months. |
| Cluster/Infra Engineer | 1 | Assumes rented compute with a provider handling hardware. If you own the cluster, this becomes 3 and something else gets cut. |
| Kernel Engineer | 1 | At 30B MoE you need the all-to-all and MoE grouped-GEMM to work. One strong person, leaning on open-source kernels for everything else. |
| Post-training Researcher | 2 | SFT mixture plus preference/RL. Two, because the mixture ablations and the RL loop are genuinely different work. |
| Post-training Data Engineer | 2 | Synthetic generation, filtering, verifiers. Chapter 19 §19.8's lesson: the data is the moat. |
| RL/Post-training Engineer | 1 | The rollout infrastructure (§13.10). |
| Evaluation Engineer | 2 | Two, and this is the allocation most people would argue with — see below. |
| Inference Engineer | 1 | Serving the product. Uses vLLM or SGLang rather than building an engine. |
| **Total** | **18** | |

**Roles set to 0, and why:**

- **Alignment/Safety Researcher** — folded into Post-training Researcher. Defensible at 18 people and it is a real risk; it is the first hire at 22.
- **Reasoning/Agents Researcher** — a 12-month timeline to a shipped chat product does not have room for it.
- **Capability Researcher / Benchmark Lead** — merged into Evaluation.
- **Applied/Solutions Engineer** — no external API surface yet.

**Justifying 2 on Evaluation**, since this is where staffing plans usually go wrong: §23.1's argument is that evaluation throughput gates experiment throughput. A team that can run 40 experiments and evaluate 3 is running 3. Under-staffing evaluation does not save headcount; it silently converts your other 16 people into a slower team.

**2. First three hires.**

1. **Large-Scale Training Engineer.** Without someone who can run the job, nothing else can be tested at scale.
2. **Pre-training Data Engineer.** The longest lead time by far. A trillion-token pipeline takes months before it produces anything, and every day it is late is a day the run cannot start.
3. **Evaluation Engineer.** Deliberately early and it is the contrarian pick. Building evaluation infrastructure *before* you have models to evaluate means it exists when you need it, and it means your first decisions are informed. Hire this late and you spend six months making decisions on vibes.

**3. The role you cannot afford.**

**A dedicated Alignment/Safety Researcher.** What breaks: safety work becomes a pre-launch scramble rather than a design input, and per §17.8, over-refusal and harmful compliance move in opposite directions in ways that need someone measuring both continuously.

Mitigation: make it an explicit part of one Post-training Researcher's charter with protected time, adopt a published constitution or model specification rather than writing one (§17.5), and contract external red teaming before launch. This is worse than a dedicated hire and it is much better than nothing.

**4. At 9 people.**

Cut to: 2 data, 1 pre-training researcher, 2 training engineers, 2 post-training, 1 evaluation, 1 inference.

**And stop trying to pre-train from scratch.** This is the important part of the answer. Nine people cannot run a competitive from-scratch pre-training run in 12 months — the data pipeline alone will consume most of the team. Start from a strong open base model (Qwen, Llama), invest everything in post-training and data, and ship a genuinely good product.

That is not a compromise, it is a better plan at that size. The failure mode of a 9-person team is attempting the 18-person plan and shipping nothing.

## 2.3 🏗️ The handoff surfaces

| Handoff | Artifact | Common failure | A good interface specifies |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data Eng → Pre-training Researcher | Tokenized, shuffled, sharded corpus + a data card | The mixture ratios in the card do not match what is on disk | Exact token counts per source, the dedup method and threshold, the decontamination report, and a reproducible manifest |
| Researcher → Training Eng | A model config and a training recipe | The config is "what we ablated at 1B" with no statement of what should change at 30B | Which hyperparameters are scale-dependent and how they scale (muP, §8.12), and which are fixed |
| Training Eng → Post-training | A base-model checkpoint | Handed over without the tokenizer, the exact context length it was trained at, or the parallelism layout | Checkpoint format, tokenizer + config, training token count, context length, and known weaknesses |
| Post-training → Evaluation | A candidate model | Evaluated with a different chat template than it was trained with (§11.6) | The chat template, the generation config, and the exact prompt format — shipped *with* the checkpoint |
| Post-training → Inference | A model to serve | Training checkpoint handed over without conversion; TP resharding done wrong (§21.10) | Serving-format weights, target TP degree, quantization spec, template, and a golden-output test set |
| Kernel Eng → everyone | A faster operation | Numerically different from the reference in a way nobody checked (§20.8) | Tolerance versus the reference, the shapes it was tuned for, the shapes where it *loses*, and determinism guarantees |

**The recurring theme:** almost every one of these failures is *metadata* not travelling with the artifact. The tokenizer, the template, the tolerance, the manifest. The interface fix is nearly always "make the artifact self-describing" rather than "communicate better."

## 2.4 🏗️ Where the boundary should sit

**1. Parallelism configuration — Researcher vs Training Engineer.**

**The line:** the researcher owns the *model* (layers, width, experts, context length); the engineer owns *how it is split* (TP, PP, DP, EP degrees, recomputation policy).

**Failure of drawing it too far toward the researcher:** architecture choices that are hostile to parallelization — a layer count that does not divide evenly by any sensible pipeline depth, a hidden size that wastes tensor-core alignment.

**Failure of drawing it too far toward the engineer:** the configuration is chosen for throughput at the cost of the recipe — a micro-batch size that changes the effective batch size and silently invalidates the LR schedule (§8.14).

**The real answer:** this boundary must be *permeable*, with the two roles in the same conversation before the architecture is frozen. §6.13's worked examples exist because these decisions are joint.

**2. SFT mixture — Researcher vs Data Engineer.**

**The line:** the researcher owns *what proportions and why*; the data engineer owns *how the data is produced and whether it is what it claims to be*.

**Failure:** if the researcher owns production too, filtering quality becomes an afterthought and the mixture is nominal rather than real. If the data engineer owns proportions, the mixture optimizes for what is easy to generate — which means more code and math and less of everything hard.

**3. Rollout generation — RL Engineer vs Inference Engineer.**

**The line:** there arguably should not be one. §13.9's point is that generation is 60–80% of RLHF wall-clock, so the rollout stack *is* a serving system.

**Failure of a hard boundary:** the RL engineer uses `model.generate()` because the serving stack is "someone else's system," and the run is 10× slower than it needs to be. This is extremely common.

**The right structure:** the RL engineer owns the loop and the inference engineer is embedded in it, or the RL engineer is expected to know Chapter 22. §25.4 notes that people who know both are scarce enough to name their role.

**4. Evaluation vs everyone optimizing against it.**

**The line should be organizationally hard**, and this is the one case in this exercise where the boundary should be *less* permeable rather than more.

The argument, made three times in this book (§12.13, §13.12, §23.9): the reward model can be hacked (§12.6), the benchmark can be overfit, and the people optimizing are — through no bad intent — an efficient search process for the evaluation's weaknesses. If the same person owns both the optimization target and the measurement, the measurement will drift toward whatever the optimization can achieve.

**Failure of a soft boundary:** evaluation gets tuned until the model passes. Nobody decides to do this; it happens one reasonable-seeming adjustment at a time.

**The concrete mechanism:** keep at least one held-out set that the optimizing team never sees results from, owned by evaluation, run at fixed intervals. Everything else can be shared.

## 2.5 📄 Reverse-engineer a team

Answers depend on which report you chose; here is what the exercise should have surfaced, using DeepSeek-V3 as the example.

**1. Roles inferable from the work.** Data engineering (the 14.8T corpus and its filtering), pre-training research (MLA, DeepSeekMoE, the auxiliary-loss-free scheme), large-scale training engineering (the 2,048-GPU run), kernel engineering (the FP8 kernels and DualPipe — this one is unusually visible), infrastructure (the cluster and network description), post-training research, and evaluation.

**2. Which sections were written by infrastructure people.** The tells: precise hardware model numbers, communication-volume tables, discussion of what was *overlapped* with what, honest reporting of a bottleneck, and any mention of failure handling. Research sections talk about what the model does; infrastructure sections talk about what the machine does. The FP8 and DualPipe sections of the V3 report read distinctly differently from the architecture sections.

**3. Team size and structure.** The author list is long and, notably, alphabetical or contribution-grouped rather than strictly ranked in these reports. A flat or grouped author list usually indicates a team that does not run on individual paper credit — which is itself a structural fact about how the lab works.

**4. What the page balance tells you.** In the V3 report, infrastructure and training-efficiency material occupies a large share relative to the architecture description. That is the report telling you where the lab's differentiated effort went: not "we invented a new architecture" but "we made this run affordable." Compare with reports where post-training dominates, which tells you a different story about that lab's priorities.

**The transferable skill:** read a report's *proportions*, not just its content. What a lab spends pages on is what a lab spends people on.

## 2.6 🏗️ Your own map

No solution — the answer is yours. Two notes on doing it honestly.

**On part 1:** the most common error is placing yourself by interest rather than by demonstrated skill. The honest question is "what would I be hired to do tomorrow, based on what I have actually shipped." If the answer is not on §2.2's list at all, that is fine and useful — §25.3 notes lateral entry from distributed systems, HPC, or GPU programming is the most common successful path, and none of those are on the list.

**On part 5:** the gap that unlocks the most others is usually **the arithmetic** (§6.1, §22.3, §20.3). It is upstream of pre-training, post-training, systems, and inference simultaneously, it takes weeks rather than months, and §24.5 notes every interview archetype tests it. If you are unsure where to start, start there.
