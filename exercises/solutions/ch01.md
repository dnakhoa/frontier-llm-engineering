## 1.1 🔢 The compute bill

**1. Wall-clock days.**

$$\frac{2{,}664{,}000 \text{ GPU-hours}}{2{,}048 \text{ GPUs}} = 1{,}301 \text{ hours} = \mathbf{54.2 \text{ days}}$$

Consistent with the report's "less than two months" for pre-training. It also matches the stated rate of 180K GPU-hours per trillion tokens: $14.8 \times 180\text{K} = 2{,}664\text{K}$. Good — the numbers are internally coherent, which is the first thing to check with any published figure. (Using the full 2,788K would mix in context extension and post-training.)

**2. Cost at $2.00/GPU-hour.**

$$2{,}788{,}000 \times \$2.00 = \mathbf{\$5.576\text{M}}$$

Which matches the reported $5.576M exactly: it is the **full** training, pre-training ($5.328M) plus context extension and post-training. That is not a coincidence: the report computes its figure this way, by multiplying GPU-hours by an assumed rental rate. Knowing that changes how you read the claim.

**3. What the figure excludes.**

| Category | Larger or smaller than the run? |
|---|---|
| **Failed and abandoned runs** | Often **larger**. Nobody gets the recipe right first time. The runs that did not work are usually a multiple of the one that did. |
| **Ablations and small-scale experiments** | Comparable or larger. Architecture and mixture decisions come from dozens of smaller runs. |
| **Data pipeline compute** | Smaller but not trivial. Deduplicating and filtering petabytes is a serious CPU and storage bill (Chapter 3). |
| **Post-training** | V3's own post-training *is* in the figure (5K GPU-hours). The internal R1 models whose outputs its SFT data was distilled from are not. |
| **Salaries** | Comparable or larger. A team of 50 strong engineers for a year is a substantial number. |
| **Cluster capital cost** | Much larger, if you own rather than rent. 2,048 H800s is a nine-figure purchase, amortized. |

The honest reading: **$5.576M is the marginal cost of the final run, assuming rented compute and assuming you already knew the recipe.** It is a real and interesting number and it is not the cost of producing DeepSeek-V3.

## 1.2 🔢 Tokens per second

**1. Aggregate throughput.**

$$\frac{14.8 \times 10^{12} \text{ tokens}}{1{,}301 \times 3{,}600 \text{ s}} = \frac{14.8\times10^{12}}{4.68\times10^6} \approx \mathbf{3.16 \times 10^6 \text{ tokens/s}}$$

About three million tokens per second, sustained for almost two months.

**2. Per GPU.**

$$\frac{3.16\times10^6}{2{,}048} \approx \mathbf{1{,}543 \text{ tokens/s/GPU}}$$

**3. FLOPs, using active parameters.**

$$6 \times 37\times10^9 \times 14.8\times10^{12} = \mathbf{3.29 \times 10^{24} \text{ FLOPs}}$$

**4. Implied MFU, and why it misleads.**

Available compute: $2{,}664{,}000 \text{ h} \times 3{,}600 \text{ s/h} \times 990\times10^{12} \text{ FLOP/s} = 9.49\times10^{24}$ FLOPs.

$$\text{MFU} = \frac{3.29\times10^{24}}{9.49\times10^{24}} \approx \mathbf{35\%}$$

**Why this is misleading, and it is misleading in two directions at once:**

- **The run used FP8, not BF16.** H800 FP8 peak is roughly double the BF16 figure, so measured against the precision actually used, MFU is closer to 17–18%. The denominator you choose changes the answer by 2×, which is why MFU claims are only meaningful with the precision stated.
- **The $6ND$ approximation ignores attention.** It counts the matmuls in the linear layers and omits the $O(N^2)$ attention FLOPs, so it *understates* actual work — pushing true utilization up.
- **MoE routing overhead is not in $6ND$ either.** All-to-all communication, expert load imbalance, and the dispatch/combine operations are real work that this formula does not count.

The transferable lesson: **an MFU number without the precision format and the FLOP-counting convention is not comparable to any other MFU number.** This is the same methodological point Chapter 23 §23.4 makes about benchmark scores, and it applies just as widely.

## 1.3 🔢 Where 671B parameters live

**1. Weights in BF16.**

$$671\times10^9 \times 2 \text{ bytes} = \mathbf{1{,}342 \text{ GB}}$$

**2. Full mixed-precision training state.**

$$671\times10^9 \times 16 \text{ bytes} = \mathbf{10{,}736 \text{ GB} \approx 10.7 \text{ TB}}$$

Note that this uses **total**, not active, parameters. Every expert's weights and optimizer state must exist somewhere, even though only a fraction are used per token. This is the central trade of MoE: compute scales with active parameters, memory scales with total.

**3. Fraction of cluster HBM.**

$$2{,}048 \times 80 \text{ GB} = 163{,}840 \text{ GB} = 164 \text{ TB}$$
$$\frac{10.7}{164} \approx \mathbf{6.5\%}$$

**4. Why 6.5% is not comfortable.**

Two consumers of the rest, both large:

- **Activations.** At the batch sizes and sequence lengths of a real run, activation memory is frequently comparable to or larger than the static state, even with recomputation (§6.12). It scales with batch × sequence × hidden × layers, and it is the term you actually tune.
- **Communication buffers and fragmentation.** NCCL needs registered buffers, the MoE all-to-all needs staging space for dispatched tokens, and CUDA's allocator fragments over a two-month run. Reserving 10–20% of HBM for this is normal.

And the structural reason: **the memory is not fungible.** You cannot use idle HBM on GPU 1,700 to hold a tensor that GPU 3 needs. What matters is per-GPU peak, and the parallelism configuration (Chapter 6) is precisely the machinery for making the per-GPU number fit. A comfortable cluster-aggregate number tells you the problem is solvable, not that it is solved.

## 1.4 🔢 The MLA claim

**1. MLA cache per token.**

$$2.5 \text{ MB} \times (1 - 0.93) = \mathbf{0.175 \text{ MB} = 175 \text{ KB per token}}$$

**2. At 128K context, one sequence.**

- MHA: $2.5 \text{ MB} \times 131{,}072 = 327{,}680 \text{ MB} = \mathbf{328 \text{ GB}}$
- MLA: $0.175 \text{ MB} \times 131{,}072 = 22{,}938 \text{ MB} = \mathbf{23 \text{ GB}}$

**3. Sequences per 80 GB H800, weights ignored.**

- MHA: $80 / 328 = \mathbf{0.24}$ — **a single 128K sequence does not fit on one GPU.**
- MLA: $80 / 23 = \mathbf{3.5}$ sequences.

That first result is the one to sit with. Under plain MHA, serving a single user at 128K context requires multiple GPUs *for the cache alone*, before you have loaded a single weight. The model is not servable at that context length in any economically sensible way.

**4. Why serving cares more than training.**

Because during training the KV cache is transient — computed in the forward pass, consumed in the backward, discarded — while during serving it must **persist for the entire generation**, for every concurrent user. Training pays for the cache once per step; serving pays for it continuously and multiplied by concurrency.

This is Chapter 22 §22.3's argument, and it is why attention-variant choices (MHA, MQA, GQA, MLA) are serving-economics decisions that get made at architecture time and cannot be revisited afterwards.

## 1.5 🏗️ Read the run backwards

**1. Model size.**

The clean route is compute. 512 GPUs × 21 days × 24 h = 258,048 GPU-hours.

Assume H100-class at 990 TFLOP/s BF16 and a realistic dense-model MFU of 40%:

$$\text{FLOPs} = 258{,}048 \times 3{,}600 \times 990\times10^{12} \times 0.40 = 3.68\times10^{23}$$

From $6ND$ with $D = 8\times10^{12}$:

$$N = \frac{3.68\times10^{23}}{6 \times 8\times10^{12}} = \mathbf{7.7\times10^9 \approx 8\text{B parameters}}$$

**Assumptions stated:** H100-class hardware, 40% MFU, the $6ND$ approximation, and that all 512 GPUs ran the whole time. Changing MFU to 30% gives 5.7B; to 50% gives 9.6B. So the honest answer is **"somewhere in the 6–10B range, most likely around 8B."** Giving a range with the sensitivity shown is a better answer than a single number, and in an interview it is the answer that scores.

**Sanity check against convention:** 8B parameters on 8T tokens is 1,000 tokens per parameter — well past Chinchilla's ~20:1, which is completely normal for a modern release where inference cost dominates (§8.11).

**2. Implied MFU.** It was an input, not an output — which is the actual lesson. With one equation and two unknowns ($N$ and MFU) you cannot solve for both. You need an independent estimate of one. Recognizing that is the point of the question.

**3. Three things to check.**

- **Loss curve shape.** Smooth decline, no unexplained spikes (§8.13), and the LR schedule's cosine visible in it.
- **Throughput stability over time.** A gradual decline usually means a degrading node or a filesystem filling up; a step change means something was rescheduled (Chapter 7).
- **Actual data consumption.** Did it consume 8T *distinct* tokens, or did the data loader restart and re-serve the first portion? Chapter 21 §21.6 — and on a run with checkpoints every 45 minutes, there were many resume opportunities.

**4. Checkpoint interval.**

Estimate cluster MTBF: at 50,000 GPU-hours per failure across 512 GPUs, $50{,}000 / 512 \approx 98$ hours between failures. Call it ~4 days, and round down to ~48 hours to account for non-GPU components.

For an 8B model, a checkpoint is $8\times10^9 \times 12 \text{ bytes} \approx 96$ GB of optimizer and master-weight state. Synchronously at 50 GB/s, about 2 seconds; call $C = 5$ s with overhead.

$$\tau_{\text{opt}} = \sqrt{2 \times 5 \times 172{,}800} \approx \sqrt{1.73\times10^6} \approx 1{,}315 \text{ s} \approx \mathbf{22 \text{ minutes}}$$

So 45 minutes is roughly **2× too long** — though not badly wrong, and the curve near the optimum is flat, so the excess cost is small. The bigger question the number raises: at a 48-hour MTBF you expect ~10 failures over 21 days, and if detection and rescheduling take longer than the ~22 minutes of lost work, that is where the effort should go instead (§21.7).

## 1.6 🏗️ The one-page summary

A complete version:

> DeepSeek-V3 is a 671B-parameter Mixture-of-Experts model with 37B active per token, trained on 14.8T multilingual tokens across 2,048 H800 GPUs in under two months of pre-training (2,788K GPU-hours in all, $5.576M at an assumed $2/hour for the final run). Four choices made it unusual: **Multi-head Latent Attention**, caching one 512-d latent plus a 64-d RoPE key per token instead of per-head K and V, which is what makes 671B servable; **DeepSeekMoE**, with 256 fine-grained routed experts plus 1 shared expert and an auxiliary-loss-*free* load-balancing scheme; **FP8 mixed precision** for the bulk of the matmuls, one of the first production-scale FP8 runs; and **DualPipe**, a pipeline schedule that hides the MoE all-to-all behind computation. It also used **multi-token prediction** for a denser training signal.

**Load-bearing versus decorative** — the useful part of this exercise:

**Load-bearing.** The MoE total/active split (it determines the entire memory-versus-compute trade). MLA (without it the model is not servable). FP8 (roughly halves the compute bill and is why the cost figure is what it is). The token count (it sets the data pipeline's scale, Chapter 3).

**Decorative on a first pass.** The exact GPU-hour figure. MTP — real and a second-order contribution. DualPipe — an important engineering achievement whose *function* (overlap communication with compute) matters more than its name.

If you omitted MLA or the total/active distinction, reread §1.2 and §1.4 before Chapter 5. Those two facts carry more of Part II than anything else in Chapter 1.

## 1.7 📄 Find the hedge

**1 & 2.** The report states the training cost in terms of GPU-hours multiplied by an assumed H800 rental price, and it is explicit that the figure covers **the official training of DeepSeek-V3** — excluding prior research, ablation experiments on architectures and algorithms, and data-related costs.

**3. What the "$5.5M frontier model" claim does and does not support.**

*Supported:* the final training run of a model competitive with contemporaneous frontier models consumed roughly that much rented compute. This is a genuine and significant result, and it is largely attributable to the FP8 training and the MoE architecture.

*Not supported:* that a lab could produce DeepSeek-V3 for $5.5M. Excluded are all the research that produced the recipe, all failed runs, all ablations, the data pipeline, post-training, salaries, and the cluster's capital cost.

**4. What would make two numbers comparable.**

Both labs would need to disclose: the same cost boundary (final run only, or total program); the assumed hardware price and whether it is rental or amortized capital; the FLOP-counting convention and the precision (§1.2 part 4); and whether failed runs are included. In practice essentially no two published figures share all four, which is why cross-lab cost comparisons are close to meaningless.

The general skill: **when a number circulates widely, find the sentence that defines it.** It usually says less than the number implies, and the gap is where the interesting reading is.

## 1.8 🔢 The failure budget

**1. Expected time between GPU failures.**

$$\frac{50{,}000 \text{ hours}}{2{,}048} \approx \mathbf{24.4 \text{ hours}}$$

**2. Failures over 60 days.**

$$\frac{60 \times 24}{24.4} \approx \mathbf{59 \text{ failures}}$$

**3. Fraction of the run lost.**

$$\frac{59 \times 25 \text{ min}}{60 \times 24 \times 60 \text{ min}} = \frac{1{,}475}{86{,}400} \approx \mathbf{1.7\%}$$

Which sounds tolerable, and would be — if the model were right.

**4. Reconciling with Meta's numbers.**

Meta: 419 unexpected interruptions over 54 days on 16,384 GPUs.

$$\text{observed} = \frac{54 \times 24}{419} \approx 3.1 \text{ hours between interruptions}$$

Scale the naive model to 16,384 GPUs: $50{,}000 / 16{,}384 \approx 3.05$ hours.

**Those agree almost exactly**, which is suspicious in a useful way — because the naive model counts *only GPUs*, and Meta's 419 interruptions included NICs, cables, switches, host memory, storage, and software. If GPUs alone predicted the observed rate, the per-GPU MTBF must be considerably *worse* than 50,000 hours, with non-GPU failures filling the rest.

**What the model is missing:**

- **50,000 GPU-hours is optimistic** for a GPU at sustained 100% utilization with high memory pressure and thermal load. Real figures under this duty cycle are worse.
- **Non-GPU components.** Meta attributed ~78% of unexpected interruptions to *confirmed hardware* issues, of which GPUs were the largest but not the only category.
- **Failures are not independent.** A rack loses power, a switch fails, a cooling loop trips — one event takes out many GPUs at once. The Poisson assumption underestimates the variance badly, even when it gets the mean right.
- **Correlated software failures.** A bad driver update or a filesystem hiccup hits everything simultaneously.
- **Detection and rescheduling dominate.** The 25-minute figure assumes you notice promptly. A *hang* — as opposed to a crash — can go undetected for a long time, and hangs are common (§7.13).

The takeaway that matters: **the naive model gets the right order of magnitude and the wrong shape.** Planning for 59 independent 25-minute events is very different from planning for a handful of correlated multi-hour outages plus many small ones. Chapter 21 is about making the small ones cheap; Chapter 7 is about the large correlated ones.
