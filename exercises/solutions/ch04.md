## 4.1 🔢 The embedding tax

**1. Embedding table parameters** ($V \times d$, with $d = 8{,}192$):

| Vocabulary | Parameters |
|---|---|
| 32K | $32{,}768 \times 8{,}192 = \mathbf{0.27\text{B}}$ |
| 128K | $131{,}072 \times 8{,}192 = \mathbf{1.07\text{B}}$ |
| 256K | $262{,}144 \times 8{,}192 = \mathbf{2.15\text{B}}$ |

**2. Untied, both tables:** double it — 0.54B, 2.15B, 4.29B.

**3. Fraction of a 65B-non-embedding model** (untied):

| Vocabulary | Total | Embedding share |
|---|---|---|
| 32K | 65.5B | **0.8%** |
| 128K | 67.1B | **3.2%** |
| 256K | 69.3B | **6.2%** |

**4. When does it exceed 10%, and why it matters beyond parameters.**

At $d = 8{,}192$ and 65B non-embedding, even 256K stays at 6.2%. You would need roughly a 420K vocabulary to cross 10%.

**But the parameter count is the least important consequence**, and that is the point of the question. Three things matter more:

- **The output softmax is computed over the whole vocabulary at every position.** For a batch of $B \times T$ positions, the final projection is a $(B \cdot T, d) \times (d, V)$ matmul. At $V = 256$K, $d = 8{,}192$, and 8,192 positions, that is $2 \times 8192 \times 8192 \times 262144 \approx 3.5\times10^{13}$ FLOPs — comparable to several transformer layers, for one operation.
- **The logits tensor is enormous.** $(8192, 262144)$ in FP32 for the loss computation is 8.6 GB, per micro-batch. This is a real memory pressure point and it is why fused cross-entropy kernels exist.
- **Embedding gradients are sparse and awkward under tensor parallelism.** The vocabulary dimension is what you shard, and it interacts with the alignment constraints kernels want.

The lesson: **vocabulary size is an inference-and-memory decision far more than a parameter-count decision.** Chapter 4 §4.4 makes this point; the arithmetic here is why.

## 4.2 🔢 Fertility and the compute bill

**1. Tokens for 1,000 words:** English 1,300; Vietnamese 2,450; Japanese 1,850; Python 1,600.

**2. Compute share by token count.**

Take 1,000 words as the unit and weight by the word-count shares:

| Language | Word share | Tokens per 1,000 words | Weighted tokens | Token share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| English | 40% | 1,300 | 520 | **30.6%** |
| Vietnamese | 20% | 2,450 | 490 | **28.8%** |
| Japanese | 20% | 1,850 | 370 | **21.8%** |
| Python | 20% | 1,600 | 320 | **18.8%** |
| | | | 1,700 | |

**Vietnamese is 20% of your content and 28.8% of your compute.** English is 40% of your content and 30.6% of your compute. The tokenizer has quietly reallocated nearly a third of your training budget.

**3. Improving Vietnamese fertility 2.45 → 1.60.**

New weighted tokens: $520 + 320 + 370 + 320 = 1{,}530$.

$$\frac{1{,}700 - 1{,}530}{1{,}700} = \mathbf{10.0\%\text{ of total compute freed}}$$

Ten percent of a multi-million-dollar training run, from a tokenizer change. That is the argument for spending real effort on tokenizer training, and it is the number that makes the case to a skeptical manager.

**4. The API billing question.**

A 500-word Vietnamese prompt costs $500 \times 2.45 = 1{,}225$ tokens; the same content in English costs $500 \times 1.30 = 650$ tokens.

$$\frac{1{,}225}{650} = \mathbf{1.88\times \text{ more expensive}}$$

**Is it defensible?** Partially, and the honest answer has two halves.

*The defensible half:* tokens are the unit of compute. A Vietnamese request genuinely costs you more to serve, because it really is more forward passes. Billing by token is billing by cost.

*The indefensible half:* the fertility gap is **a property of your tokenizer, which is your choice**, not a property of the Vietnamese language. A tokenizer trained with more Vietnamese in its corpus would have lower fertility. So the user is paying 88% more because of a decision your team made about training-corpus composition — and paying it forever, since §4.5 means you cannot fix it after the fact.

This is the concrete form of what Chapter 4 §4.6 calls the "Chinese is punished" problem, and it generalizes to every under-represented language. It is a real equity issue in deployed systems, it is invisible in every benchmark, and the only time to fix it is before the tokenizer is trained.

## 4.3 🔢 Context length is a tokenizer property

**1. Words fitting in a 128K-token context** ($131{,}072 / \text{fertility}$):

| Language | Words |
|---|---|
| English | **100,825** |
| Vietnamese | **53,499** |
| Japanese | **70,850** |
| Python | **81,920** |

**2. A 60,000-word Vietnamese legal document.** **No.** It needs $60{,}000 \times 2.45 = 147{,}000$ tokens against a 131,072 limit. It overflows by 12%.

The same document in English would fit with 40% to spare.

**3. "Fits a 300-page book" — 75,000 words.**

| Language | Tokens needed | Fits in 131,072? |
|---|---|---|
| English | 97,500 | **Yes** |
| Vietnamese | 183,750 | **No** — 40% over |
| Japanese | 138,750 | **No** — 6% over |
| Python | 120,000 | Yes |

The claim holds in two of four and fails in two, including one that fails badly.

**4. Why "128K context" misleads, and what to report instead.**

The window is measured in tokens, but every user-facing quantity — a document, a book, a codebase, a conversation — is measured in content. The conversion factor between them is the tokenizer's fertility, which varies by roughly 2× across languages.

**What to report:** effective context in *words or characters, per language*. "128K tokens ≈ 100K English words ≈ 53K Vietnamese words." That is a claim a user can act on.

And note this compounds with §9.11's separate problem: the *advertised* 128K may itself exceed the *effective* context length at which the model still performs, per RULER [\[49\]](../../book/appendix/b-references.md#49-ruler). A Vietnamese user faces both discounts at once.

## 4.4 🏗️ Choose a vocabulary

**1. The choice: 128K.** (Specifically something like 131,072 = $2^{17}$, or a nearby multiple of 128 for kernel alignment.)

**2. Fertility.** Four scripts with genuinely different needs — Latin with diacritics (Vietnamese), kanji/kana (Japanese), plain Latin (English), and code's punctuation-dense token distribution. 32K cannot give all four reasonable coverage; §4.2 showed the compute cost of getting Vietnamese wrong is ~10% of the run. 128K allows each script a meaningful allocation.

**3. Embedding parameters.** From §4.1, at 30B scale (say $d \approx 6{,}144$), a 128K untied vocabulary is $2 \times 131{,}072 \times 6{,}144 = 1.6$B parameters — about 5% of the model. Acceptable. 256K would be 10%, which starts to cost real capacity that would be better spent on layers.

**4. Softmax cost.** The output projection scales linearly in $V$. Going 128K → 256K doubles the cost of one of the most expensive single operations in the forward pass, and doubles the logits tensor's memory. This is the strongest argument against going larger, and it is the one most often overlooked.

**5. Rare-token undertraining.** See §4.9 for the full calculation. Under a Zipf distribution over 6T tokens, the least-frequent tokens in a 256K vocabulary receive on the order of $10^5$–$10^6$ occurrences — which is enough, but the *marginal* tokens between 128K and 256K contribute vanishingly little fertility improvement for their softmax cost.

**6. `uint16` storage.** 128K exceeds 65,536, so this vocabulary forces `uint32` and doubles corpus storage from 30 TB to 60 TB (Ex 3.8). Real, and **not decisive** — 30 TB of extra storage is cheap against a 10% compute swing. But it is worth knowing you are paying it rather than discovering it. A 64K vocabulary would be the choice if storage were binding.

**7. What would make 32K right?**

You would have to believe: the model is English-only or nearly so; you are strongly capacity-constrained and want every parameter in layers rather than embeddings; inference cost is dominated by the output softmax; and you will never need to serve the languages in the brief. Essentially, a small English-only model where the softmax dominates. For the stated brief — four scripts including two non-Latin — 32K is not defensible, and the §4.2 arithmetic is why.

## 4.5 🔢 Why you cannot re-tokenize

**1. Why the weights do not survive.**

Every weight in the model was learned against a specific mapping from text to integer IDs. Change the tokenizer and:

- **The embedding table is meaningless.** Row 4,721 was the vector for whatever the old tokenizer's token 4,721 was. Under a new tokenizer it is a different string, so every row is now attached to the wrong meaning.
- **The output projection is meaningless** for the same reason, in reverse.
- **And critically, the internal representations depend on the tokenization.** The model learned that certain subword sequences compose into words. A different segmentation of the same text produces a different sequence of embeddings, so every downstream layer sees an input distribution it was never trained on.

It is not that the embedding table needs remapping. The whole model was trained in a coordinate system you just deleted.

**2. Cost of retraining from scratch, 6T tokens on a 30B model.**

$$\text{FLOPs} = 6 \times 30\times10^9 \times 6\times10^{12} = 1.08\times10^{24}$$

At 990 TFLOP/s and 40% MFU: $1.08\times10^{24} / (990\times10^{12} \times 0.40) = 2.73\times10^9$ GPU-seconds $= 757{,}000$ GPU-hours.

On 512 GPUs: **62 days.** At $2/GPU-hour: **≈ $1.5M**, before counting the ablations you would redo.

**3. Three partial mitigations.**

- **Vocabulary extension.** Add new Vietnamese tokens to the existing vocabulary, initialize their embeddings (mean of existing, or from the constituent old tokens), and continue pre-training on Vietnamese-heavy data. *Cost:* the new tokens are undertrained relative to the rest, the old bad segmentation is still what the model prefers, and you have spent a mid-training run. Improves fertility for *new* text; does not undo what the model learned.
- **Continued pre-training on more Vietnamese**, without touching the tokenizer. *Cost:* does nothing for fertility — the 2.45 tokens/word remains — so the compute and context-length penalties stay. Improves quality, not efficiency.
- **Accept it and fix it in the next generation.** *Cost:* honest, and it is what most labs actually do. The tokenizer is a once-per-model-family decision.

**4. The check that would have caught it, in under a day.**

**Measure fertility per language on a held-out sample, before training anything.** Tokenize 10,000 documents in each target language with the candidate tokenizer, compute tokens-per-word, and compare against a reference tokenizer known to handle that language well.

This takes an afternoon. It would have shown Vietnamese at 2.45 against a plausible 1.6, and the §4.2 arithmetic turns that into "you are about to waste 10% of a $1.5M run." It is the single highest-return check in the whole pre-training pipeline relative to its cost, and it is skipped constantly.

## 4.6 🏗️ Special tokens, planned properly

**1. The set.**

| Token | Purpose |
|---|---|
| `<\|bos\|>` / `<\|eos\|>` | Sequence boundaries |
| `<\|pad\|>` | Padding (distinct from EOS — conflating them causes §11.4 bugs) |
| `<\|system\|>`, `<\|user\|>`, `<\|assistant\|>` | Chat roles (§11.6) |
| `<\|end\|>` | Turn terminator |
| `<\|tool_call\|>`, `<\|tool_result\|>` | Tool protocol (§18.2) — note `tool_result` is a *distinct role*, not a user turn |
| `<\|think\|>`, `<\|/think\|>` | Reasoning trace delimiters (§15.4) |
| `<\|fim_prefix\|>`, `<\|fim_suffix\|>`, `<\|fim_middle\|>` | Fill-in-the-middle code completion |
| `<\|repo_name\|>`, `<\|file_sep\|>` | Repository-level code context |
| `<\|reserved_0\|>` … `<\|reserved_N\|>` | The point of part 2 |

**2. How many reserved tokens, and why.**

**Allocate 128 to 256.** The cost is trivial — 256 tokens at $d = 6{,}144$ is 1.6M parameters, about 0.005% of a 30B model. The benefit is that any protocol extension over the next three years becomes a *rename* rather than a vocabulary resize.

What a resize costs, from [Exercise 11.10](../ch11.md) part 4: untrained embedding rows adjacent to rows shaped by trillions of tokens, an output projection with arbitrary initial probabilities for the new tokens, tensor-parallel resharding because the vocabulary dimension changed, and possible breakage of a padding-to-multiple-of-128 alignment your kernels assumed.

Llama, Qwen, and most modern releases ship reserved tokens for exactly this reason.

**3. Placement and alignment.**

Put special tokens at the **top** of the ID space, immediately below the vocabulary size, so the ordinary learned tokens occupy a contiguous block starting at 0. This keeps merges and vocabulary tooling simple, and it means adding specials later does not renumber anything.

**The alignment constraint:** the vocabulary size should be a multiple of 128 (ideally 256) for tensor-core efficiency and for clean sharding across tensor-parallel ranks. Pick the total first — 131,072 — then partition it into learned tokens plus specials plus reserves, rather than picking a learned-token count and adding specials on top.

**4. Reserved-token embeddings during pre-training.**

If a reserved token never appears in the data, its embedding row **never receives a gradient** and remains at its random initialization for the entire run. At the end you have a row whose norm is wildly out of distribution relative to every trained row — typically much larger, since converged embeddings shrink.

The fix, in ascending order of care:

- **Initialize to the mean of the trained embeddings at the end of pre-training**, before any fine-tuning uses the token.
- **Better: inject the reserved tokens into the pre-training data at low frequency**, in innocuous contexts, so they receive some gradient and settle into a sensible region.
- **Verify before use:** compare the reserved rows' norms against the distribution of trained rows. An order-of-magnitude mismatch is the visible symptom, and it takes one line to check.

## 4.7 📄 Compare three real vocabularies

**1. Vocabulary sizes.** As embedding rows (`vocab_size`): Llama 3 128,256, DeepSeek-V3 129,280, Qwen3 151,936. As tokenizer entries: 128,256, 128,818 and 151,669; the embedding table is padded past the tokenizer ([fact sheet](../../book/appendix/fact-sheets/tokenizers.md)). All three landed in the same order of magnitude, which is itself informative: the 128K–150K range is where the field converged after the 32K era.

**2 & 3.** Do the measurement yourself — it is a ten-minute script and the numbers are more convincing when you generate them. What you should find:

- **English:** all three are close. This is the saturated case; no vocabulary in this size range struggles with English.
- **Chinese:** DeepSeek and Qwen are meaningfully better than Llama 3. Both teams trained on Chinese-heavy corpora and allocated vocabulary accordingly.
- **Python:** all three are decent; code has been a priority everywhere since 2023.

**No single one wins across the board**, and that is the expected result. Vocabulary allocation is zero-sum — tokens given to Chinese are tokens not given to something else.

**4. What it tells you about target markets.**

The vocabulary is one of the most honest signals a model ships, because it cannot be adjusted after training and it directly encodes what the team optimized for. A tokenizer with strong Chinese coverage was trained by a team for whom Chinese is a first-class market, not an afterthought. Compare the fertility numbers across languages and you have, in effect, read the team's priority list.

This generalizes: **when evaluating an open model for a language you care about, measure its fertility on your language before anything else.** It predicts the compute cost, the effective context length (§4.3), and the API bill your users will pay, and it is a five-minute check.

## 4.8 🧪 Break the tokenizer lab

**1. English-only BPE, measured on Vietnamese.**

*Prediction people make:* fertility around 2, since Vietnamese uses the Latin alphabet.

*What happens:* considerably worse, typically 3–4+. Vietnamese diacritics (ế, ộ, ữ) are multi-byte in UTF-8 and appear in no English merge, so they fragment to individual bytes. A Vietnamese word frequently becomes one token per *byte* rather than per subword.

The Latin-alphabet intuition is the trap: what matters is not the script but whether the *byte sequences* appeared in the merge-training corpus.

**2. Doubling the vocabulary.**

*Prediction:* everything improves proportionally.

*What happens:* improvement is very uneven, and **the language with the worst starting fertility improves most**. BPE greedily adds the merges that save the most tokens, so the doubled budget goes disproportionately to the under-served scripts — provided they are present in the merge-training corpus. If Vietnamese was not in the corpus at all, doubling the vocabulary does nothing for it, which is the §4.5 lesson in miniature.

**3. Removing the pre-tokenizer regex.**

*Prediction:* merges get a bit messier.

*What happens:* merges begin spanning word boundaries and whitespace. You get tokens like `" the"`, `"ing the"`, `". The"` — and worse, merges spanning *punctuation and digits*, so `"2023"` and `"2024"` become unrelated single tokens while arithmetic becomes impossible to represent consistently.

This is why every production tokenizer has a pre-tokenizer regex, and why the GPT-2/GPT-4 regex patterns (splitting on whitespace, digit runs, and letter/non-letter boundaries) are so widely copied. Digit handling in particular — splitting numbers into consistent chunks — is a deliberate design choice that directly affects arithmetic ability.

**4. 90% code corpus.**

*Prediction:* English gets somewhat worse.

*What happens:* English fertility degrades noticeably, and **the effect is asymmetric**. Code's token distribution is dominated by punctuation, indentation runs, and a small set of keywords, so BPE spends its merge budget on `"    "`, `"();"`, `"self."`, `"def "`. English prose loses the multi-character subwords that make it efficient.

The asymmetry: a code-heavy tokenizer handles English badly, but an English-heavy tokenizer handles code *tolerably* — because code contains a great deal of English (identifiers, comments, keywords). The lesson for mixture design: **code needs explicit vocabulary allocation; English partially comes along for free.**

## 4.9 🔢 The rare-token problem

**1. Top 1,000 tokens' share, Zipf exponent 1.**

Under Zipf, $f(r) \propto 1/r$, so the share of the top $k$ of $V$ ranks is approximately

$$\frac{H_k}{H_V} \approx \frac{\ln k + \gamma}{\ln V + \gamma}$$

with $\gamma \approx 0.577$. For $k = 1{,}000$, $V = 131{,}072$:

$$\frac{\ln 1000 + 0.577}{\ln 131072 + 0.577} = \frac{6.908 + 0.577}{11.783 + 0.577} = \frac{7.485}{12.360} \approx \mathbf{60.6\%}$$

**Under 1% of the vocabulary accounts for about 60% of all token occurrences.**

**2. Occurrences of the 100,000th token.**

$$f(r) = \frac{D}{r \cdot H_V} = \frac{6\times10^{12}}{100{,}000 \times 12.360} \approx \mathbf{4.9 \times 10^6}$$

About 4.9 million occurrences.

**3. Is that enough to learn an embedding?**

**Yes, comfortably.** Five million occurrences is a very large number of gradient updates for a single $d$-dimensional vector. For comparison, a word that a human encounters a few thousand times in a lifetime is well-learned.

The intuition that rare tokens are undertrained is largely wrong *at this corpus scale*. It was a real concern at 300B tokens; at 6T it mostly is not. Zipf is brutal, but 6T is a very large number.

**4. Marginal value of tokens 128K–256K.**

Extending the vocabulary to 256K, the 256,000th token gets

$$\frac{6\times10^{12}}{256{,}000 \times (\ln 262144 + 0.577)} = \frac{6\times10^{12}}{256{,}000 \times 13.06} \approx 1.8\times10^6$$

Still 1.8 million occurrences — so still trainable. But its *contribution to fertility* is what matters, and that is tiny: by construction BPE added the highest-value merges first, so the second 128K tokens each save a vanishing number of tokens.

Meanwhile the softmax cost (§4.1) doubles, in full, for all of them.

**Conclusion: the marginal tokens are trainable and not worth their price.** This is the real argument against very large vocabularies, and it is a cost argument rather than an undertraining argument.

**5. The argument for going larger anyway.**

Because **you cannot re-tokenize** (§4.5), the vocabulary decision is irreversible for the life of the model family, and the two error directions are not symmetric:

- **Too large:** you pay a somewhat higher softmax cost forever. Measurable, bounded, and roughly linear in the excess.
- **Too small:** you pay a fertility penalty forever, on every token of training compute (§4.2: ~10% of the run) *and* every token of inference *and* every user's context length (§4.3) *and* every user's bill — with no remedy short of a full retrain.

Given asymmetric costs and an irreversible decision, you should err on the side of the *bounded* error. That is the argument for making the vocabulary somewhat larger than a pure fertility-versus-softmax optimization would suggest — and it is a large part of why the field moved from 32K to 128K and settled there.

The general form of this reasoning is worth keeping: **for an irreversible decision with asymmetric error costs, optimize for the tolerable failure, not the expected value.**
