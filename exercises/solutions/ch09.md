## 9.1 🔢 The cost of long context

$d = 6144$, 60 layers, GQA 8 KV heads × 128 head dim. Take $d_{\text{ff}} = 4d = 24{,}576$ (approximating SwiGLU's three matrices as an effective $3 \times \frac{8}{3}d$).

**1. Attention FLOPs per layer, one sequence.**

The $O(s^2)$ part is $QK^\top$ and the $PV$ product, each $2 s^2 d$ (using full $d$ for the query side):

$$\text{attn}_{O(s^2)} \approx 4 s^2 d$$

| $s$ | Per layer | All 60 layers |
|---|---|---|
| 8,192 | $4 \times 8192^2 \times 6144 = 1.65\times10^{12}$ | $9.9\times10^{13}$ |
| 32,768 | $2.64\times10^{13}$ | $1.58\times10^{15}$ |
| 131,072 | $4.22\times10^{14}$ | $2.53\times10^{16}$ |

**2. FFN FLOPs per layer** ($2 \times 3 \times s \times d \times d_{\text{ff}}$ for SwiGLU's three matrices):

$$6 s d \cdot d_{\text{ff}} = 6 \times s \times 6144 \times 24576 = 9.06\times10^8 \cdot s$$

| $s$ | Per layer | All 60 layers |
|---|---|---|
| 8,192 | $7.42\times10^{12}$ | $4.45\times10^{14}$ |
| 32,768 | $2.97\times10^{13}$ | $1.78\times10^{15}$ |
| 131,072 | $1.19\times10^{14}$ | $7.13\times10^{15}$ |

**3. Crossover.**

Attention overtakes FFN when $4 s^2 d > 6 s d \cdot d_{\text{ff}}$, i.e.

$$s > \frac{6 d_{\text{ff}}}{4} = 1.5 \times 24{,}576 = \mathbf{36{,}864 \text{ tokens}}$$

Confirmed by the tables: at 32K, FFN ($1.78\times10^{15}$) still exceeds attention ($1.58\times10^{15}$); at 128K, attention ($2.53\times10^{16}$) is 3.5× the FFN.

**The useful form of this result:** the crossover is at roughly $1.5 \times d_{\text{ff}}$, or about $6d$ for a standard FFN ratio. **Below ~32K, long context is cheap; above it, attention takes over and the cost curve bends upward.** That single number explains why 32K became a common intermediate stage and why 128K is qualitatively more expensive than 32K rather than merely 4× more.

**4. Ratio for 5B tokens at 128K versus 8K.**

Per *token* cost (dividing the per-sequence totals by $s$):

- At 8K: $(9.9\times10^{13} + 4.45\times10^{14})/8192 = 6.65\times10^{10}$ FLOPs/token
- At 128K: $(2.53\times10^{16} + 7.13\times10^{15})/131072 = 2.45\times10^{11}$ FLOPs/token

$$\text{ratio} = \frac{2.45\times10^{11}}{6.65\times10^{10}} \approx \mathbf{3.7\times}$$

Note this is the *per-token* ratio, and it is much less alarming than the per-sequence $16\times$. This distinction is the key to §9.2: you pay 3.7× per token at 128K, not 16×, because a 128K sequence contains 16× more tokens.

## 9.2 🔢 Budget the mid-training phase

**1. Progressive budget.**

| Stage | Context | Tokens | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8K → 32K | 40B | The larger jump in *number of newly-seen positions* (24K new); needs the most data |
| 2 | 32K → 128K | 15B | Fewer relative steps in log-position space; the model has learned the extension pattern |
| 3 | 128K anneal | 5B | Settle at the final length with the annealing mixture |
| | | **60B** | **1% of the bulk run** |

The reasoning for front-loading: RoPE frequencies are logarithmic in position, so 8K → 32K and 32K → 128K are each a 2-octave step, but the first step is where the model learns *that* it must attend at longer distances at all. The second step is more of the same skill.

**2. Compute cost as a fraction of the bulk run.**

Using per-token FLOPs from §9.1, and taking 32K's per-token cost as $(1.58\times10^{15} + 1.78\times10^{15})/32768 = 1.03\times10^{11}$:

$$40\text{B} \times 1.03\times10^{11} + 20\text{B} \times 2.45\times10^{11} = 4.1\times10^{21} + 4.9\times10^{21} = 9.0\times10^{21}$$

Bulk run: $6\times10^{12} \times 6.65\times10^{10} = 3.99\times10^{23}$.

$$\frac{9.0\times10^{21}}{3.99\times10^{23}} = \mathbf{2.3\%}$$

**3. If the whole 6T had been at 128K.**

$$6\times10^{12} \times 2.45\times10^{11} = 1.47\times10^{24}$$

$$\frac{1.47\times10^{24}}{3.99\times10^{23}} = \mathbf{3.7\times \text{ the bulk run}}$$

**4. The case for mid-training, in one sentence.**

**Mid-training buys 128K context for about 2% of the pre-training budget, where training at 128K throughout would have cost 3.7× the entire run** — a factor of roughly 160 in efficiency, for a capability that is essentially as good.

That number is why every frontier model does context extension as a separate phase rather than training long from the start. It is one of the highest-leverage structural decisions in the whole pipeline.

## 9.3 🔢 RoPE extension arithmetic

**1. Out-of-distribution dimension pairs at 128K.**

From [Exercise 5.5](../ch05.md), a pair is in-distribution if $\lambda_i \lesssim L_{\text{train}} = 8{,}192$, which gave $i \le 49.8$ of 64 pairs — so **roughly 14 pairs are out of distribution at any length beyond 8K**, and going to 128K does not change *which* pairs are affected, only *how far* out of distribution they go.

At 128K, those 14 pairs see angles up to 16× larger than anything in training.

**2. Position Interpolation.**

Scale factor $= L_{\text{train}}/L_{\text{target}} = 8192/131072 = \mathbf{1/16}$.

Every position $m$ is replaced by $m/16$, so position 131,071 is treated as position 8,192 — squarely in distribution.

**What it costs:** the *effective resolution* at short distances is reduced 16×. Positions 0 and 1, previously separated by a full rotation step, are now separated by $1/16$ of one. For the fast-rotating dimensions that encode fine-grained local position, adjacent tokens become 16× harder to distinguish.

This is PI's characteristic failure: it works at long range and **degrades short-range performance**, because it compresses the very resolution the model was using for local structure.

**3. NTK-aware scaling.**

The intuition: instead of compressing positions, stretch the *wavelengths* so the slowest one covers the new context. Scale $\theta$ by roughly $s^{d/(d-2)}$ where $s = 16$; for $d = 128$ that exponent is $128/126 \approx 1.016$:

$$\theta_{\text{new}} \approx 10{,}000 \times 16^{1.016} \approx 10{,}000 \times 16.5 \approx \mathbf{165{,}000}$$

Because the exponent $2i/d$ means high-frequency dimensions are barely affected by a change in $\theta$ while low-frequency ones are stretched nearly proportionally, this **stretches the slow dimensions much more than the fast ones** — which is exactly the targeted fix, achieved with a one-line change.

**4. YaRN.**

YaRN makes the per-dimension treatment explicit rather than emergent:

- **High-frequency dimensions** (wavelength ≪ $L_{\text{train}}$ — the model has seen their full angular range): **leave alone**. No interpolation, so short-range resolution is preserved exactly.
- **Low-frequency dimensions** (wavelength ≫ $L_{\text{train}}$ — never completed even one rotation in training): **interpolate fully**, as PI does, since the model has no learned behaviour for large angles here anyway.
- **Middle dimensions**: **ramp smoothly** between the two treatments.

It also applies an attention-temperature adjustment to compensate for the entropy change that comes from attending over longer sequences.

**Why it beats both extremes:** PI interpolates everything, damaging the high-frequency dimensions that did not need it. NTK-aware stretches everything by a smooth function of frequency, which is closer but is a single global rule rather than a decision per dimension about whether that dimension was ever in distribution. YaRN makes the in-distribution / out-of-distribution split explicit and treats each side correctly.

**5. Rankings.**

*Quality at 128K:* **YaRN > NTK-aware > PI.** All three make 128K usable; YaRN's per-dimension handling extends furthest before degrading.

*Quality retained at 8K:* **YaRN > NTK-aware ≫ PI.** PI is the clear loser here, for the §9.3 part 2 reason — it compresses short-range resolution by the full scale factor. YaRN preserves the high-frequency dimensions untouched, so short-context behaviour is nearly unchanged.

This is why frontier labs use YaRN despite it being the most complex of the three: it is the only one that does not force a trade between long-context capability and short-context quality.

## 9.4 🏗️ Design the annealing mix

**1. Proposed mixture.**

| Source | Bulk | Anneal | Shift | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Web | 50% | **25%** | 0.5× | Highest volume, lowest density. The bulk phase already extracted what it offers. |
| Code | 20% | **25%** | 1.25× | High-quality, verifiable, and it improves general reasoning. Worth upweighting. |
| Math | 10% | **18%** | 1.8× | Scarce, high-value, and it sets up the reasoning post-training (§9.12). |
| Multilingual | 15% | **12%** | 0.8× | Slight downweight; the capability is established and further repetition risks the §3.4 epoch problem. |
| Books | 5% | **12%** | 2.4× | Long-form coherence and high writing quality — exactly what benefits from being seen last. |
| Curated/reference | 0% | **8%** | new | Encyclopedic and textbook-quality material held back specifically for this phase. |

**2. Why it is disproportionately influential.**

Three mechanisms, and they compound:

- **Recency under a decaying LR.** The final gradients are the ones that are not subsequently overwritten by large updates. Data seen at LR $3\times10^{-5}$ leaves a more durable imprint on the final weights than data seen at $3\times10^{-4}$ 5T tokens ago, because there is no subsequent large update to move away from it.
- **The model is at the bottom of the loss basin.** Early training determines *which* basin; annealing determines *where in it* you settle. The final position is what you ship.
- **Post-training inherits it.** Everything in Part III starts from this checkpoint. §11.1's "a bad SFT stage cannot be recovered by good RL" has an upstream twin: a badly annealed base model constrains everything downstream.

**3. Risk of over-weighting one domain, and detection.**

The risk is **capability regression elsewhere**, and it can be sharp. Push math to 40% and the model's conversational fluency and world knowledge measurably degrade — the same capacity-allocation trade as §11.9, now at the pre-training layer.

**Detection:** run a *broad* evaluation suite on annealing checkpoints, not just the capability you are targeting. The specific check: compare each annealed checkpoint against the pre-annealing checkpoint on every benchmark, and look for *any* that regressed by more than a point or two. Because annealing is short and cheap, you can afford to run several mixtures and compare — which is the whole argument for part 4.

**4. Branching to three variants.**

Take **one bulk checkpoint** at the end of the stable phase, then run three separate annealing passes:

| Variant | Anneal mixture | Budget |
|---|---|---|
| General | The table above | 300B |
| Code specialist | 45% code, 20% math, 20% web, 15% other | 300B |
| Math specialist | 40% math, 25% code, 20% web, 15% other | 300B |

Total extra cost: 600B tokens beyond the general variant — about 10% of the bulk run for two additional specialist models.

**What it requires of the LR schedule:** the branch point must be at a **known, stable LR that is the same for all three branches**, and each branch must run its own complete decay. Under a cosine schedule parameterized by total steps, "the LR at the branch point" is a specific value on a curve you committed to at step 0, and you cannot decay to zero three times from it without redefining the schedule.

**5. What that implies about cosine versus WSD.**

**It is a decisive argument for WSD.**

Under WSD (Exercise [8.2](../ch08.md) part 4), the stable phase is at constant LR by construction. *Any* checkpoint in the stable phase is a valid branch point, all branches start from the same LR, and each simply runs its own decay. Branching is free and requires no schedule surgery.

Under cosine, the LR is monotonically decreasing throughout, so a branch point is a specific value on a specific curve; running three decays from it means three ad-hoc schedule redefinitions, each of which introduces the discontinuity risk from §8.2 part 3.

This — the ability to branch cheaply for annealing experiments and specialist variants — is arguably a stronger practical argument for WSD than the "you do not need to know the run length in advance" one, and it is the reason labs producing model *families* rather than single models have moved to it.

## 9.5 🔢 Long-context evaluation

**1. Why 99% NIAH is not evidence.**

Needle-in-a-haystack asks the model to retrieve a single, verbatim, lexically distinctive fact from a long context. That is close to a **string-matching task**, and attention is very good at string matching over long distances even when it cannot *reason* over long distances.

Passing NIAH shows the information is reachable. It says nothing about whether the model can aggregate across multiple locations, track state, resolve references, or reason about relationships spanning the context — which is what long-context use actually requires.

NIAH is a necessary condition and a very weak one. A model that fails it is definitely broken; a model that passes it is unmeasured.

**2. A test that reveals the gap.**

The design principle: **make the task require combining information from multiple positions, and make retrieval insufficient.**

A concrete protocol:

- Build tasks at 8K, 16K, 32K, 64K, 128K where the *task* is identical and only the context length varies (pad with distractor text of the same distribution).
- Include several task types spanning difficulty (part 3).
- Define **effective context length** as the longest length at which accuracy remains within some threshold — say 85–90% — of the model's own 8K accuracy on the same task.
- Report per-task-type, because the effective length differs by task.

The self-relative baseline matters: comparing to the model's own short-context performance isolates the *length* effect from the model's general capability.

**3. Four task categories, increasing difficulty.**

1. **Single-needle retrieval.** One fact, verbatim, distinctive. The easy case.
2. **Multi-needle retrieval.** Several facts at different depths, all required. Now attention must find $k$ things, not one, and performance degrades with $k$.
3. **Multi-hop / aggregation.** Facts that must be combined — "how many of the mentioned items are red," or a chain where fact A points to the location of fact B. Requires reasoning over retrieved content, not just retrieval.
4. **Variable tracking / state.** Follow a variable's value through many reassignments scattered across the context. This is the hardest standard category, because there is no distinctive string to match on — the model must maintain state, and distractors are *the same variable with different values*.

RULER's contribution is systematizing exactly this ladder and showing that models fall off it at very different lengths per rung.

**4. Reporting a 32K effective length against a 128K advertised length.**

**Report both numbers, everywhere, to everyone.** Specifically:

- **In the model card and documentation:** "128K maximum context; effective context length 32K on RULER-style aggregation tasks, 128K on single-needle retrieval." Per-task, because a user doing retrieval genuinely gets 128K and a user doing multi-document reasoning does not.
- **To product and sales:** before they write the marketing copy, not after. The failure mode here is a customer discovering the gap in production.
- **To the post-training team:** because this is fixable — more long-context data in SFT (§11.7), and long-context-specific RL.
- **Externally:** labs that report both numbers are doing the professional thing, and per §23.8 it is becoming the expectation.

The framing that makes this an easy conversation rather than a hard one: **a 32K effective context is a good result**, and stating it precisely is more valuable to a user than a 128K number they will discover is optimistic. The reputational risk is in the discovery, not the number.

## 9.6 🔢 Diagnose six mid-training failures

| # | Symptom | Two likely causes | First check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8K perplexity −8%, 128K retrieval fine | (a) Position Interpolation used, compressing short-range resolution (§9.3 part 2); (b) the extension mixture had no short sequences, so short-context behaviour drifted | Check which RoPE method was used. If PI, switch to YaRN. Also check the extension data's length distribution — it should include short sequences, not only long ones. |
| 2 | 128K perplexity fine, 128K reasoning much worse | (a) The model retrieves but does not aggregate — exactly §9.5's effective-vs-advertised gap; (b) the extension data was long *documents* with local structure, teaching continuation rather than long-range reasoning | Run the §9.5 part 3 ladder. Then check the extension data: does it contain tasks *requiring* long-range aggregation, or just long text? |
| 3 | 32K trains, 128K OOMs at the same micro-batch | (a) Attention is not using FlashAttention, so the $O(s^2)$ matrix is materialized; (b) activation memory scaled linearly and you were already near the limit | Check whether FlashAttention varlen is actually active. A 16× memory jump from 32K→128K means the $O(s^2)$ term; a 4× jump means the linear term. The ratio identifies the cause. |
| 4 | Worked at small scale, failed at 30B | (a) The extension hyperparameters (LR, token budget) were tuned at small scale and do not transfer (§8.7 part 4); (b) at 30B the model had learned sharper short-range position behaviour, so it has more to lose from interpolation | Rerun the small-scale test with the *same* LR the 30B run used, in absolute terms. Extension is sensitive to LR — too high and it destroys short-context quality, too low and the long positions never adapt. |
| 5 | Fine to 100K, collapses past 120K | (a) The scale factor was set for a target below the advertised length; (b) something in the pipeline has a hard limit near 128K — a position-embedding table, a cache allocation, a mask construction that overflows | Check the RoPE scale factor against the actual target, then check for an off-by-one or an integer limit at exactly 131,072. A *collapse* rather than a *degradation* usually means a bug, not a quality issue. |
| 6 | 40× slower at 128K, not the predicted 16× | (a) The attention kernel is not tiling well at this length, or is falling back to a slower path; (b) recomputation policy changed under memory pressure, or the batch shrank so GEMMs are inefficient | Profile it. Compare the measured attention time against the §9.1 FLOP prediction to see whether the extra factor is in attention or elsewhere. A 2.5× discrepancy over the arithmetic is a kernel or configuration problem, not physics. |

## 9.7 📄 Compare three annealing descriptions

**1. What each calls it.**

- **Llama 3:** the **annealing phase** — explicitly named, describing LR decay on a high-quality mixture at the end of pre-training.
- **DeepSeek-V3:** treats **context-length extension** as a distinct named stage, separate from the bulk pre-training and from post-training.
- **Qwen3:** describes a multi-stage pre-training pipeline with a **long-context extension** stage (32K → 128K), and distinct stages with different mixture emphases.

The naming divergence is §9.1's point: the same work, three vocabularies.

**2. Token fractions.** Llama 3 is the most specific about the annealing phase being a small final portion of the budget. DeepSeek-V3 and Qwen3 describe their long-context stages in terms of stage token counts. All three allocate a small single-digit percentage or less of total tokens to these final phases — consistent with the §9.2 finding that this is a ~2% investment.

**3. Mixture shift.** All three describe upweighting high-quality sources for the final phase. Llama 3 is explicit that the annealing mixture emphasizes high-quality data. Qwen3 describes stage-specific mixture emphases including reasoning-relevant data. DeepSeek-V3 discusses the long-context data specifically.

**4. Most reproducible, and what is still missing.**

**Llama 3** gives the most operationally reproducible description of annealing as a technique — it names the phase, describes the mechanism, and reports the effect.

**What is still missing from all three:** the exact mixture proportions in the annealing phase, the exact token counts per stage, the LR values at the branch and end points, and — most importantly — **the ablations**. Nobody reports "we tried these five annealing mixtures and here is how they compared," which is the information you would actually need to design your own.

Same pattern as §19.8 and §3.6: **the mechanism is published, the data is not.**

## 9.8 🧪 Break the RoPE lab

**1. No modification, 2K → 8K.**

*Prediction:* perplexity degrades gradually with position.

*What happens:* perplexity is **fine up to about 2K and then rises sharply** — often catastrophically, by orders of magnitude, rather than gradually. The transition is much more abrupt than most people expect.

The reason is §5.5's: within the training length, all dimensions are in distribution. Past it, the slow dimensions immediately enter angles never seen, and the attention pattern they were encoding breaks. It is a cliff, not a slope.

**2. Position Interpolation.**

*Prediction:* long positions get much better.

*What happens:* long positions get much better **and short positions get measurably worse.** Plot perplexity against position and you see the cliff removed and the whole curve shifted slightly upward, including at position 100.

That short-context penalty is PI's signature and it is the thing to look for. It is small on a toy model and it is the reason PI is not used at frontier scale.

**3. NTK-aware.**

*Prediction:* similar to PI.

*What happens:* **better than PI at short positions, comparable or slightly worse at the longest positions.** Because the $\theta$ change barely affects high-frequency dimensions, short-range resolution is largely preserved.

The comparison to make: plot PI and NTK-aware on the same axes against position. The curves cross — PI is better at the extreme long end, NTK-aware is better everywhere else. That crossing is the whole design tension.

**4. YaRN.**

*Prediction:* best everywhere.

*What happens:* **best at short positions (essentially matching the unmodified model) and best or near-best at long positions.** It does not dominate by a large margin at the very longest positions — PI's aggressive interpolation is hard to beat at the extreme — but it gives up almost nothing at short range, which is the trade that matters.

Where YaRN does *not* obviously win: on a small toy model with few dimensions, the per-dimension distinction is coarse and the three methods converge. YaRN's advantage grows with head dimension, which is worth noticing as a limitation of what a lab-scale experiment can show.

**5. Which benefits most from fine-tuning.**

*Prediction people make:* they all improve equally.

*What happens:* **PI benefits most in relative terms.** Fine-tuning at the extended length lets the model re-learn the short-range resolution that PI compressed away, recovering much of the short-context loss. NTK-aware and YaRN start closer to correct, so they have less to recover.

The practical implication, and it is a genuinely useful one: **if you are going to fine-tune substantially at the extended length, the choice of extension method matters less than if you are applying it zero-shot.** PI plus adequate fine-tuning approaches YaRN plus fine-tuning. The methods differ most in the low-fine-tuning regime — which is exactly the regime the §9.2 budget puts you in, since 60B tokens is a small amount of adaptation.
