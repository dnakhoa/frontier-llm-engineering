## 13.1 🔢 The four-model budget

**1. Per model and total.**

| Model | Trained? | Bytes/param | Memory |
|---|---|---|---|
| Policy (70B) | Yes | 16 | 1,120 GB |
| Value (70B) | Yes | 16 | 1,120 GB |
| Reference (70B) | No | 2 | 140 GB |
| Reward (7B) | No | 2 | 14 GB |
| **Total** | | | **2,394 GB** |

**2. GPUs for static state.** $2{,}394 / 80 = \mathbf{30 \text{ H100s}}$ — before activations, before the KV cache the generation phase needs, before communication buffers.

**3. Value model shrunk to 7B.**

$$1{,}120 + 112 + 140 + 14 = \mathbf{1{,}386 \text{ GB}} \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{18 \text{ GPUs}}$$

A 42% saving from one decision. The cost is a less accurate value function, which raises advantage-estimate variance — but per §13.7, the value function is frequently the *least* reliable component anyway, so the trade is usually good.

**4. RLOO (no value model).**

$$1{,}120 + 140 + 14 = \mathbf{1{,}274 \text{ GB}} \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{16 \text{ GPUs}}$$

**5. Saving and what else it removes.**

$$\frac{2{,}394 - 1{,}274}{2{,}394} = \mathbf{47\% \text{ less memory}}$$

Beyond memory, deleting the value network removes:

- **Value-function divergence** as a failure mode (§13.7). The value model trains on a moving target — returns depend on the current policy — and when it diverges, `explained_variance` goes to zero and your advantages become noise. That entire class of failure disappears.
- **Three hyperparameters**: the GAE $\lambda$, the value loss coefficient, and the value clipping range.
- **A second optimizer state** to checkpoint and resume (Chapter 21).
- **The value model's forward and backward pass** from every training step.

The cost, per §13.8: $k$ generations per prompt instead of 1, and since generation dominates wall-clock (§13.2), that is a real trade — memory and stability bought with compute.

## 13.2 🔢 Where the wall-clock goes

**1. Generation.**

$$512 \text{ prompts} \times 600 \text{ tokens} = 307{,}200 \text{ tokens}$$
$$\frac{307{,}200}{2{,}400 \times 32} = \frac{307{,}200}{76{,}800} = \mathbf{4.0 \text{ s}}$$

**2. Reward and reference forward passes.**

Training throughput is 8,000 tok/s/GPU; a forward pass at 1/8 the cost of a full training step gives roughly $8{,}000 \times 8 = 64{,}000$ tok/s/GPU for a forward-only pass. Two such passes (reward, reference) over the prompt+response tokens — take ~1,800 tokens per sequence including prompts, so ~922k tokens:

$$2 \times \frac{922{,}000}{64{,}000 \times 32} = 2 \times 0.45 = \mathbf{0.9 \text{ s}}$$

**3. Four PPO epochs of training.**

$$4 \times \frac{922{,}000}{8{,}000 \times 32} = 4 \times 3.6 = \mathbf{14.4 \text{ s}}$$

**4. Fraction that is generation.**

Total: $4.0 + 0.9 + 14.4 = 19.3$ s.

$$\frac{4.0}{19.3} = \mathbf{21\%}$$

**Which contradicts Chapter 13's claim of 60–80%** — and that is deliberate. The setup here has 1 rollout per prompt, short responses, and 4 training epochs, which is the configuration *least* favourable to the generation-dominates story.

**5. With vLLM at 8× generation throughput.**

Generation drops to 0.5 s. Total: $0.5 + 0.9 + 14.4 = 15.8$ s, generation is **3%**, and **training is now the bottleneck at 91%**.

**The real lesson, and it is more useful than the headline number:** whether generation dominates depends entirely on $k$, response length, and epochs per batch. Recompute it for *your* configuration rather than repeating the 60–80% figure.

Where generation genuinely does dominate: RLOO or GRPO with $k = 8$–$64$ (8–64× the generation, same training), long reasoning traces of 5,000+ tokens rather than 600, and 1 training epoch per batch rather than 4. Under §13.7's configuration — $k=8$, 700 tokens — generation is $8 \times 700/600 \approx 9.3\times$ the work above, or ~37 s against ~14 s of training: **73% generation.** That is where the chapter's figure comes from, and it is the configuration modern post-training actually uses.

## 13.3 🔢 Baselines and variance

Rewards: `[2.1, 2.3, 1.9, 2.2, 2.0, 3.4, 2.1, 2.2]`. Mean = 2.275, sample std ≈ 0.451 (population std ≈ 0.422).

**1. No baseline.** The gradient weight is the raw reward:

$$[2.1,\; 2.3,\; 1.9,\; 2.2,\; 2.0,\; 3.4,\; 2.1,\; 2.2]$$

**All positive.** Every response's log-probability is pushed *up*, including the three below-average ones.

**2. RLOO leave-one-out.** $b_i = \frac{\text{sum} - r_i}{7}$, $A_i = r_i - b_i = \frac{8}{7}(r_i - \bar{r})$.

Sum = 18.2.

| $r_i$ | $b_i$ | $A_i$ |
|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | 2.300 | **−0.200** |
| 2.3 | 2.271 | **+0.029** |
| 1.9 | 2.329 | **−0.429** |
| 2.2 | 2.286 | **−0.086** |
| 2.0 | 2.314 | **−0.314** |
| 3.4 | 2.114 | **+1.286** |
| 2.1 | 2.300 | **−0.200** |
| 2.2 | 2.286 | **−0.086** |

**3. GRPO group-mean.** $A_i = r_i - 2.275$:

$$[-0.175,\; +0.025,\; -0.375,\; -0.075,\; -0.275,\; +1.125,\; -0.175,\; -0.075]$$

Dividing by the population std (0.422):

$$[-0.415,\; +0.059,\; -0.889,\; -0.178,\; -0.652,\; +2.665,\; -0.415,\; -0.178]$$

Note RLOO's advantages are exactly $8/7 = 1.143\times$ GRPO's unnormalized ones — the leave-one-out correction is a constant rescaling when the group size is fixed. **RLOO and unnormalized GRPO differ only by a scale factor**, which is worth knowing: the "unbiased versus biased" distinction between them has essentially no practical consequence, while the std division does.

**4. Largest positive weight.**

Under **no baseline**: sample 6 ($r = 3.4$) — but only barely ahead of sample 2 at 2.3, a ratio of 1.48.

Under **RLOO and GRPO**: sample 6, by an enormous margin. Its advantage is 3.0× the next-largest positive (RLOO: 1.286 vs 0.029) and it is the only strongly-positive sample.

**5. Why no baseline has ruinous variance.**

Look at the numbers. Without a baseline, the gradient is dominated by the **shared component** of the reward — every sample contributes a positive push of roughly 2.275, and the *informative* differences are ±0.4 around that.

So roughly **85% of the gradient magnitude is a signal that says "make all of these more likely,"** which is not what you want to learn, and only ~15% carries the comparative information that sample 6 was better.

Worse, the useless 85% does not cancel across the batch — it is a coherent push in whatever direction increases the probability of everything the policy already produces, which is a push toward *reducing entropy*. So the no-baseline gradient is both high-variance and systematically biased toward mode collapse.

Subtracting the baseline removes the shared component entirely, leaving pure comparative signal.

**6. All rewards zero.**

**RLOO:** $b_i = 0$ for all $i$, so $A_i = 0$ for all $i$. Every advantage is zero, the gradient is zero, **the prompt contributes nothing**.

**GRPO with std normalization:** mean = 0, std = 0, so $A_i = 0/(0 + \epsilon) = 0$ — also zero, saved from a division by zero only by the epsilon in the denominator.

**What goes wrong:** nothing numerically, and everything informationally. This prompt was completely wasted — you spent 8 generations on it and got no gradient.

This is §15.9's zero-variance-group problem, and it is why prompt difficulty calibration matters so much. If all 8 samples fail (or all succeed), the group is uniform and contributes nothing. **Your effective batch size is only the prompts where the group disagrees.**

Two consequences worth carrying:

- **Filter prompts by measured pass rate.** Prompts near 50% success are where the signal is; prompts at 0% or 100% are pure cost.
- **The epsilon in the denominator matters.** With $\epsilon = 10^{-4}$ and a genuinely tiny-but-nonzero std, GRPO's normalization *amplifies* noise enormously — advantages of $0.001/0.0001 = 10$. This is exactly the bias Dr. GRPO [\[70\]](../../book/appendix/b-references.md#70-dr-grpo) identifies, and it is an argument for dropping the std division.

## 13.4 🔢 The KL budget

**1. KL after 2,000 steps.** $0.4 \times 20 = \mathbf{8.0 \text{ nats}}$.

**2. When you cross 1.8 nats.**

$$\frac{1.8}{0.4} \times 100 = \mathbf{450 \text{ steps}}$$

You blow the budget less than a quarter of the way through the run you planned.

**3. Adaptive $\beta$ to hold 1.8.**

The KL rate is roughly inversely proportional to $\beta$ (a higher price on drift buys less drift). To go from a trajectory reaching 8.0 nats to one holding at 1.8:

$$\beta_{\text{new}} \approx 0.02 \times \frac{8.0}{1.8} \approx \mathbf{0.089}$$

Call it **0.09**, roughly 4.5× the original. In practice you would let the §13.6 controller find it rather than setting it directly — but the estimate tells you the controller needs to move $\beta$ by a large factor, not a small one, which is worth knowing before you set the controller's update rate.

**4. Why stop anyway, with reward still rising.**

Because **the reward is the proxy, and the proxy rises monotonically past the peak** (§12.6, Gao et al.). That is the defining finding: the proxy reward carries *no information* about whether you are past the optimum. It will keep rising all the way into the region where the model is genuinely worse.

The best-of-$n$ diagnostic ([Exercise 12.4](ch12.md) part 5) estimated that your reward model stops being trustworthy at ~1.8 nats. Past that, the policy is optimizing against your reward model's errors rather than against quality. Continuing produces a model with higher reward and lower quality, and the reward curve will look excellent the entire time.

The rising reward at step 450 is not evidence that you should continue. It is the expected behaviour whether you should continue or not, which is exactly why it cannot be used to decide.

**5. What would change my mind.**

**An independent evaluation that is still improving**, run on the current checkpoint. Specifically:

- **Pairwise comparison against the step-0 SFT model**, judged by humans or by a judge model that is *not* the reward model, on a fixed prompt set — and per [Exercise 12.3](ch12.md) part 3, **length-matched**, because otherwise you are measuring length inflation.
- **Held-out capability benchmarks** the reward model was not trained on.

If those are still improving at 1.8 nats, the best-of-$n$ estimate was conservative and you can extend the budget — and you should then re-estimate the budget from the new evidence rather than removing it.

If they are flat or declining while reward rises, you are past the peak and the diagnostic was right.

**The operational rule this implies:** run the independent evaluation on a schedule during RL, not at the end. It is the only measurement that can locate the peak, and locating it after the run is over is locating it too late.

## 13.5 🏗️ Diagnose six runs

| # | Curves | Failure | First change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reward ↑, KL ↑ unbounded, eval flat | **Reward hacking / overoptimization** (§12.6). The canonical signature. | Stop. Read 20 generations from the current checkpoint — you will find a specific phrase or structure the model has locked onto. Then roll back to the last checkpoint where eval was still improving, raise $\beta$, and set a KL target (§13.6). |
| 2 | Reward flat, KL ≈ 0, `clip_frac` ≈ 0 | **$\beta$ too high** — the KL penalty is so expensive the policy cannot move. You are burning GPU-hours reproducing the SFT model. | Lower $\beta$ by 3–5×. Check the reward scale first: if reward $\sigma \ll 1$, the KL term dominates the objective regardless of $\beta$, and the fix is reward normalization (§12.5), not $\beta$. |
| 3 | Reward ↑ then collapse at 900, entropy cliff at 850 | **Entropy collapse.** The policy became deterministic, so rollouts for the same prompt are identical, the group/batch carries no comparative signal, and learning stalls then degrades. | Roll back to step 800. Lower the learning rate, and add a small entropy bonus. Check the sampling temperature — a temperature that decayed or was set too low produces this directly. |
| 4 | Reward noisy, no trend, value `explained_variance` ≈ 0 | **Value-function divergence** (§13.7). The value model is uninformative, so advantages are noise and PPO is doing an expensive random walk. | The quick fix is to lower the value learning rate and increase value warmup. The better fix is **switch to RLOO** — this failure mode does not exist without a value network, and you get 47% of your memory back (§13.1). |
| 5 | Reward ↑, KL ↑, length 280→610, eval slightly down | **Length exploitation** (§12.6, and [Exercise 12.3](ch12.md)). The reward model prefers longer responses and the policy found it. | Measure the reward model's length correlation. Then: balance the RM's training pairs on length, or add a length penalty. Do *not* just lower $\beta$ — the problem is the reward, not the drift. |
| 6 | Reward and KL fine, `clip_frac` = 0.45 | **Batch reuse past its usefulness.** Nearly half the tokens are hitting the clip, meaning the policy has moved far from the one that generated the rollouts, so most of the batch contributes no gradient. | Reduce PPO epochs per batch from 4 to 1–2, or lower the learning rate. You are wasting most of each expensive rollout batch. |

**The cross-cutting point:** in five of six cases the *reward* curve was either fine or actively misleading. Reward is the least informative metric on the dashboard, and §13.11's list — KL, length, entropy, `clip_frac`, explained variance, independent eval — is where the diagnosis lives.

## 13.6 🏗️ Choose the method

**1. Math with an answer key, 100k prompts, 64 GPUs.**

**GRPO.** The reward is verifiable, so there is nothing to hack (§15.4) and the KL machinery matters far less; GRPO's group baseline needs no value network, which matters on only 64 GPUs. DPO would require synthesizing preference pairs from a signal that is already a clean binary reward, discarding information.

**2. General helpfulness, good reward model, 512 GPUs, experienced team.**

**PPO** — or RLOO if the team is willing. This is the one case where PPO's advantages are all available: you have the GPUs for four models, the expertise to tune seven hyperparameters, and — critically — a learned reward model that needs explicit KL targeting (§13.6) because overoptimization is a live risk.

**3. General helpfulness, public preference pairs, 8 GPUs, two engineers.**

**DPO.** Eight GPUs cannot hold PPO's four models, two engineers cannot operate a generation cluster, and the data is already pairs. DPO is one model and one loss, and it will produce a real improvement over SFT. Precompute the reference log-probabilities (§14.3) so you need only one model in memory.

**4. Code with unit tests, working in a week.**

**Rejection sampling** (§11.10), then GRPO if time remains. Execution is a perfect verifier, and rejection-sampling fine-tuning gets a large fraction of the benefit with none of RL's operational risk — no KL to tune, no value function, no rollout buffer. In one week, a working rejection-sampling loop beats a half-debugged GRPO run.

**5. Safety refusals, must state exactly how far the model moved.**

**PPO.** The requirement is *explicit KL control*, and PPO is the only method here that targets a KL you choose (§13.6). DPO buys an unknown amount of drift at a fixed price (§14.4) and cannot report how far it went; GRPO's KL term is usually small or absent. When "how far did it move" is an auditable requirement — which it is for safety — you need the method that answers it.

**6. Never run RL, want the most improvement per unit of risk.**

**Rejection sampling.** It is approximately a single very conservative policy-gradient step (§11.10), it is operationally identical to SFT, it cannot reward-hack through an RL loop, and it captures a large share of the available gain. Many teams reach for DPO or PPO before exhausting it, which is a sequencing mistake.

## 13.7 🔢 Generation is a serving problem

**1. Tokens per iteration.**

$$2{,}048 \text{ prompts} \times 8 \text{ samples} \times 700 \text{ tokens} = \mathbf{11.47\text{M tokens}}$$

**2. KV cache saved by prefix sharing.**

For a 70B GQA model, KV cache is 320 KB/token ([Chapter 22 §22.3](../../book/part-4-infra/22-inference-serving.md)).

Prompt cache per sequence: $1{,}200 \times 320 \text{ KB} = 384 \text{ MB}$.

- **Naive:** 8 copies per prompt = $8 \times 384 = 3.07$ GB per prompt.
- **Copy-on-write:** 1 copy = 384 MB per prompt.
- **Saved: 2.69 GB per prompt, an 87.5% reduction on the prompt portion.**

Across a batch of 64 concurrent prompts, that is **172 GB saved** — more than two H100s' worth of memory, from one feature of the inference engine.

This is why §22.5 says the GRPO rollout batch is *exactly* the workload paged attention is best at, and why running rollouts through `model.generate()` is not merely slower but memory-infeasible at realistic $k$.

**3. Over 500 iterations, versus the SFT run.**

$$500 \times 11.47\text{M} = \mathbf{5.74\text{B tokens generated}}$$

The SFT run from [Exercise 11.2](ch11.md) processed **2.59B tokens** of training.

So the RL run **generates more than twice as many tokens as the entire SFT run trains on** — and generation is far more expensive per token than training, because it is memory-bandwidth-bound and autoregressive (§22.2) rather than compute-bound and parallel.

**4. Where post-training compute actually goes.**

**Into generation, not into gradient steps.**

[Exercise 11.2](ch11.md) established that the SFT training run is ~0.017% of pre-training compute — a rounding error. This exercise establishes that the *generation* for one RL run exceeds it several times over, and that is before counting rejection sampling ([Exercise 11.6](ch11.md): 117–933 GPU-hours per round) and before counting evaluation.

The consequences, which are the actual point:

- **Post-training's cost structure is an inference cost structure.** Budget it that way.
- **Serving optimizations are post-training optimizations.** An 8× generation speedup from vLLM (§13.2 part 5) is an 8× speedup on the dominant cost.
- **This is why serving expertise appears in post-training job descriptions** (§13.12), and why Chapter 22 is on the post-training learning path.

## 13.8 📄 Read the RLOO paper

**1. The central claim.**

PPO was designed for settings its RLHF application does not resemble — long episodes, per-step rewards, partial observability, high-variance returns needing careful credit assignment. In RLHF the episode is a single response, the reward arrives once at the end, and the policy is initialized from a strong pre-trained model rather than from scratch. Under those conditions, **PPO's machinery (learned value network, GAE, per-token advantages, clipping) is largely unnecessary**, and a much simpler REINFORCE-style estimator with a leave-one-out baseline matches or beats it.

**2. Why per-token credit assignment is unnecessary here.**

The reward genuinely **is a property of the complete response.** There is no intermediate reward to propagate — the reward model reads the whole response and emits one scalar. Assigning different credit to token 12 versus token 340 requires the value function to estimate "how good is this partial response," which is both hard to learn and, arguably, not a well-defined quantity for a half-finished sentence.

RLOO gives every token in a response the same advantage, treating the whole-sequence reward as what it is. The argument is not that credit assignment is impossible but that the machinery for it introduces more variance (via a poorly-fit value network) than it removes.

**3. How they compare.**

RLOO matches or exceeds PPO on standard RLHF benchmarks while being substantially simpler — fewer models in memory, fewer hyperparameters, and more robust to noise in the reward. They also report better robustness to the KL coefficient and to reward-model noise.

The axes on which PPO is not clearly ahead are the ones that matter for adoption: final quality, sample efficiency in the regime tested, and stability.

**4. The strongest counterargument not fully addressed.**

**Scale and regime.** Their experiments are at a scale below frontier post-training, and PPO's variance-reduction machinery plausibly matters more as the policy moves further from initialization and as reward variance grows. The argument "the policy starts from a strong pre-trained model, so it does not move far" is load-bearing — and if you *do* run a long RL phase (as reasoning models do, §15.4), the assumption weakens.

A second, related one: **RLOO's cost is $k$ generations per prompt**, and the paper's efficiency comparison depends on how you price generation. Per §13.7, generation is the dominant cost in a modern post-training setup, so "simpler and equally good" is not the same as "cheaper" — RLOO trades memory and hyperparameters for compute, and the paper does not fully engage with regimes where compute is the binding constraint.

Neither counterargument undermines the result; both are reasons to verify at your own scale rather than assuming transfer.

## 13.9 🧪 Break the PPO lab

**1. $\beta = 0$.**

*Prediction:* the policy drifts and quality degrades slowly.

*What happens:* **collapse, and faster than expected** — typically within a few hundred steps on a toy setup. The policy discovers a specific output the reward model scores highly and emits variants of it for *every* prompt, regardless of the prompt.

Reward goes to a very high value and stays. Entropy drops sharply. Generations become nearly identical across prompts. The tell is that reward looks *better* than any legitimate run ever achieves — if your reward exceeds what a human-written response would score, you have found an exploit, not a capability.

**2. Clip $\epsilon = 10$, 4 epochs.**

*Prediction:* faster learning.

*What happens:* **divergence within one or two batches.** Without the clip, epochs 2–4 apply large updates using importance ratios computed against a policy that has already moved substantially. The ratios grow, the updates grow, and the policy leaves the region where the rollouts were valid.

This is precisely what the clip exists to prevent, and running without it is the cleanest demonstration of why PPO is not just REINFORCE-with-reuse. Watch `clip_frac` in the healthy run first: if it is ~0.2, then removing the clip means 20% of tokens were being actively restrained.

**3. PPO versus RLOO, same task and budget, wall-clock to a given reward.**

*Prediction:* RLOO is faster because it is simpler.

*What happens:* **it depends on $k$, and on a toy task PPO often wins wall-clock.** RLOO with $k=8$ does 8× the generation per prompt. On a small model where generation is fast and the value network trains easily, PPO's single rollout per prompt is cheaper per unit of progress.

RLOO wins when: the value network struggles (large models, sparse rewards), memory is binding, or the reward is verifiable so the value estimate adds little. Which is to say — RLOO wins at realistic scale and loses in the lab. Worth knowing so you do not over-generalize from the lab result.

**4. $k$: 8 → 4.**

*Prediction:* variance doubles.

*What happens:* **gradient variance rises by roughly $\sqrt{2}$, and wall-clock per iteration halves.** The baseline is estimated from 3 samples instead of 7, so it is noisier — and the leave-one-out advantage $A_i = \frac{k}{k-1}(r_i - \bar r)$ picks up a larger correction factor (4/3 versus 8/7).

Net effect on progress per unit wall-clock: usually **slightly favourable to smaller $k$**, down to a point. $k=2$ is where it breaks — the baseline is a single other sample, and the advantage is just $2(r_i - \bar r) = \pm(r_1 - r_2)$, which is extremely noisy.

The useful finding: there is an optimum, it is task-dependent, and $k=8$ is a default rather than a derived value. Measure it.

**5. One corrupted reward, 20σ out, in 500 prompts.**

*Without advantage normalization:* that single sample's advantage is ~20, against a typical ±1. It contributes roughly $20/500 = 4\%$ of the batch's total gradient magnitude on its own — from one prompt out of 500. Its tokens get a large, coherent push. One corrupted reward has measurably steered the update.

*With per-batch advantage normalization* (subtract mean, divide by std): the outlier **inflates the batch standard deviation**, which *shrinks every other sample's advantage*. So normalization does not remove the outlier's influence — it dilutes everyone else's. The outlier still dominates, and now the legitimate signal is weaker too.

*What actually works:* **clip the rewards to a fixed range before computing advantages** (§13.7's "batch-level instability" note). Clipping at, say, ±4σ bounds the damage from any single reward-model failure, and it costs nothing on well-behaved batches.

The general lesson, which recurs in §8.6's gradient clipping: **normalization is not robustness.** A statistic computed over a batch containing an outlier is itself corrupted by the outlier. If you want robustness to outliers, clip them.
