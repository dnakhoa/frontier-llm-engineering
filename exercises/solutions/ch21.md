## 21.1 🔢 Checkpoint size

**1. A 70B dense model.**

| Tensor | Precision | Size |
|---|---|---|
| FP32 master weights | 4 B/param | 280 GB |
| Adam first moment $m$ | 4 B/param | 280 GB |
| Adam second moment $v$ | 4 B/param | 280 GB |
| **Total** | **12 B/param** | **840 GB** |

**2. Why the BF16 copy is excluded.**

It is **derived** — it is the FP32 master weights cast down. On resume you recompute it in microseconds.

Saving it costs $70\times10^9 \times 2 = 140$ GB per checkpoint, a **17% increase** for zero information. At the §21.7 optimum of one checkpoint every ~8 minutes over 45 days, that is 8,100 checkpoints × 140 GB = 1.1 PB of writes for nothing.

**3. DeepSeek-V3's 671B.**

$$671\times10^9 \times 12 = \mathbf{8{,}052 \text{ GB} \approx 8 \text{ TB}}$$

Note this uses **total**, not active, parameters — every expert has optimizer state whether or not it was routed to ([Exercise 6.1](ch06.md) part 5).

**4. With an EMA copy** (FP32, +4 B/param):

- 70B: $840 + 280 = \mathbf{1{,}120 \text{ GB}}$
- 671B: $8{,}052 + 2{,}684 = \mathbf{10{,}736 \text{ GB} \approx 10.7 \text{ TB}}$

A 33% increase. Worth knowing before someone adds EMA to a run whose checkpoint budget was sized without it.

**5. Write time at 100 GB/s aggregate.**

| Checkpoint | Size | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 70B | 840 GB | **8.4 s** |
| 70B + EMA | 1,120 GB | **11.2 s** |
| 671B | 8 TB | **80 s** |
| 671B + EMA | 10.7 TB | **107 s** |

The 70B case is comfortable. **The 671B case is not** — 80 seconds of synchronous stall, and per [Exercise 21.2](ch21.md) that pushes the optimal interval out to nearly half an hour, which means each failure costs ~15 minutes of lost work. This is the arithmetic that makes asynchronous checkpointing mandatory rather than optional at MoE scale.

## 21.2 🔢 The optimal interval

**1. The result.**

$$\tau_{\text{opt}} \approx \sqrt{2\,C\,\text{MTBF}}$$

- $C$ — the cost of writing one checkpoint, in seconds of lost training time. Under asynchronous checkpointing this is the *stall*, not the full write.
- **MTBF** — mean time between failures for the whole job, which is the cluster MTBF, not a component's.
- $\tau_{\text{opt}}$ — the interval that minimizes total overhead (time spent checkpointing plus expected work lost per failure).

The intuition: checkpoint too often and you pay $C$ constantly; too rarely and each failure costs $\tau/2$ in expectation. The optimum balances $C/\tau$ against $\tau/(2\,\text{MTBF})$.

**2. Three cases.**

| Case | $C$ | MTBF | $\tau_{\text{opt}}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) | 60 s | 12 h = 43,200 s | $\sqrt{2 \times 60 \times 43200} = \sqrt{5.18\times10^6} = 2{,}277$ s = **38 min** |
| (b) | 10 s | 3 h = 10,800 s | $\sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 10800} = \sqrt{2.16\times10^5} = 465$ s = **7.8 min** |
| (c) | 2 s | 3 h = 10,800 s | $\sqrt{2 \times 2 \times 10800} = \sqrt{4.32\times10^4} = 208$ s = **3.5 min** |

**3. Fraction of the run lost, case (b).**

Overhead has two parts:

- **Checkpointing:** $C/\tau = 10/465 = 2.15\%$
- **Lost work:** on average half an interval per failure, so $(\tau/2)/\text{MTBF} = 232.5/10800 = 2.15\%$

$$\text{total} \approx \mathbf{4.3\%}$$

The two terms being equal at the optimum is not a coincidence — it is what setting the derivative to zero gives you, and it is a useful sanity check that you computed $\tau_{\text{opt}}$ correctly.

**4. Why halving $C$ helps twice.**

- **Directly:** the checkpointing term $C/\tau$ falls.
- **Indirectly:** $\tau_{\text{opt}} \propto \sqrt{C}$, so halving $C$ also shrinks the optimal interval by $\sqrt{2}$, which halves — well, reduces by $\sqrt 2$ — the expected lost work per failure.

Net: total overhead scales as $\sqrt{C/\text{MTBF}}$, so halving $C$ cuts overhead by $\sqrt{2} \approx 29\%$. Comparing cases (b) and (c): $C$ falls 5×, overhead falls from 4.3% to $2\sqrt{2 \times 2/10800} = 1.92\%$ — a $\sqrt{5}$ improvement.

**This is the argument for asynchronous checkpointing** (§21.5), which is exactly a mechanism for reducing $C$ from the full write time to the device-to-host copy time.

**5. Adding detection and rescheduling to case (b).**

Fixed cost per failure: $5 + 10 = 15$ minutes = 900 s, regardless of $\tau$.

Expected cost per failure: $900 + \tau/2 = 900 + 232 = 1{,}132$ s, of which **80% is detection and rescheduling.**

Total run overhead: $2.15\% + 1132/10800 = 2.15\% + 10.5\% = \mathbf{12.6\%}$.

**What actually matters:** the checkpoint interval is now nearly irrelevant. Driving $\tau$ to zero would save 2.15%; halving detection-plus-rescheduling from 15 to 7.5 minutes saves 5.2%.

**The engineering priority inverts.** Fast failure detection (§7.7's watchdog) and a hot spare pool with fast rescheduling are worth more than checkpoint optimization, and a team that has spent weeks on asynchronous checkpointing while restarts take 15 minutes has optimized the wrong term. This is [Chapter 21 §21.7](../../book/part-4-infra/21-checkpointing-resumption.md)'s note about the formula's omissions, made quantitative.

## 21.3 🏗️ Enumerate the state

| # | Item | Size (70B) | Forgotten? | Symptom if missing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | FP32 master weights | 280 GB | Never | Nothing works |
| 2 | Adam $m$ | 280 GB | Rarely | Loss spike on resume; the optimizer re-accumulates momentum from zero |
| 3 | Adam $v$ | 280 GB | Rarely | **Large** loss spike — $\hat v$ near zero gives enormous effective step sizes, exactly the no-warmup failure ([Exercise 8.2](ch08.md) part 2) |
| 4 | Global step / token count | bytes | Rarely | LR schedule restarts, so the LR jumps back to its earlier value |
| 5 | LR scheduler internal state | bytes | **Sometimes** | Same as 4 if the scheduler is stateful (warm restarts, plateau-based) |
| 6 | **Data-loader position** | KB–MB | **Very often** | [Exercise 21.4](ch21.md). Silent. Loss looks *better*. |
| 7 | RNG state, per rank and per device | KB | **Often** | Dropout masks and shuffling differ; run is unreproducible |
| 8 | Loss scale (FP16) / FP8 scaling factors | KB | **Often** | Burst of skipped steps at every resume; possible instability |
| 9 | Gradient accumulation buffer | 0 or 140 GB | Only if you checkpoint mid-accumulation | Partial gradients lost; one bad step |
| 10 | EMA weights, if used | 280 GB | Sometimes | The EMA restarts from the current weights, discarding its history |
| 11 | Expert-balance bias vector (MoE) | KB | **Often** | Load balance restarts from zero bias; imbalance until the controller re-converges ([Exercise 10.4](ch10.md)) |
| 12 | Best-metric / early-stopping state | bytes | Sometimes | Bookkeeping only |
| 13 | Parallelism topology metadata | bytes | Sometimes | Cannot reshard ([Exercise 21.5](ch21.md) part 5) |

**The pattern:** items 5–8 and 11 total a few megabytes against 840 GB — **0.0005% of the checkpoint** — and they account for essentially all the resumption bugs. They are forgotten *because* they are small enough to overlook and because they live in different subsystems from the model.

**Three to test explicitly, and how.**

- **Data-loader position (item 6).** Assert that a resumed run consumes identical sample IDs to an uninterrupted one over the same step range. This is [Exercise 21.4](ch21.md) part 5 and it is the highest-value test in the chapter.
- **RNG state (item 7).** Run 10 steps, checkpoint, run 10 more; separately resume from the checkpoint and run 10. Assert the loss values match to within floating-point tolerance. A mismatch localizes to RNG if the data IDs already matched.
- **Optimizer state (items 2, 3, 8).** After resume, assert that the first post-resume step's gradient norm and loss are continuous with the pre-checkpoint trajectory — no spike. A spike here is the signature of a missing moment or a reset loss scale.

All three are cheap enough to run in CI on a tiny model, and all three catch failures that are invisible in production.

## 21.4 🔢 The silent data-loader bug

**1. How many times the first 1% of the corpus is seen.**

180 interruptions plus the initial start = **181 passes that begin at token 0**.

Each pass reads at least the first 1% (80B tokens) before the next interruption, provided the mean segment length exceeds 80B tokens. Total consumed is 15T over 181 segments, so the mean segment is $15\times10^{12}/181 = 83$B tokens — just above 80B.

So the first 1% is seen roughly **170–181 times**, call it **~175**.

**2. How many times the last 20% is seen.**

The last 20% starts at token 6.4T of the corpus. A segment reaches it only if that segment consumed more than 6.4T tokens — and the mean segment is 83B tokens, roughly **77× too short**.

With 181 segments averaging 83B tokens, the probability any single segment exceeds 6.4T is essentially zero.

**The last 20% of the corpus is seen zero times.** So is the last 90%, in fact: the maximum segment length would need to be 1.5T tokens for even a single pass to reach the 20% mark, and no segment comes close.

Effectively, **the run trained on roughly the first 1% of the corpus, ~175 times over.**

**3. What the loss curve looks like.**

**Better than a correct run.** Smoothly decreasing, with no anomalies, and reaching a *lower* value than the correct run at the same step count.

Why: the model is being evaluated (implicitly, via training loss) on data it has already seen ~175 times. It has memorized it. Training loss on memorized data is very low, and it declines faster than training loss on fresh data would.

If your validation set is drawn from the same first 1%, validation loss also looks great. Only a validation set drawn from the *unseen* 99% would reveal anything — and if it did, it would look like a mysteriously large train/validation gap that someone would attribute to overfitting and address by adding regularization.

**4. Effect on the final model.**

Catastrophic and not subtle once you look at capabilities rather than loss:

- **It saw ~80B unique tokens, not 15T.** That is roughly a 2023-era small-model data budget on a 2025-era compute budget.
- **It memorized rather than generalized.** 175 epochs is far past the ~4-epoch guidance ([Exercise 3.3](ch03.md)); the model has learned the training set rather than the distribution.
- **Coverage is destroyed.** If the corpus was ordered or grouped at all — by source, by language, by quality tier — the model saw only whatever was first. Any domain not in the first 1% is absent entirely.
- **You spent the full compute budget.** 60 days on 2,048 GPUs, ~$9M at rental rates, for a model equivalent to a much smaller run.

**5. The test.**

```python
def test_dataloader_resumption(make_loader, tmp_path):
    """A resumed loader must consume exactly the samples the uninterrupted
    one would have. Runs in seconds on a toy dataset; catches the most
    expensive silent bug in distributed training.
    """
    # Reference: 100 steps, uninterrupted.
    loader = make_loader(seed=0)
    reference = [loader.next_batch_ids() for _ in range(100)]

    # Interrupted: 50 steps, checkpoint, new loader, restore, 50 more.
    loader = make_loader(seed=0)
    first_half = [loader.next_batch_ids() for _ in range(50)]
    state = loader.state_dict()

    resumed = make_loader(seed=0)
    resumed.load_state_dict(state)
    second_half = [resumed.next_batch_ids() for _ in range(50)]

    assert first_half == reference[:50], "loader is not deterministic"
    assert second_half == reference[50:], "loader did not resume at the right position"
```

Two assertions, and both matter. The first catches non-determinism (a loader that shuffles differently on each construction cannot be resumed at all). The second catches the actual bug.

Run it in CI on a toy dataset, and run it **as a post-restart validation in production** ([Exercise 7.6](ch07.md) part 5) — after every automatic restart, compare the first batch's sample IDs against what the checkpoint says should come next.

## 21.5 🏗️ Design the checkpoint system

200B MoE, 1,024 GPUs, 45 days.

**1. Sharded or consolidated.**

**Sharded**, and it is not close.

Checkpoint size: $200\times10^9 \times 12 = 2{,}400$ GB.

Consolidated means every rank sends its shard to rank 0, which assembles 2.4 TB in host memory and writes it. Two problems: rank 0 likely does not *have* 2.4 TB of host RAM, and at a single node's ~25 GB/s NIC the transfer alone is $2400/25 = 96$ seconds with all 1,024 GPUs idle.

Sharded: each rank writes its own $2400/1024 = 2.3$ GB in parallel. At an aggregate 200 GB/s the write is 12 seconds, and no single node is a bottleneck.

**2. Synchronous or asynchronous.**

**Asynchronous.** From [Exercise 21.2](ch21.md), $C$ enters the overhead as $\sqrt{C}$, and reducing it from a 12-second write to a sub-second device-to-host copy cuts total overhead by ~3.5×.

**What async requires:**

- **Host memory for staging**: 2.4 TB across 128 nodes = 19 GB per node. Fine.
- **A guarantee the staged buffer is not mutated during the write.** Either copy into a dedicated buffer, or block the next optimizer step until the D2H copy completes (not until the disk write completes).
- **Handling a failure mid-write** — part 4.
- **Backpressure**: if a write is still in flight when the next checkpoint is due, skip it rather than queueing. Two concurrent 2.4 TB writes will saturate the filesystem and stall training anyway.

**3. Cadence and retention.**

Cluster MTBF at 1,024 GPUs: from [Exercise 7.3](ch07.md)'s method, roughly $40{,}000/1024 \approx 39$ h for GPUs alone, halved for non-GPU components ≈ **20 hours**. With async $C \approx 1$ s:

$$\tau_{\text{opt}} = \sqrt{2 \times 1 \times 72{,}000} = 379 \text{ s} \approx \mathbf{6 \text{ minutes}}$$

**Retention policy** — two tiers, because restart checkpoints and archival checkpoints have different purposes:

| Tier | Cadence | Keep | Storage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Restart | Every 6 min | Last 3 | $3 \times 2.4 = 7.2$ TB |
| Archival | Every 2,000 steps | All | see below |

Archival: at ~8 s/step, 2,000 steps is ~4.5 hours, so 45 days gives ~240 archival checkpoints × 2.4 TB = **576 TB**.

That is a lot. Options: keep every archival checkpoint for the last week and thin older ones to daily (reduces to ~100 TB), or store archival checkpoints as weights-only (4 B/param FP32 = 800 GB, a third the size) since you rarely need to *resume* from an old checkpoint, only to evaluate or branch from it.

**Recommendation: 7.2 TB rolling + weights-only archival every 2,000 steps ≈ 190 TB.** Budget for it before the run starts, because a filesystem filling up at 3am is [Chapter 21 §21.11](../../book/part-4-infra/21-checkpointing-resumption.md)'s row 6.

**4. Atomicity.**

Write every shard to `checkpoint-{step}.tmp/`, and when **all ranks report success**, rank 0 atomically renames the directory to `checkpoint-{step}/` and writes a `_COMPLETE` marker inside it.

The loader's rule: **a directory without `_COMPLETE` is garbage.** It is skipped, and optionally deleted.

Why the marker rather than relying on the rename alone: on distributed filesystems, directory rename atomicity is not always guaranteed across all clients, and a rank may have written a truncated file that "exists." The marker is written last, by one process, after a barrier confirming every shard closed successfully.

A partial write therefore looks like: a `.tmp` directory containing some complete shards and possibly one truncated one, no `_COMPLETE`, ignored by the loader, cleaned up on the next successful checkpoint.

**5. Resharding 1,024 → 512.**

The format must record **logical tensor structure, not physical shard contents**:

- For each tensor: its **global shape**, its **dtype**, and **which axis** it is sharded along.
- For each shard: the **offset and extent** within the global tensor.
- The **parallelism topology** the checkpoint was written under (TP, PP, EP, DP degrees) so the loader can reason about how to redistribute.
- For MoE: **which expert indices** live in each shard, since experts are sharded by identity rather than by tensor slicing.

Given that, `torch.distributed.checkpoint` [\[87\]](../../book/appendix/b-references.md#87-pytorch-distributed-checkpoint) can compute any target sharding from any source sharding. Given only "rank 7's bytes," it cannot, and you are stuck at 1,024 forever — including when a node dies and you have 1,016 working GPUs.

**6. Post-restart validation.**

Three checks, all of which run in under a minute:

- **Loss continuity.** Run one forward pass on a fixed held-out batch and compare the loss against the value recorded at checkpoint time. A mismatch means the model or optimizer state did not restore correctly.
- **Data-loader position.** Assert the first batch's sample IDs match what the checkpoint says should come next ([Exercise 21.4](ch21.md) part 5).
- **World-size and topology consistency.** Assert the loaded topology matches the current one, or that a deliberate reshard was requested.

**Why:** automation that restarts on crash will happily restart into a state that is running and wrong. Per [Exercise 7.6](ch07.md) part 5, the failure mode is 40 confident automatic restarts overnight into a silently broken run. These three checks turn "the process started" into "the run resumed correctly," which are very different claims.

## 21.6 🔢 Bit-exact or not

**1. Four reasons a resume may not be bit-identical.**

- **Non-associative floating-point reduction.** An all-reduce over 1,024 ranks may complete in a different order on different runs, giving different last bits ([Exercise 20.5](ch20.md) part 3).
- **Nondeterministic kernels.** Anything using atomics — some scatter operations, some backward passes, some custom kernels ([Exercise 20.5](ch20.md) part 5).
- **cuDNN/cuBLAS autotuning.** The library benchmarks several algorithms on first use and picks the fastest; a different machine or a different thermal state can select a different algorithm with different numerics.
- **Resharding.** Changing the parallelism degree changes the reduction tree, so bit-exactness across a reshard is essentially impossible by construction.

A fifth: **different library or driver versions** between the original run and the resume.

**2. What `torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)` costs.**

- **Performance.** Deterministic implementations of scatter-add, index-put, and some convolutions are meaningfully slower — commonly 10–30% on affected operations, occasionally worse.
- **It raises an exception** for operations with no deterministic implementation, rather than silently falling back. This is the intended behaviour and it means enabling it may simply refuse to run your model until you replace those operations.
- It also requires setting `CUBLAS_WORKSPACE_CONFIG` for deterministic cuBLAS behaviour, which costs a little extra memory.

**3. Exact state resumption versus bit-exact reproduction.**

**Exact state resumption:** the resumed run consumes the same data in the same order, with the same RNG stream, the same scheduler position, and the same optimizer state. The run is **statistically identical** — it is the same trajectory, up to floating-point noise in the last bits.

**Bit-exact reproduction:** every tensor is bitwise identical to the uninterrupted run.

**A production run needs the first, not the second.**

Exact state resumption is what prevents [Exercise 21.4](ch21.md)'s data bug and makes the run's data budget mean what you think it means. Bit-exactness buys only debuggability, at a real throughput cost, on a run where throughput is the thing you are paying for.

Conflating them is common and expensive in both directions: teams that demand bit-exactness pay 20% throughput for it, and teams that dismiss reproducibility entirely skip the data-loader state too.

**4. Bisecting a spike at step 47,000.**

**You need bit-exactness, and only for the bisection.**

The approach: **do not run the whole job deterministically.** Instead:

- Resume from the archival checkpoint before the spike — say step 46,000.
- Enable `torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)` and fix all seeds for this debugging run only.
- Replay forward to 47,000. Confirm the spike reproduces.
- Now bisect: skip the suspect data batches, or disable a suspect component, and replay again. Because the run is now deterministic, a change in outcome is attributable to your intervention rather than to noise.

If the spike does **not** reproduce deterministically, that is itself the finding — it points at a hardware or nondeterminism-related cause (silent data corruption, a flaky rank) rather than at the recipe or data.

The general principle: **pay for determinism in the debugging loop, not in the production loop.** Archival checkpoints ([Exercise 21.5](ch21.md) part 3) are what make this possible, which is a good argument for keeping them even though restart checkpoints are what the Young/Daly analysis is about.

## 21.7 🏗️ The conversion pipeline

**1. Training versus serving checkpoint.**

| | Training | Serving |
|---|---|---|
| Sharding | Whatever the trainer used (TP × PP × EP × DP) | The serving TP degree, usually smaller |
| Precision | FP32 master + optimizer state | BF16 or FP8, weights only |
| Optimizer state | Present (2/3 of the size) | Absent |
| Layout | Separate Q, K, V; separate gate and up projections | Often fused QKV and fused gate-up for fewer kernel launches |
| Format | Framework-specific sharded format | `safetensors`, memory-mappable |
| Extras | Scheduler, RNG, data loader | Tokenizer, chat template, generation config |

**2. Resharding along the wrong axis.**

Take a row-parallel output projection $W_O$ of shape $(d, d) = (8192, 8192)$, sharded across 8 training ranks **along the input dimension** (rows) — so rank $i$ holds rows $1024i$ to $1024(i+1)$.

Reassemble correctly and re-split across 4 ranks along rows: each serving rank gets 2,048 rows. Correct.

Re-split along **columns** instead: each serving rank gets a $(8192, 2048)$ slice. The tensor has the right total size, every shape assertion passes, and the matmul runs — but rank 0 now holds the first 2,048 *output* features of every input, when the tensor-parallel all-reduce expects it to hold a partial sum over the first 2,048 *inputs*.

The result: the all-reduce sums four partial products that were never meant to be summed. No error, plausible-looking activations, and the model generates fluent nonsense.

**Why this is so easy to get wrong:** for a square weight, transposing the split is dimensionally undetectable. The only defence is the golden test in part 5.

**3. `safetensors` versus `torch.save`, precisely.**

`torch.save` uses Python's **pickle** format, and unpickling **executes arbitrary code** — a pickle stream can contain a `__reduce__` that runs any callable with any arguments. Loading an untrusted `.pt` file is equivalent to running an untrusted script.

`safetensors` is a **flat header plus raw tensor bytes**: a JSON header giving names, dtypes, shapes, and byte offsets, followed by the data. Parsing it cannot execute anything.

Two secondary benefits that also matter: it is **memory-mappable**, so loading a 140 GB model does not require reading it all into RAM first, and it is **framework-agnostic**.

For anything you distribute publicly, `safetensors` is not a preference; it is the responsible default.

**4. What must ship alongside the weights.**

- **The tokenizer**, including the merges/vocabulary and every special token.
- **The chat template** ([Chapter 11 §11.6](../../book/part-3-post-training/11-sft-frontier-style.md)). A model artifact without it is incomplete, and a mismatched one degrades every benchmark with no visible cause.
- **The model config**: architecture, layer count, hidden size, attention variant and its parameters, RoPE base and any scaling.
- **The generation config**: default temperature, top-p, EOS token ids, max length.
- **Quantization metadata**, if quantized: the scheme, the group size, the scale locations.

**5. The golden test.**

```python
def test_conversion(training_ckpt, serving_ckpt, golden_path):
    """Assert the converted model produces the same logits as the trainer.

    Run on every conversion, before anything is published. Catches axis
    errors, fusion errors, dtype errors, and template drift.
    """
    prompts = load_golden_prompts(golden_path)      # ~50 fixed prompts

    # Reference logits, produced ONCE from the training checkpoint at the
    # time of conversion, using the training-time forward path.
    expected = load_golden_logits(golden_path)

    actual = serving_model(serving_ckpt).forward_logits(prompts)

    # BF16 through a different kernel path: expect last-bit differences,
    # not structural ones.
    torch.testing.assert_close(actual, expected, rtol=2e-2, atol=2e-2)

    # And a behavioural check the numerical one can miss.
    for p in prompts:
        out = serving_model(serving_ckpt).generate(p, max_new_tokens=64)
        assert out.endswith_eos or len(out) == 64, "generation does not terminate"
        assert "<|assistant|>" not in out, "spurious role marker"
```

The properties that make it catch part 2's bug: it compares **logits**, not just whether the model runs, and it compares them against a reference captured from the *training* forward path. An axis error produces logits that are structurally different — not off by a last bit, but off by a lot — so a 2% tolerance catches it immediately while still tolerating legitimate kernel differences.

The behavioural checks catch the other common conversion failures: a wrong EOS id (generation never terminates) and a template mismatch (spurious role markers).

## 21.8 🧪 Break the checkpoint lab

**1. The sample-ID assertion on a naive loader.**

*Prediction people make:* it passes, since the loader is deterministic.

*What happens:* **it fails.** A naive loader constructed fresh at resume time restarts its iterator from the beginning, so `second_half` equals `reference[:50]` rather than `reference[50:]`.

The instructive part is that **no other assertion in a normal test suite would catch this.** The model loads, the optimizer loads, the loss is finite, training proceeds. Only an explicit comparison of consumed sample IDs reveals it.

**2. Omitting RNG state.**

*Prediction:* the sample-ID assertion fails.

*What happens:* **the sample-ID assertion passes** (if the loader's shuffle uses its own seeded generator, which a well-built one does), and the **loss-continuity assertion fails** — the resumed run's loss differs slightly from the reference because dropout masks differ.

The difference is small and does not diverge, which is why this is a reproducibility bug rather than a correctness bug ([Exercise 21.6](ch21.md) part 3). If your loader shares the global RNG with dropout, both assertions fail, which is a good argument for giving the loader its own generator.

**3. Omitting data-loader state.**

*Prediction:* the loss spikes.

*What happens:* **the loss improves**, and **no assertion fails** unless you wrote the sample-ID test.

This is [Exercise 21.4](ch21.md) part 3 reproduced at lab scale, and seeing it directly is worth more than reading about it. The loss curve after the resume is visibly *better* than the reference — which, in a real run, is the moment someone says "the resume worked fine, look at the loss."

**4. Resharding 4 shards → 2.**

*Prediction:* it works if the total size matches.

*What happens:* with a format storing **physical** layout — "rank 2's bytes" — the loader has no way to know what rank 2 held, so it either refuses (best case), or loads shards by index and silently assembles the tensor wrong (worst case, and it will run).

With **logical** structure recorded (global shape, shard axis, offsets), the loader computes the redistribution correctly and the assertion passes.

The lab makes this concrete by letting you toggle the format. The failure mode with physical layout is the [Exercise 21.7](ch21.md) part 2 bug: it runs, and the outputs are wrong.

**5. Killing the process mid-write.**

*Without atomic rename:* the next start finds `checkpoint-{step}/` containing some complete shards and one truncated file. Loading either throws a deserialization error (lucky) or reads a truncated tensor and produces garbage (unlucky, and format-dependent). Worse, an automatic restart loop will retry this same broken checkpoint repeatedly.

*With atomic rename plus `_COMPLETE`:* the partial write is in `checkpoint-{step}.tmp/` with no marker. The loader skips it entirely and falls back to the previous complete checkpoint. You lose one interval of work, which is exactly what the Young/Daly analysis assumed.

The lab lets you kill mid-write and observe both. The takeaway worth carrying: **checkpoint integrity is a durability problem with a standard solution** (write-temp-then-rename, plus an explicit completion marker), and every system that does not implement it eventually loses a run to it.
