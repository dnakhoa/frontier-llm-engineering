## 25.1 🏗️ Revise your map

**1. Should the target change?**

For most readers, **not the five-year target — the entry point.**

§25.1's table has a consistent shape: the roles that attract the most applications (Pre-training Researcher, Alignment Researcher) have the fewest seats, and the roles labs cannot fill (Data Engineer, ML Systems, Kernel, Inference, Evaluation, Environment) have open headcount that stays open.

If your target is in the crowded column, the useful revision is not to abandon it but to **stop treating a direct application as the plan.**

**2. The closest scarce role.**

Map your actual current skills — [Exercise 24.6](ch24.md) part 1's "what would I be hired to do tomorrow" — against the scarce roles:

| If you currently do | The closest scarce role |
|---|---|
| Backend / distributed systems | Training Infrastructure, or Inference |
| HPC / scientific computing | ML Systems, Kernel Engineer |
| GPU / graphics / CUDA | Kernel Engineer |
| Data engineering / ETL at scale | Data Engineer (pre-training) |
| QA / test infrastructure | Evaluation Engineer, Environment Engineer |
| DevOps / SRE / containers | Environment Engineer, Cluster Infrastructure |
| Applied ML / fine-tuning | Data Engineer (post-training), Evaluation |

The domain knowledge on top of any of these is the contents of this book — months, not years. The underlying engineering skill is what took years, and it is what the labs cannot hire.

**3. Enter-then-transition versus direct.**

Almost always **faster**, per §25.4: internal transitions are dramatically easier because colleagues have direct evidence of your judgement rather than a résumé and an interview loop.

The arithmetic is blunt. A direct application to a crowded research role competes against people with directly relevant published work, and the acceptance rate reflects that. An application to a role with standing open headcount competes against a much smaller pool. Once inside, moving is a conversation with a manager who has watched you work.

**4. What you give up, and is it recoverable.**

*You give up:* time spent on the target role's specific skills; possibly a title that reads as "engineer" rather than "researcher"; and the risk of becoming load-bearing in the entry role and being hard to release.

*Recoverable?* Mostly yes, with two caveats worth naming:

- **The "hard to release" risk is real.** The mitigation is to state the intent at hiring time and to keep doing visible work in the target area — an internal ablation, a paper reading group, a side project the target team uses.
- **Publications, if your target is research, are harder to accumulate from an engineering role.** But not impossible: infrastructure and evaluation work produces publishable results, and being a co-author on the team's main paper is a normal outcome of doing the work.

The thing you do *not* give up is the learning. §25.8's compounding items — the arithmetic, the failure modes, the debugging methodology — accrue in any of these roles.

## 25.2 🏗️ The PhD question, for you specifically

**1. Which list.**

**Genuinely helps:** Pre-training Researcher, Alignment Researcher, and anything whose job description is "propose what to try next" rather than "make this work."

**Does not matter:** training infrastructure, ML systems, kernels, inference, data engineering, evaluation, environments — which is most of the scarce column and most of this book.

**2. The underlying skill.**

**Designing experiments whose results are ambiguous, on budgets where being wrong is expensive.**

The credential is not the mechanism. A PhD is four to six years of supervised practice at: forming a hypothesis, designing a discriminating test, running it, being wrong, and correctly updating. Labs want that skill because a frontier ablation costs real money and an experiment that cannot distinguish two hypotheses has wasted it.

Naming the skill rather than the credential is what makes part 3 possible.

**3. Demonstrating it another way.**

Concretely, and in rough order of strength:

- **A published reproduction with an ablation.** Not "I reproduced X" but "I reproduced X, then tested whether component Y was load-bearing, and here is the result." [Exercise 19.7](ch19.md) is exactly this shape and it is affordable on rented GPUs.
- **A write-up of a negative result.** "I expected A, ran the experiment, got B, and here is why my model was wrong." This is the single most credible artifact because it cannot be produced without having actually done the work, and because most people will not publish one.
- **A merged contribution to an open project that required a design decision**, with the design discussion visible in the PR thread.
- **An evaluation or benchmark others adopt.** Designing a measurement that survives contact with other people's models is experimental design.

What does not work: a project with no hypothesis, or a reproduction with no ablation.

**4. Opportunity cost.**

The honest accounting: **four to six years**, during which the alternative is four to six years of doing the work at a lab, with a salary, on real infrastructure, alongside people who have done it.

The comparison is not "PhD versus nothing." For a strong engineer it is "PhD versus being three years into a frontier lab," and the second is a substantial position.

The case flips when: you cannot get into a lab now and cannot see a path (a PhD is a well-defined path with an admissions process); you want to work on problems only academia poses; or your target is specifically a research role at a lab that gates on it.

**5. What would not be superseded.**

Not the techniques. Between 2023 and 2026, DPO went from a revelation to one option among six, GRPO went from a paper to standard practice, and verifiable rewards went from niche to organizing principle. A thesis on any of them would be historical by graduation.

What survives:

- **The experimental design skill** (part 2) — the actual thing.
- **The collaborators.** Your cohort and advisor become a network that lasts decades, and in a field this small that matters.
- **The habit of reading primary sources properly** and of noticing what a paper does not say ([Exercise 10.6](ch10.md), [Exercise 19.6](ch19.md)).
- **Comfort with being wrong in public**, which is the hardest and most durable of the four.

§25.8's framing applies to the PhD decision as much as to anything else: **learn the structure, not the recipe** — and choose the path that teaches structure.

## 25.3 🏗️ Design the transition

No solution. The method:

**On part 2 — classifying your transition.**

| Your move | §25.4's classification |
|---|---|
| Systems → kernels | Easy — same mental model, narrower focus |
| Kernels → inference | Easy — decode kernels are the bridge |
| Pre-training eng → post-training eng | Easy — the RL loop is a training loop plus a generation cluster |
| Inference → post-training infrastructure | Easy, and undersupplied — §13.9 means post-training *is* a serving problem |
| Data engineering → post-training research | Easy — the mixture is the research |
| Engineering → research | **Harder** — the bar is demonstrated experimental design, not permission |
| Anything → pre-training research | **The narrow door** |

**On part 3 — the intermediate step.** For a hard transition, the usual route is **doing the target work inside the current role until the title catches up.** An infrastructure engineer who runs the team's ablations, writes them up, and is right about them becomes a research engineer without changing employers.

**On part 4 — the plan.** A quarterly deliverable is the mechanism that makes a 12-month plan survive month four. A workable shape:

- **Q1:** close the largest learnable gap; deliverable = a written artifact (the [Exercise 24.4](ch24.md) project).
- **Q2:** contribute to an open project in the target area; deliverable = a merged PR.
- **Q3:** a reproduction with an ablation; deliverable = a published write-up including what failed.
- **Q4:** apply, or make the internal case; deliverable = interviews, or a conversation with a manager holding three artifacts.

Each deliverable is public and dated, which is what stops the plan from quietly becoming a reading list.

**On part 5 — this week.** The correct answer is small and specific. Not "start learning distributed training" but "read Chapter 6, do Exercises 6.1 and 6.2, and write the answers down." A plan whose first step takes a month has no first step.

## 25.4 🏗️ Where to work

The comparison framework, applied to the two most common pairings.

**Large frontier lab versus small lab / startup.**

| | Large lab | Small lab |
|---|---|---|
| Compute | Unmatched | Constrained |
| Your slice | Narrow — you will not see the whole pipeline | Wide — you may own something end to end |
| Colleagues | People who have done this before | People figuring it out with you |
| Learning rate on *your* component | High | High |
| Learning rate on the *whole system* | Low | High |
| What you cannot learn elsewhere | What a 10,000-GPU run actually looks like | How the pieces fit together |

The five-year question in part 5 usually resolves this. If your target is a senior role owning a cross-cutting concern, breadth compounds and the small lab is better preparation. If your target is deep specialization — kernels, or pre-training research — the large lab's scale is the thing you cannot get elsewhere.

**Cloud/hardware vendor versus lab.**

§25.6 calls vendors an underrated entry point, and the reasoning is worth spelling out: NVIDIA, AMD, AWS, and Google Cloud do systems and kernel work at enormous scale, on the infrastructure the labs run on, and **they hire far more people than the labs do.** The work is genuinely relevant — FlashAttention-3's Hopper optimizations, Trainium's software stack, vLLM's kernel layer are all this kind of work.

What you would not learn there: the model side. You will know the machine deeply and the recipe barely, which is a fine position from which to move into a lab's systems team and a poor one from which to move into research.

**Open source.**

Maximum visibility, zero compute. §19.9 notes the R1 reproductions came out of this world within weeks and several of the people who did them were hired shortly after. As a *path* it works; as a *destination* it depends on funding.

## 25.5 📄 Read across the divide

**1. The four reports this book leans on most.**

| Report | Lab | Country |
|---|---|---|
| DeepSeek-V3 [\[1\]](../../book/appendix/b-references.md#1-deepseek-v3) | DeepSeek | China |
| DeepSeek-R1 [\[10\]](../../book/appendix/b-references.md#10-deepseek-r1) | DeepSeek | China |
| Qwen3 [\[6\]](../../book/appendix/b-references.md#6-qwen3) | Alibaba | China |
| Llama 3 [\[5\]](../../book/appendix/b-references.md#5-llama-3) | Meta | US |

Three of the four. Kimi k1.5 [\[69\]](../../book/appendix/b-references.md#69-kimi-k15), used as the independent check on R1's recipe, is Moonshot AI — also China.

Chapters 1, 5, 6, 8, 10, 15, and 19 are anchored on these, and Chapter 10 and Chapter 19 are entirely case studies of them. This is not an editorial choice about geography; it is a consequence of §1.2's criterion — **the book anchors on whoever published the most detail**, and over 2024–2025 that was these teams.

**2 & 3. What their reports tell you that contemporaneous Western ones did not.**

Concretely, from the V3 and R1 reports:

- **A working FP8 training recipe at production scale**, including the fine-grained scaling strategy and the accumulation-precision workaround for a specific hardware limitation ([Exercise 8.8](ch08.md)). No Western report of that period gave this level of numerical detail.
- **The auxiliary-loss-free load-balancing scheme** ([Exercise 10.4](ch10.md)), described precisely enough to implement.
- **A complete post-training pipeline with an ablation** — R1-Zero is the recipe minus the SFT stage, at frontier scale, which essentially nobody else publishes.
- **A section on what did not work** (§19.7). PRMs and MCTS, with reasons. Negative results at this scale are rare and disproportionately informative.
- **DualPipe and the communication-overlap design**, shaped by a specific interconnect constraint.

The general observation: these reports are unusually strong on **systems and numerics**, and the Llama 3 paper is unusually strong on **reliability statistics and evaluation methodology** ([Exercise 7.8](ch07.md), [Exercise 3.6](ch03.md)). Reading only one tradition gives you half the picture.

**4. What you are already using.**

Worth actually checking rather than assuming. If you have fine-tuned an open model recently, or used one as a judge, or used an open embedding model, there is a substantial chance it is a Qwen or DeepSeek derivative — they dominate the open fine-tuning ecosystem, and many "open" models on leaderboards are one of the two with a different name.

The point §25.7 makes is practical rather than political: **an engineer who filters out this literature is working with less information than their peers, while already depending on its artifacts.**

## 25.6 🏗️ What compounds

**1 & 3.** Yours to answer. The sorting rule from §25.8:

| Compounds | Does not |
|---|---|
| The arithmetic — bytes, FLOPs, bandwidth, memory hierarchies | Specific GPU peak numbers |
| Failure modes — reward hacking, overoptimization, silent data bugs, distribution shift | Specific mitigations for one framework's version |
| Debugging methodology — hypotheses ordered by cost, data before hyperparameters | Specific tool invocations |
| Structural understanding of algorithms | Algorithm recipes |
| Reading primary sources, including for absence | Knowledge of any particular paper |
| Experimental design | The current SOTA number |

**Half-lives**, roughly: framework APIs 1–3 years; specific algorithm implementations 2–4 years; hardware-specific kernel optimizations 2–3 years (one GPU generation, per [Exercise 20.7](ch20.md) part 4); the arithmetic, indefinite.

**2. Another example of structure-over-recipe transfer.**

Several in this book, and the strongest three:

- **Length exploitation.** Someone who understood *why* RLHF's length bias occurs — the reward model learned a correlation and RL exploits whatever is easiest to increase ([Chapter 12 §12.6](../../book/part-3-post-training/12-reward-modeling.md)) — recognized reasoning-RL's length growth immediately, and correctly identified it as a *different* mechanism with the same symptom ([Exercise 15.6](ch15.md)). Someone who learned "RLHF makes responses longer" as a fact learned nothing transferable.
- **Safe as a ranker, dangerous as an objective** ([Exercise 16.5](ch16.md)). The same structure explains reward models, PRMs, generative judges, and benchmarks. Four techniques, one principle.
- **Activation recomputation.** The memory-for-compute trade appears at the layer level ([Chapter 6 §6.12](../../book/part-2-pretraining/06-distributed-training.md)) and inside a single kernel (FlashAttention's backward, [Exercise 20.3](ch20.md) part 5). Recognizing it as the same trade at two scales is what makes the second one obvious.

A fourth, and the most general: **"more compute on a wrong objective makes the wrong thing better, never right"** ([Exercise 11.9](ch11.md) part 2). It explains masking bugs, reward hacking, format collapse, and adversarial-training evasion ([Exercise 17.7](ch17.md)) — four chapters, one fact.

**4. What you are spending time on that will not compound.**

The common candidates, and what to substitute:

| Not compounding | Substitute |
|---|---|
| Learning a framework's API surface | Learning what the framework is *doing* — the arithmetic underneath |
| Tracking every new paper | Reading a dozen deeply, and re-reading them |
| Chasing benchmark numbers | Building the evaluation that tells you whether a number means anything |
| Reimplementing a published algorithm | Implementing it *and* ablating one component |
| Reading about distributed training | Doing [Exercise 6.7](ch06.md) until it is boring |

**5. One sentence of career advice.**

A candidate, drawing on §24.7, §25.1, §25.3, and §25.8:

> **Learn the arithmetic until it is boring, enter through a role the labs cannot fill, ship something public that includes what did not work, and optimize for understanding structure rather than accumulating recipes — because the recipes will all be replaced and the structure will not.**

Write your own. The exercise is worth more than the model answer, and the sentence you write is a reasonable thing to reread in a year.
