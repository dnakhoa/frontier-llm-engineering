## 15.1 🔢 Zero-variance groups

**1. Probability all 8 samples agree**, $p^8 + (1-p)^8$:

| Bucket | $p$ | $p^8$ | $(1-p)^8$ | All-same |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Easy | 0.95 | 0.6634 | 0.0000 | **0.663** |
| Medium | 0.50 | 0.0039 | 0.0039 | **0.008** |
| Hard | 0.10 | 0.0000 | 0.4305 | **0.431** |
| Impossible | 0.00 | 0 | 1.0000 | **1.000** |

**2. Fraction contributing zero gradient.**

$$0.30(0.663) + 0.20(0.008) + 0.30(0.431) + 0.20(1.000)$$
$$= 0.199 + 0.002 + 0.129 + 0.200 = \mathbf{0.530}$$

**53% of your prompts produce no gradient at all.**

**3. Effective batch size.** **47% of nominal.** You are paying for 1,024 prompts of generation and learning from 481.

**4. $G = 32$.**

| Bucket | $p^{32} + (1-p)^{32}$ |
|---|---|
| Easy | $0.1937 + 0 = 0.194$ |
| Medium | $\approx 4.7\times10^{-10}$ |
| Hard | $0 + 0.0343 = 0.034$ |
| Impossible | $1.000$ |

$$0.30(0.194) + 0.20(0) + 0.30(0.034) + 0.20(1.000) = 0.058 + 0 + 0.010 + 0.200 = \mathbf{0.268}$$

Effective batch rises from 47% to **73%** — a 1.55× improvement.

**The cost: 4× the generation compute.** Since generation dominates wall-clock ([Exercise 13.7](ch13.md)), you spent 4× to gain 1.55×. **Increasing $G$ is a poor way to buy effective batch size**, and the arithmetic says so clearly.

Note also that $G = 32$ does nothing for the "impossible" bucket — 20% of prompts are dead weight at any $G$.

**5. A filtering rule that doubles effective batch size.**

**Drop prompts whose measured pass rate is below 0.05 or above 0.90.**

That removes the Easy (30%) and Impossible (20%) buckets, leaving Medium and Hard. Recomputing at $G = 8$ over the surviving 50% of prompts:

$$\frac{0.20(0.008) + 0.30(0.431)}{0.50} = \frac{0.002 + 0.129}{0.50} = 0.262 \text{ zero-variance}$$

**Effective batch: 73.8% of the (now smaller) prompt set** — but per unit of generation compute, you now get $0.738$ useful prompts per prompt generated versus $0.47$ before. **A 1.57× improvement at zero extra compute**, matching what $G=32$ bought for 4× the cost.

**What it costs:**

- **You need the pass rates**, which means an extra generation pass over the prompt set to measure them. Cheap at low $G$ (say $G=4$ purely for measurement) and it must be **redone periodically**, because the policy improves and today's Hard prompt is tomorrow's Easy one.
- **You permanently discard the Impossible bucket**, which may contain exactly the problems you most want the model to eventually solve. The mitigation is to re-test them each round rather than deleting them — a prompt at 0% pass rate now may cross 5% after the model improves.
- **Curriculum risk:** training only on medium-difficulty problems can make the model good at medium-difficulty problems specifically. Keep a small share of easy and hard prompts for coverage.

This is why §15.9 says prompt-set calibration is one of the highest-leverage things you can do, and why the reproductions that filtered by pass rate outperformed those that did not (§19.9).

## 15.2 🔢 Group size economics

**1. Generation time.**

Aggregate throughput: $1{,}800 \times 64 = 115{,}200$ tok/s.

| $G$ | Tokens per iteration | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | $1{,}024 \times 8 \times 3{,}000 = 24.6\text{M}$ | **213 s** |
| 16 | 49.2M | **427 s** |
| 32 | 98.3M | **853 s** |
| 64 | 196.6M | **1,707 s** |

**2. Effective prompts** (from [Exercise 15.1](ch15.md)'s distribution, computing $1 - \sum_b s_b(p_b^G + (1-p_b)^G)$):

| $G$ | Zero-variance fraction | Effective prompts |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | 0.530 | **481** |
| 16 | 0.361 | **654** |
| 32 | 0.268 | **749** |
| 64 | 0.222 | **796** |

**3. Useful gradient per second of generation.**

| $G$ | Effective prompts | Time (s) | Prompts/s |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 481 | 213 | **2.26** |
| 16 | 654 | 427 | **1.53** |
| 32 | 749 | 853 | **0.88** |
| 64 | 796 | 1,707 | **0.47** |

**Monotonically decreasing.** By this metric, smaller $G$ is strictly better, and $G=8$ beats $G=64$ by nearly 5×.

**4. Which $G$, and what changes the answer.**

The table says "as small as possible," but $G$ has a floor for reasons the table does not capture:

- **Baseline quality.** At $G = 2$ the group mean is the average of two samples and the advantage is $\pm(r_1 - r_2)/2$ — extremely noisy. The gradient *variance* rises as $G$ falls, and this table counts only whether a group is non-degenerate, not how good its baseline estimate is.
- **Advantage granularity with binary rewards.** At $G=8$ a group can have 0–8 successes, giving 9 distinct advantage patterns. At $G=2$, three. Coarse advantages mean coarse credit assignment.

**Choose $G = 8$ to $16$**, which is where the field has landed empirically, and note that the table's message — *do not reach for large $G$ to fix a prompt-quality problem* — is the actionable part.

**What changes the answer:**

- **A better-calibrated prompt set** ([Exercise 15.1](ch15.md) part 5) makes small $G$ even better, because the zero-variance penalty that large $G$ was mitigating largely disappears.
- **Continuous rather than binary rewards** (partial credit, test-pass fraction) make groups far less likely to be degenerate at any $G$, again favouring small $G$.
- **Very hard prompt sets** where pass rates are genuinely low and cannot be raised — here large $G$ is the only way to find any successful sample, and you accept the cost.
- **Prefix sharing.** With paged attention's copy-on-write ([Exercise 13.7](ch13.md) part 2), the $G$ samples share the prompt's KV cache, so larger $G$ is cheaper in *memory* than the naive count suggests — though not in generated tokens, which is what this table measures.

## 15.3 🔢 The Dr. GRPO biases

**1. Per-token gradient weight under $1/|o_i|$.**

The objective term is $\frac{1}{|o_i|}\sum_{t} \rho_{i,t} A_i$, so each token carries weight $A_i / |o_i|$.

- 500-token response: $A_i / 500 = \mathbf{0.002 A_i}$ per token
- 5,000-token response: $A_i / 5000 = \mathbf{0.0002 A_i}$ per token

The long response's tokens each receive **one tenth** the gradient of the short response's tokens.

**2. Which wrong answer is penalized more per token, and what it selects for.**

For a wrong answer, $A_i < 0$. The 500-token wrong answer receives $0.002 A_i$ per token; the 5,000-token wrong answer receives $0.0002 A_i$ per token.

**The short wrong answer is penalized 10× harder per token.**

**What that selects for: being wrong at length.** If you are going to fail, failing verbosely costs less per token than failing concisely. Over many steps, the policy learns that padding a failing response reduces its penalty — which is a direct, mechanical incentive toward verbosity that has nothing to do with reasoning quality.

This is Liu et al.'s [\[70\]](../../book/appendix/b-references.md#70-dr-grpo) first identified bias, and it is a genuine contributor to the length growth in §15.7, distinct from the "longer reasoning is genuinely better" mechanism in §15.6.

**3. Group standard deviations.**

**Prompt A** `[1,1,1,1,1,1,1,0]`: mean $= 7/8 = 0.875$. Deviations: seven of $+0.125$, one of $-0.875$.

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{7(0.125)^2 + (0.875)^2}{8} = \frac{0.109 + 0.766}{8} = 0.1094 \;\Rightarrow\; \sigma = \mathbf{0.331}$$

**Prompt B** `[1,0,1,0,1,0,1,0]`: mean $= 0.5$. All deviations $\pm 0.5$.

$$\sigma = \mathbf{0.500}$$

**4. Which prompt's advantages are larger after normalization, and is that right?**

Prompt A's raw advantages are $\{+0.125 \times 7,\ -0.875\}$; after dividing by 0.331: $\{+0.378,\ -2.644\}$.

Prompt B's raw advantages are $\pm 0.5$; after dividing by 0.500: $\{\pm 1.000\}$.

**Prompt A's largest-magnitude advantage is 2.64 versus Prompt B's 1.00** — a factor of 2.6.

**Is that the prompt you want to learn from?** **No.**

Prompt A is nearly solved — the model gets it right 7 times in 8. There is little left to learn. Prompt B is at exactly 50%, which per [Exercise 15.1](ch15.md) is where the signal is: the model is genuinely uncertain and the comparison between successes and failures is maximally informative.

The std normalization does the opposite of what you want: it **amplifies advantages on low-variance prompts**, which are precisely the near-solved and near-impossible ones. It systematically upweights the least informative parts of your batch.

**5. The two Dr. GRPO fixes and their costs.**

**Fix 1 — remove the $1/|o_i|$ length normalization.** Use $\sum_t \rho_{i,t} A_i$ without dividing, or normalize by a *constant* (e.g. a fixed maximum length) so all tokens carry equal weight.

*Cost:* long responses now contribute proportionally more total gradient than short ones, since they have more tokens. That is arguably correct — a longer response *is* more of the policy's output — but it means batch gradient magnitude varies with the length distribution, so you may need to adjust the learning rate or normalize by total tokens in the batch rather than per-response.

**Fix 2 — remove the division by group standard deviation.** Use $A_i = r_i - \bar r$ directly.

*Cost:* advantage magnitudes now vary across prompts with the reward scale. With a binary reward this is fine (advantages are bounded in $[-1, 1]$). With a continuous or multi-component reward, one prompt whose rewards happen to span a wide range will dominate the batch. If you use continuous rewards, you need *some* normalization — but do it **globally over the batch**, not per-group, so the relative informativeness of prompts is preserved.

Both fixes are one-line changes, both were reported to help, and both are worth trying before reaching for anything more elaborate.

## 15.4 🏗️ Design a reward for SQL generation

**1. The reward function.**

```python
def sql_reward(response: str, question: str, schema: Schema, gold_result) -> float:
    reward = 0.0

    # (a) Format: reasoning in <think>, final query in a fenced sql block.
    if has_think_tags(response) and has_sql_block(response):
        reward += 0.05                      # deliberately small; see part 6

    query = extract_sql(response)
    if query is None:
        return reward

    # (b) Parses as valid SQL for this dialect.
    if not parses(query, dialect="postgres"):
        return reward
    reward += 0.05

    # (c) References only tables and columns that exist.
    if not schema_valid(query, schema):
        return reward
    reward += 0.10

    # (d) Executes without error against a read-only replica, under a timeout.
    ok, result = execute_readonly(query, timeout_s=5)
    if not ok:
        return reward
    reward += 0.10

    # (e) Result-set equivalence with the gold answer. The main term.
    if results_equivalent(result, gold_result):   # order-insensitive, type-aware
        reward += 0.70

    return reward
```

**2. Verifiable versus not.**

| Component | Verifiable? |
|---|---|
| (a) Format | **Yes** — regex |
| (b) Parses | **Yes** — a SQL parser |
| (c) Schema-valid | **Yes** — check identifiers against the catalogue |
| (d) Executes | **Yes** — run it |
| (e) Result equivalence | **Yes**, with care — see part 4 |
| Query *quality* (readable, efficient, idiomatic) | **No** |
| Whether it answers the *intent* when the gold answer is itself ambiguous | **No** |

The domain is unusually well-suited to RLVR because four of five components are programmatically checkable, and the fifth is checkable against a reference result set.

**3. What gaming looks like.**

The obvious exploit, if `results_equivalent` compares result sets:

```sql
-- Question: "How many orders did customer 42 place in 2024?"
SELECT 3;
```

If the gold answer is the single value 3, this returns an equivalent result set without touching a table. The model has learned to *guess the answer* rather than *write the query*.

A subtler one:

```sql
SELECT COUNT(*) FROM orders;  -- ignores both the customer and the year filters
```

which happens to return the right number for this particular database state.

**4. Fixing the reward.**

Three changes, all necessary:

- **Require the query to reference the relevant tables.** Extend check (c): the query must reference at least the tables the gold query references. This kills `SELECT 3;` outright.
- **Evaluate against multiple database states.** Run the query against the real state *and* against 2–3 perturbed states (different row counts, shifted dates) with recomputed gold answers. A query that guesses a constant, or that ignores a filter, fails on the perturbed states. **This is the important one** — it converts "produces the right number" into "computes the right function," and it is the general fix for constant-guessing in any verifiable domain.
- **Penalize trivially-degenerate queries** — no `FROM` clause, or a query with no predicates when the gold has predicates.

The general principle: **a verifier that checks the output can be satisfied by guessing the output; a verifier that checks the output across multiple inputs cannot.** This is exactly why code verification uses a *test suite* rather than one test case.

**5. Difficulty calibration.**

Per [Exercise 15.1](ch15.md) part 5:

- Sample $G = 4$ from the current policy on the full prompt pool, purely to measure pass rates. Cheap.
- Keep prompts with pass rate in $[0.05, 0.90]$ for training.
- Re-measure every few hundred steps, because the distribution shifts as the model improves.
- Keep the excluded hard prompts in a holding set and re-test them each round rather than discarding.
- Construct difficulty deliberately: single-table lookups (easy), joins (medium), window functions and CTEs and correlated subqueries (hard). This gives you a supply of prompts at every difficulty rather than relying on a natural distribution.

**6. The format bonus, and why it must be small.**

Here it is 0.05 out of a maximum 1.00 — **5%**.

**Why small:** format is far easier to optimize than correctness. If the format bonus were, say, 0.4, the policy would saturate it within a few hundred steps and then have little marginal incentive to improve accuracy — the gradient from the format term is large and easily obtained, the gradient from the accuracy term is small and hard-won.

The failure mode is §15.7's **format collapse**: perfect `<think>` tags containing "Let me think. The answer is:" followed by a wrong query. The model has learned the theatre of reasoning because that is what you paid for.

The format bonus exists so that *parsing is reliable* — you need to be able to extract the query. It is infrastructure, not an objective.

## 15.5 🏗️ Diagnose the three GRPO failures

**1. Accuracy plateaus, length rises 2,000 → 9,000.**

**Failure: length exploitation.**

**Mechanism, and there are two overlapping ones:**
- The genuine one (§15.6): longer reasoning helps up to a point, and past that point the model continues extending because nothing penalizes it — restating the problem, enumerating irrelevant cases, re-verifying what it already verified.
- The mechanical one ([Exercise 15.3](ch15.md) part 2): the $1/|o_i|$ normalization penalizes long wrong answers *less per token*, actively selecting for verbose failure.

**Fix:** remove the length normalization (Dr. GRPO fix 1). Then, if length still grows without accuracy: add an explicit penalty past a threshold — not a global length penalty, which punishes legitimately-long reasoning, but a soft penalty above, say, the 90th percentile of successful-response lengths. Also cap generation length and accept truncation; the accuracy cost is usually smaller than expected.

**2. Format 99.8% by step 400, accuracy flat.**

**Failure: format collapse.**

**Mechanism:** the format bonus is large relative to the accuracy bonus. Format is trivially learnable — a few hundred steps — and once saturated, the model has captured most of the available reward. The accuracy term's gradient is comparatively tiny, so improvement on it is slow, and if the format term dominated the early training the model may have converged to a policy that emits well-formed empty reasoning.

**Fix:** reduce the format bonus to a few percent of the total. Check the reward decomposition — log the mean of each reward component separately. If format contributes more than ~10% of mean total reward at convergence, it is too large.

Also check the accuracy verifier itself: flat accuracy at the step-0 value can mean the verifier is broken and never returns 1.

**3. Second-language fragments, readability degrading, accuracy fine.**

**Failure: specification gap** — the R1-Zero problem (§19.4).

**Mechanism:** nothing in the reward mentions language or readability. The model uses whatever tokens are most efficient for reasoning, and if a phrase in another language is a denser representation of a mathematical concept, the policy discovers it. Accuracy is fine because accuracy is what you rewarded.

**Fix:** add a **language-consistency term** — the fraction of the trace in the target language — as R1 did. Note that R1 reported this slightly *degrades* measured accuracy and they kept it anyway, which is the correct call: a trace users cannot read is worse in a way accuracy does not measure.

Alternatively (or additionally): a small KL penalty against a reference model that writes properly, or a cold-start SFT stage on readable traces before RL.

**4. Is any of this reward hacking in the §12.6 sense?**

**No — and the distinction matters.**

§12.6's reward hacking is: **the policy finds inputs where a *learned proxy* scores highly and a human would not.** It exists because the reward model is an *approximation* of an underlying preference, and approximations have exploitable error.

All three failures here are different. The reward is a **program**, and in every case it returned exactly what it was written to return:

- Case 1: the reward genuinely does not penalize length. Not an error in the reward — an *omission*.
- Case 2: the reward genuinely does give a large bonus for format. Working as specified.
- Case 3: the reward genuinely says nothing about language. Working as specified.

**The precise distinction: reward hacking is the reward being *wrong*; these are the reward being *incomplete*.** With a learned reward model you cannot enumerate the ways it is wrong, which is why you need a KL budget and independent evaluation. With a verifiable reward you *can* enumerate what it does not measure — and every property you care about but did not encode is free to degrade.

That is why §15.4 says the KL machinery matters far less with verifiable rewards, and why §15.7 says the failures you get instead are specification failures. Two different problems requiring two different defences: for reward hacking, constrain the optimization; for specification gaps, fix the specification.

## 15.6 🔢 Length growth: two causes, one symptom

**1. The mechanisms.**

**Run A (learned reward model):** annotators mildly preferred longer responses; the reward model learned the correlation; RL discovered that length is far easier to increase than quality and exploited it. The reward model is *scoring length* and calling it quality. This is [Chapter 12 §12.6](../../book/part-3-post-training/12-reward-modeling.md)'s length bias.

**Run B (verifier):** longer reasoning produces correct answers more often — more chances to catch an error, try a second approach, verify a step. Length rises as a *consequence* of optimizing accuracy, and the reward never mentions length at all. This is §15.6's emergent length growth.

**2. Which is which.**

**A is the artifact. B is genuine** — up to the point where accuracy stops improving, after which B becomes the exploitation described in §15.5 case 1.

**3. A single measurement that distinguishes them.**

**Plot accuracy (or an independent quality measure) against response length, within the same checkpoint.**

Sample many responses at the current checkpoint, bin them by length, and measure the success rate per bin.

- **Run B (genuine):** success rate rises with length, at least over the range the policy occupies. Longer responses really are more often correct.
- **Run A (artifact):** success rate is **flat or declining** with length, while the *reward model's score* rises with length. The reward and the quality disagree as a function of length, which is the signature.

The key design point: **measure within a single checkpoint, not across training.** Across training, both length and quality rise together for confounded reasons. Within one checkpoint, length varies naturally across samples and you can read the relationship directly.

A cheaper approximation for run A specifically: compute the reward model's score-versus-length correlation on held-out data ([Exercise 12.3](ch12.md)). A correlation above ~0.5 means you have the artifact regardless of what else is happening.

**4. Should you intervene?**

**Run A: yes, and at the reward model, not at the policy.**

The problem is that your measurement instrument is broken. Adding a length penalty to the policy's objective treats the symptom and leaves you with a reward model that still cannot distinguish thorough from padded — which will cause other problems. Fix it by balancing the preference pairs on length ([Exercise 12.3](ch12.md) part 4), and use the length penalty only as a stopgap.

**Run B: it depends, and this is a genuine product decision rather than a technical one.**

*Argument against intervening:* length growth is the mechanism by which the model gets better. R1's traces reach tens of thousands of tokens and that is where the capability comes from. Capping it caps the capability, and §16.6's test-time-compute scaling says the extra tokens are buying real accuracy.

*Argument for intervening:* tokens cost money and latency. A model that reasons for 20,000 tokens to gain two points is a different product from one that answers in 2,000. And past the point where the accuracy-versus-length curve flattens (part 3), you are paying for padding.

**The resolution:** intervene when the *marginal* accuracy per token goes to zero, not before. Use the part-3 measurement to find where the curve flattens, and set your penalty threshold there. Kimi k1.5 [\[69\]](../../book/appendix/b-references.md#69-kimi-k15) treats length control as an explicit, tuned component for exactly this reason.

## 15.7 🔢 Memory and the KL term

**1. With the reference model.**

- Policy (trained, mixed precision): $70\times10^9 \times 16 = 1{,}120$ GB
- Reference (frozen, BF16): $140$ GB
- Verifier: **free** (it is a program)

$$\mathbf{1{,}260 \text{ GB}} \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{16 \text{ GPUs}}$$

**2. With $\beta = 0$.**

$$\mathbf{1{,}120 \text{ GB}} \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{14 \text{ GPUs}}$$

An 11% saving — smaller than the RLOO-versus-PPO saving, because the reference is frozen and BF16 rather than a trained model.

But note what else goes: **a forward pass through a 70B model on every rollout token, every step**. That is a throughput saving as well as a memory one, and it may be the larger consideration.

**3. What you give up at $\beta = 0$.**

- **Readability and language consistency anchoring** (§15.7). The reference model writes properly; the KL term pulls the policy toward writing properly. Remove it and nothing does.
- **General-capability preservation.** Unconstrained optimization on math and code can degrade everything else — the model becomes a specialist by forgetting. The KL term bounds how far it can drift from the general-purpose reference.
- **A measurable drift budget.** Without the KL term you have no number describing how far the policy moved, which matters when you need to report it (§13.6, and [Exercise 13.6](ch13.md) case 5).

What you do *not* give up, and this is why $\beta = 0$ is viable here: **protection against reward hacking.** With a verifiable reward there is no learned proxy to hack (§15.4), so the KL term's primary job in RLHF does not apply.

**4. How cold-start SFT substitutes.**

Two mechanisms:

- **It sets a better starting point.** RL amplifies what the policy already does. A policy that already writes readable, single-language, well-formatted traces will produce rollouts with those properties, and the successful ones — which are what get reinforced — will retain them. The behaviour is preserved by being *present in the data RL learns from*, rather than by a penalty.
- **It reduces the exploration burden**, so RL needs to move less far, so unconstrained drift is smaller in practice even without a formal constraint.

This is a genuinely different mechanism from the KL term. The KL term says "do not move far from the reference"; cold-start says "start close to where you want to end up, and let RL move freely from there." R1 chose the second, and it worked — but note it is a *weaker* guarantee. Nothing prevents the policy from drifting arbitrarily far over a long enough run; it merely starts in a better place.

**5. When to insist on keeping the KL term.**

- **When the reward is partly learned.** R1's Stage 4 used learned reward models for helpfulness and safety (§19.5), and there §12.6 applies in full.
- **When you must report the drift.** Safety-relevant training where "how far did the model move" is an auditable question.
- **When general capability matters and you are optimizing hard on a narrow domain.** A long RL run on math with $\beta = 0$ can measurably degrade conversational quality; the KL term is the cheapest insurance.
- **When you have no cold-start stage.** If you are running R1-Zero-style directly from a base model, the KL term is the only thing anchoring readability — and R1-Zero's problems (§19.4) are what happens without either.

## 15.8 📄 Read the R1 emergent-behaviour section

**1. AIME numbers and budget.**

R1-Zero's AIME 2024 **pass@1** rose from **15.6% to 71.0%** over training. With **majority voting at 64 samples**, it reached **86.7%**.

Both numbers matter and they are different claims. 71.0% is a single-attempt capability figure; 86.7% is a capability-plus-inference-budget figure. Reporting the second without the first — which is common when these numbers circulate — overstates what the model does per query (§23.8).

**2. Length over training, and what the reward said about it.**

They report that average response length **increased steadily throughout training**, with the model spontaneously producing longer and longer reasoning traces.

**The reward said nothing about length.** It had two components: accuracy (does the final answer match) and format (are the reasoning tags present). No length term, no length penalty, no length target.

That is what makes it an emergent behaviour rather than a trained one, and it is the cleanest published demonstration of §15.6's mechanism.

**3. The "aha moment," reported and restated.**

**As reported:** at an intermediate training checkpoint, the model — mid-derivation — stops, states something to the effect of "wait, let me reconsider," and revisits its approach. The paper presents this as a striking observation and notes it emerged without being demonstrated in any training data.

**Restated mechanically:** GRPO increases the log-probability of token sequences that appeared in responses receiving above-average reward within their group. Traces containing a self-correction were, on average, more often correct — because reconsidering an approach catches errors. So the policy gradient increased the probability of the tokens that constitute self-correction. Over many steps, "wait, let me reconsider" became a high-probability continuation in contexts where the model's current path is going badly.

No mechanism beyond policy gradient on a correctness signal is required. **The interesting fact is not that gradient descent produced it — it is that the base model already assigned non-trivial probability to such traces**, which is §19.4's point about reasoning being latent.

**4. R1-Zero's two problems, and the reward-function explanation.**

- **Poor readability.** The reward measured whether the answer was right and whether the tags were present. It did not measure whether a human could follow the reasoning. So readability was unconstrained and drifted toward whatever was efficient for the model.
- **Language mixing.** The reward did not mention language. The model reasoned in whatever token sequences worked, mixing English and Chinese within a trace when that was a more efficient representation.

Both have the same explanation and it is §15.7's general principle: **RL optimizes exactly what you specify. Every property you care about but did not encode is free to degrade.** R1-Zero is the cleanest published demonstration of that at frontier scale, which is a large part of why the paper is worth reading directly.

## 15.9 🧪 Break the GRPO lab

**1. Length over training.**

*Prediction people make:* length stays flat, since nothing rewards it.

*What happens:* **it rises**, often substantially, and the shape is typically a rise followed by a plateau. On a toy task the plateau comes early because the task does not benefit from unbounded reasoning.

Watch for the rise beginning *before* accuracy improves much — the model discovers that longer traces help before it becomes reliably correct.

**2. Format bonus = accuracy bonus.**

*Prediction:* the model gets better at both.

*What happens:* **format saturates within ~100–200 steps and accuracy stalls.** You get perfectly-formatted reasoning blocks containing nothing — the model has captured half the available reward for a fraction of the effort, and the remaining accuracy gradient is small by comparison.

Look at actual generations at step 300. The `<think>` blocks will be short and contentless. This is §15.7's format collapse, reproducible on demand.

**3. Std normalization off.**

*Prediction:* little change, since it is just a scale factor.

*What happens:* **the prompts dominating the gradient change.** With normalization, near-solved and near-impossible prompts (low variance) had their advantages amplified ([Exercise 15.3](ch15.md) part 4). Without it, prompts near 50% pass rate — which have the *largest* raw advantage spread — dominate.

The observable: log which prompts contribute the largest gradient norms, before and after. The composition shifts toward medium-difficulty prompts, which is the desired direction. Sample efficiency typically improves.

**4. Filter to pass rates in [0.2, 0.8].**

*Prediction:* modest improvement.

*What happens:* **substantial improvement in sample efficiency**, consistent with [Exercise 15.1](ch15.md) part 5's arithmetic — roughly 1.5× more useful gradient per unit of generation, sometimes more.

The important secondary observation: **it must be redone periodically.** Filter once at step 0 and by step 2,000 the policy has improved, half your "medium" prompts are now easy, and you are back to a degenerate batch. Re-measuring every few hundred steps is what keeps the gain.

**5. $G = 2$.**

*Prediction:* noisier but workable.

*What happens:* **noticeably unstable.** With two samples, the advantages are $\pm(r_1 - r_2)/2$ — the baseline is a single other sample, so the estimate has maximum variance. With a binary reward, a group is either degenerate (both same, zero gradient) or maximally polarized (one $+$, one $-$), with nothing in between.

From [Exercise 15.1](ch15.md)'s distribution, at $G=2$ the zero-variance fraction is $\sum_b s_b(p_b^2 + (1-p_b)^2) = 0.30(0.905) + 0.20(0.5) + 0.30(0.82) + 0.20(1.0) = 0.81$ — **81% of prompts contribute nothing.** You are throwing away four fifths of your generation budget.

The lesson pairs with [Exercise 15.2](ch15.md): $G$ has a floor as well as a ceiling, and the useful range is narrower than either extreme suggests.
