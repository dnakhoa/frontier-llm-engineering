## 8.1 🔢 Chinchilla arithmetic

**1. Compute-optimal $N$.**

With $D = 20N$ and $C = 6ND$:

$$C = 6N(20N) = 120N^2 \;\Rightarrow\; N = \sqrt{\frac{C}{120}} = \sqrt{\frac{10^{24}}{120}} = \sqrt{8.33\times10^{21}} \approx \mathbf{9.1\times10^{10} = 91\text{B}}$$

**2. Tokens.** $D = 20 \times 9.1\times10^{10} = \mathbf{1.82\times10^{12} = 1.82\text{T}}$.

**3. Inference FLOPs.**

Inference costs roughly $2N$ FLOPs per generated token (forward only, no backward):

$$2 \times 9.1\times10^{10} \times 10^{10} \text{ requests} \times 500 \text{ tokens} = \mathbf{9.1\times10^{23} \text{ FLOPs}}$$

**Nearly equal to the entire training budget of $10^{24}$.** (And this ignores prefill, which for typical prompt lengths adds substantially more.)

**4. The argument for a smaller model.**

Chinchilla minimizes the loss for a fixed *training* compute budget. It says nothing about inference, and part 3 shows inference here is comparable to training in total FLOPs — so the true objective is

$$\min_{N} \; \text{loss}(N, D) \quad \text{subject to} \quad C_{\text{train}}(N,D) + C_{\text{inference}}(N) \le \text{budget}$$

Since inference cost scales with $N$ and not with $D$, and training cost scales with both, **the optimum shifts toward smaller $N$ and larger $D$.** You spend more training tokens on a smaller model, accepting a slightly worse loss per unit of training compute in exchange for a permanently cheaper model to serve.

How much smaller? Sardana et al. [\[43\]](../../book/appendix/b-references.md#43-beyond-chinchilla-sardana-et-al-2024) work this out formally, and the practical answer at high inference volume is **substantially smaller — often 3–10× below Chinchilla-optimal**, with the token count raised to compensate. At 10 billion requests, something in the 10–30B range trained on far more than 1.82T tokens dominates a 91B model trained to Chinchilla.

**5. Llama-3-8B.**

$$\frac{15\times10^{12}}{8\times10^9} = \mathbf{1{,}875 \text{ tokens per parameter}}$$

Roughly **94× past Chinchilla's 20:1.**

What it says about Meta's view: they were not optimizing training compute at all. They were optimizing *lifetime* cost for a model intended to be downloaded and served enormously many times, by many parties. In that regime the training run is a one-time cost amortized over an effectively unbounded number of inference calls, and the correct move is to push $D$ as far as the returns hold and keep $N$ small.

The general principle worth carrying: **Chinchilla answers a question almost nobody actually has.** It is the right analysis for "I have this much compute and want the best model," and the wrong analysis for "I want the best model to deploy," which is what nearly everyone means.

## 8.2 🔢 The learning-rate schedule

**1. LR values.**

Warmup is linear over 2,000 steps to $3\times10^{-4}$; cosine decays from peak to $3\times10^{-5}$ over the remaining 498,000 steps.

- **Step 1,000:** halfway through warmup — $1.5\times10^{-4}$.
- **Step 2,000:** peak — $3.0\times10^{-4}$.
- **Step 250,000:** progress through decay $= (250{,}000 - 2{,}000)/498{,}000 = 0.498$.
  $$\text{LR} = 3\times10^{-5} + \tfrac{1}{2}(3\times10^{-4} - 3\times10^{-5})\left(1 + \cos(0.498\pi)\right)$$
  $\cos(0.498\pi) \approx 0.0063$, so LR $\approx 3\times10^{-5} + 0.5 \times 2.7\times10^{-4} \times 1.0063 \approx \mathbf{1.66\times10^{-4}}$.
- **Step 500,000:** final — $3.0\times10^{-5}$.

**2. Why warmup.**

At initialization, Adam's second-moment estimate $v$ is near zero and poorly estimated from the first few gradients. The update is $\eta \cdot \hat{m}/(\sqrt{\hat{v}} + \epsilon)$, so a small, noisy $\hat{v}$ produces **enormous effective step sizes** in the first steps.

Concretely, without warmup: the first few updates move weights far from initialization, into a region where the loss surface is badly conditioned, and the run either diverges immediately or converges to a noticeably worse solution. It is the most reliably reproducible instability in transformer training.

Warmup gives $v$ time to accumulate a reasonable estimate before the step size becomes large.

**3. Extending to 700,000 steps mid-run.**

**What breaks:** the cosine schedule is defined in terms of the *total* step count. Change the total and the entire curve changes shape, including the part you already trained through. If you simply recompute the cosine for 700,000 total steps, the LR at step 500,001 *jumps up* — the model experiences a discontinuous LR increase, which commonly produces a loss spike and can destabilize the run.

**Options, in order of preference:**

- **Keep the original cosine and append a constant-low-LR tail.** Ugly, safe, and it wastes the extra compute — the model is barely learning at $3\times10^{-5}$.
- **Restart the cosine as a new decay from the current LR** down to a lower final value. This is a "warm restart" and it works, with a brief loss bump at the transition.
- **Accept the discontinuity but re-warm over a few thousand steps** to the new schedule's value, rather than jumping.

**The real lesson:** this problem exists *only* because cosine is parameterized by total steps. It is the strongest practical argument for part 4.

**4. Why WSD (warmup-stable-decay) instead.**

Constant LR for the bulk of the run, then a short decay at the end (typically the last 10–20%).

What it buys:

- **The run length does not need to be known in advance.** You can decide to stop at any point, run the decay, and get a properly annealed model. This directly solves part 3.
- **You can branch.** From a single stable-phase checkpoint, run several different decays with different data mixtures (§9.4's annealing) and compare, without redoing the expensive bulk.
- **Intermediate checkpoints are usable.** Under cosine, a mid-run checkpoint is at a high LR and is not a finished model. Under WSD, any stable-phase checkpoint plus a short decay is a finished model.

The cost is a small quality gap relative to a well-tuned cosine at a known length — and in practice the flexibility usually wins, which is why WSD has been steadily displacing cosine.

## 8.3 🔢 Batch size and LR coupling

**1. Square-root scaling.** $3\times10^{-4} \times \sqrt{2} = \mathbf{4.24\times10^{-4}}$.

**2. Linear scaling.** $3\times10^{-4} \times 2 = \mathbf{6.0\times10^{-4}}$.

**3. Which is right for Adam.**

**Square root.** The reasoning: linear scaling is derived for SGD, where the update magnitude is proportional to the gradient, so doubling the batch halves the gradient noise and you can afford a proportionally larger step.

Adam **normalizes** the update by $\sqrt{v}$, so the update magnitude is roughly scale-invariant in the gradient. Doubling the batch reduces gradient *variance* by 2×, hence the noise scale by $\sqrt{2}$, and the LR can grow by $\sqrt{2}$ rather than 2.

In practice, at large batch even square-root scaling is optimistic, and labs tune empirically rather than trusting either rule.

**4. Critical batch size.**

The batch size above which increasing it further stops improving convergence per *step* proportionally. Below it, doubling the batch roughly halves the number of steps needed. Above it, doubling the batch gives progressively less step reduction — you are averaging over gradients that were already low-noise, and the extra samples add little information.

Above the critical batch size you are spending compute for no gain in progress-per-token. It grows over training (gradients get noisier relative to the signal as the loss flattens), which is why batch-size ramp-up schedules exist.

**5. Why 2× GPUs may not give 2× progress.**

Because throughput and progress are different quantities.

Doubling GPUs and doubling batch gives you 2× **tokens per second**. But if you were already at or above the critical batch size, those extra tokens produce sub-proportionally fewer steps to a given loss — so wall-clock time to target loss improves by less than 2×.

The alternatives, both of which have costs:
- **Keep the batch and use the GPUs for more parallelism** (TP, PP), reducing time per step instead. Costs communication, which is why it does not scale indefinitely (§6.4).
- **Use the extra compute for a larger model** instead of more throughput. Often the right answer, and it changes the run rather than accelerating it.

This is the fundamental reason very large clusters are hard to use efficiently, and it is why §6.8's "make DP as large as possible" has a ceiling.

## 8.4 🔢 Precision and dynamic range

**1. Ranges.**

| Format | Exp / mant | Max magnitude | Relative precision |
|---|---|---|---|
| BF16 | 8 / 7 | $\approx 3.4\times10^{38}$ | $2^{-8} \approx 3.9\times10^{-3}$ |
| FP16 | 5 / 10 | $\approx 65{,}504$ | $2^{-11} \approx 4.9\times10^{-4}$ |

BF16 has FP32's exponent range with fewer mantissa bits; FP16 has more precision over a much narrower range.

**2. Gradients at $10^{-8}$.**

**BF16 handles it; FP16 does not.** FP16's smallest normal is $\approx 6.1\times10^{-5}$, and subnormals extend only to $\approx 6\times10^{-8}$ with rapidly degrading precision. A gradient of $10^{-8}$ in FP16 is at the edge of subnormal territory and many smaller values flush to zero outright.

This is exactly why FP16 training requires **loss scaling** — multiply the loss by a large constant so gradients land in the representable range, then divide before the optimizer step — and why BF16 does not. Removing loss scaling removed an entire category of instability, and it is the main reason BF16 displaced FP16 for transformer training despite having 3 fewer mantissa bits.

**3. FP8 E4M3.**

4 exponent bits, 3 mantissa. Max magnitude 448; smallest normal $\approx 2^{-6} = 0.0156$; relative precision $2^{-4} \approx 6\%$.

**Dynamic range is about $2^{9} \approx 512$** between smallest normal and max — versus BF16's $\approx 2^{277}$.

**Why per-tensor scaling is insufficient:** a single scale factor for a whole tensor must be chosen so the largest element does not overflow. If the tensor contains one outlier 1,000× larger than the typical element — which activations routinely do — then scaling for the outlier pushes every typical element down near or below the smallest normal, where it either quantizes to a handful of distinct values or flushes to zero.

The fix is **finer-grained scaling**: per-block (DeepSeek-V3 uses tile-wise and block-wise scaling), per-channel, or per-group, so an outlier only degrades its own block.

**4. Accumulating 8,192 BF16 values in BF16.**

Each addition has relative error up to $\epsilon = 2^{-8} \approx 3.9\times10^{-3}$. For a naive sequential sum of $n$ terms, worst-case relative error grows like $n\epsilon$ and random-walk error like $\sqrt{n}\epsilon$:

$$\sqrt{8192} \times 3.9\times10^{-3} \approx 0.35 \quad \text{(random)}, \qquad 8192 \times 3.9\times10^{-3} \approx 32 \quad \text{(worst case)}$$

**A 35% error in the typical case.** The mechanism is that once the running sum is large, adding a small element rounds it away entirely — the accumulator has only 7 mantissa bits, so anything more than $2^{8}$ times smaller than the running total contributes nothing.

FP32 accumulation gives $\epsilon = 2^{-24} \approx 6\times10^{-8}$, so $\sqrt{8192} \times 6\times10^{-8} \approx 5\times10^{-6}$ — negligible.

This is why every kernel in Chapter 20 accumulates in FP32 regardless of storage precision.

**5. Three operations to keep in BF16 under an FP8 regime.**

- **The embedding layer and the output projection / softmax.** Logits have large dynamic range and the softmax is exponential, so quantization error is amplified. Also, this is where the loss is computed and errors here affect every gradient.
- **Normalization layers (RMSNorm/LayerNorm).** They compute a reduction over the hidden dimension and then divide — precisely the §8.4 part 4 accumulation problem, and the division by a slightly-wrong norm scales the entire activation.
- **The master weights and gradients** stay FP32, so small updates do not vanish against large weights. V3 went the other way on the **optimizer moments**: it stored them in BF16, not FP32. V3's own list of operators kept in BF16/FP32 is the embedding, the output head, **MoE gating**, normalization and attention ([fact sheet](../../book/appendix/fact-sheets/deepseek-v3.md#precision)).

A fourth worth mentioning: **attention's softmax**, for the same exponential-amplification reason as the output softmax.

## 8.5 🔢 Read the loss curve

| # | Pattern | Two likely causes | First change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Jump of 0.4, no recovery | (a) A genuine divergence event — LR too high for the current loss landscape; (b) corrupted data batch or a hardware fault at that step | **Roll back to a checkpoint before the spike and skip the data batches around step 47,000.** If it recurs at the same data, it is the data. If it recurs at a different step, it is the LR. This bisection is the standard procedure. |
| 2 | Repeated spike-and-recover | (a) Occasional pathological batches — very long repeated sequences, or unusual token distributions; (b) LR marginally too high, so the model periodically leaves the stable region and returns | Lower peak LR by 20–30%, or tighten gradient clipping. If spikes correlate with specific data shards, filter them. Recovery is a good sign — the run is stable but operating near the edge. |
| 3 | Plateau above the expected curve, early | (a) LR too *low* — the model is under-training; (b) a bug: wrong loss masking, wrong data, a frozen parameter group | **Check that all parameters are receiving gradients**, then check the data by decoding a batch. A very early plateau is far more often a bug than a hyperparameter. |
| 4 | Gradient norm growing steadily | (a) Weight norms growing without effective weight decay; (b) an unbounded logit or attention-score growth — the classic reason for z-loss and QK-norm | Check whether weight decay is actually decoupled and applied. Then add QK-norm or z-loss. Growing gradient norm is the leading indicator of a future spike. |
| 5 | Faster early, worse final | (a) LR too high — fast initial progress, worse final convergence; (b) the data ordering differed and the model saw easy data first | Compare final-LR values and the decay shape between runs. This pattern is the signature of an over-aggressive LR, and it is deceptive precisely because the early curve looks better. |
| 6 | `NaN` at step 3 | (a) No warmup, or warmup far too short (§8.2); (b) a bad initialization scale, or FP16 without loss scaling | Check warmup length first — it is the single most common cause of an immediate `NaN`. Then check the initialization and the precision configuration. |

**The general diagnostic principle:** distinguish *data* causes from *hyperparameter* causes by rolling back and rerunning with different data ordering. If the event follows the step number, it is the recipe; if it follows the data, it is the data.

## 8.6 🏗️ Design a recipe

For a 30B MoE (~3.5B active) on 6T tokens.

**1. Optimizer.** AdamW, $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.95$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$, weight decay 0.1.

$\beta_2 = 0.95$ rather than 0.999: shorter second-moment memory adapts faster to changing gradient scale, which matters for MoE where an expert's gradient magnitude changes as routing shifts. This is the near-universal choice for LLM pre-training and it differs from the Adam paper's default for exactly this reason.

**2. Schedule.** Peak LR $3\times10^{-4}$; warmup 2,000 steps; **WSD** — constant at peak until 85% of the planned tokens, then cosine decay to 10% of peak over the final 15%.

WSD rather than cosine, per §8.2 part 4: the token budget on a first run of a new architecture is not reliably known in advance, and being able to branch the decay for mixture ablations (§9.4) is worth the small quality difference.

**3. Batch.** Ramp from 1M to 8M tokens over the first 5% of training, then hold at 8M.

The ramp exists because the critical batch size (§8.3 part 4) is small early and grows. A large batch at step 0 wastes compute on gradients that are already low-noise; a small batch late is unnecessarily noisy.

**4. Gradient clipping.** Global norm clip at **1.0**.

Not tuned — 1.0 is the field standard and it works. Its job is not optimization, it is *insurance*: it bounds the damage from a single pathological batch. Monitor the fraction of steps that clip; if it exceeds ~5%, the LR is too high rather than the clip too tight.

**5. Precision.**

- **FP8 (E4M3)** for the forward and weight-gradient GEMMs in the FFN/expert layers and the attention projections, with **block-wise scaling** (§8.4 part 3).
- **BF16** for: embeddings, the output projection and logits, all normalization layers, the attention softmax, the router (routing decisions must not be quantization-noisy or load balance degrades), and the MoE all-to-all payload.
- **FP32** for: all accumulation inside GEMMs, the master weights, and both optimizer moments.

**On a first run, start in pure BF16.** FP8 is a throughput optimization worth ~2×, and it is also a new source of instability. Validate the recipe in BF16 at small scale, then enable FP8 — do not debug two things at once.

**6. Stability measures.**

- **QK-norm from step 0.** Cheap, and it prevents the attention-logit growth that drives §8.5 pattern 4. There is essentially no downside.
- **z-loss with a small coefficient ($10^{-4}$) from step 0.** Keeps the output logits from drifting to large magnitudes, which matters more under FP8.
- **Held in reserve:** lowering $\beta_2$, tightening the clip, and skipping data batches around a spike. These are interventions for when something goes wrong, not defaults.

The reasoning for "from step 0" on the first two: both are nearly free, and retrofitting a stability measure mid-run introduces a discontinuity of exactly the kind §8.2 part 3 warns about.

**7. Monitoring and intervention thresholds.**

| Metric | Watch for | Intervene at |
|---|---|---|
| Loss | Spikes | >3σ above trailing 100-step mean |
| Gradient norm | Steady growth | Trend upward over 10k steps |
| Fraction of steps clipped | Rising | >5% sustained |
| Expert load balance (max/mean) | Imbalance | >1.5× sustained (§5.7) |
| FP8 overflow/underflow counts | Any nonzero trend | Any sustained increase |
| Activation and weight norms per layer | Divergence between layers | Any layer >3× the others |
| Throughput | Regression | >10% for 5 min (§7.6) |

**The intervention that is almost always right for a loss spike:** roll back 500–1,000 steps, skip the implicated data, and resume. Reducing the LR mid-run is a bigger hammer and it changes the schedule (§8.2 part 3), so reach for it only when rollback fails twice.

## 8.7 🔢 muP and what it buys

**1. Why the optimal LR moves with width under standard parameterization.**

Under standard parameterization, as width $d$ grows, the scale of activations and of gradient updates through each layer changes. Concretely, a pre-activation is a sum of $d$ terms; its magnitude grows with $d$ unless the initialization compensates, and the *update* to that pre-activation after a gradient step scales differently again.

The consequence is that the same nominal learning rate produces a *different effective change in the function* at different widths. Empirically, the optimal LR shrinks roughly as $1/d$ under standard parameterization — so hyperparameters tuned at 100M are wrong at 30B, in a predictable direction and by a large factor.

**2. What muP does.**

It re-parameterizes the initialization scales, the learning rates, and the output multipliers **per layer type** so that the magnitude of the change in each layer's output per optimizer step is invariant to width.

The consequence is the useful one: **the optimal learning rate becomes width-independent.** Tune it at small width, use the same value at large width.

**3. Compute saving.**

Tuning 40 configurations at 30B on 6T tokens:

$$40 \times 6 \times 30\times10^9 \times 6\times10^{12} = 40 \times 1.08\times10^{24} = 4.3\times10^{25} \text{ FLOPs}$$

Which is absurd — 40× the cost of the actual run. Nobody does this; it is the point of the comparison.

Tuning 40 configurations at 100M on, say, 2B tokens (a proxy run):

$$40 \times 6 \times 10^8 \times 2\times10^9 = 4.8\times10^{20} \text{ FLOPs}$$

$$\text{ratio} = \frac{4.3\times10^{25}}{4.8\times10^{20}} \approx \mathbf{90{,}000\times \text{ cheaper}}$$

That is what muP buys: hyperparameter search becomes a thing you can actually do, rather than a thing you guess at.

**4. What muP does not transfer, and why it matters.**

muP addresses **width**. It does not, by itself, give you transfer across:

- **Depth.** Optimal hyperparameters still drift with layer count, and depth-muP variants are less settled.
- **Data quantity.** The optimal LR schedule shape depends on the token budget, which is not a muP-controlled dimension.
- **Batch size.** The LR–batch coupling (§8.3) is orthogonal and still needs its own treatment.
- **Architecture changes.** Adding MoE, changing the attention variant, or changing the activation invalidates the transfer, because muP's scaling rules are derived per layer type.

The practical limitation: **you rarely scale width alone.** A real scale-up increases width, depth, batch, and token count together, and muP handles one of the four. It is a large improvement over nothing and it is not the "tune once at 100M" story it is sometimes described as. Labs using muP still run confirmation experiments at intermediate scales.

## 8.8 📄 Read the FP8 section of the V3 report

**1. The format, and why only one.**

**E4M3 on all tensors**, forward and backward. The report states this in explicit contrast to the usual hybrid (E4M3 forward, E5M2 for gradients) ([fact sheet](../../book/appendix/fact-sheets/deepseek-v3.md#precision)).

Why the hybrid exists: activation *gradients* span a wider dynamic range than activations, so E5M2's extra exponent bit usually matters more than E4M3's extra mantissa bit. This is the same trade as BF16-versus-FP16 (§8.4 part 1), applied one level down.

Why V3 can skip it: range only matters relative to the scale factor. With a separate scale per 1×128 tile and per 128×128 block (part 2), each group only has to cover its *own* range, so E4M3's narrower range suffices and its extra mantissa bit is pure gain.

*Correction in v1.0.1:* earlier editions said V3 used E5M2 for activation gradients. See the [errata](../../book/appendix/d-errata.md).

**2. Scaling strategy and granularity.**

**Fine-grained** rather than per-tensor: tile-wise scaling for activations (groups of elements within a tensor) and block-wise for weights. This is precisely the §8.4 part 3 problem — a single scale per tensor cannot accommodate outliers without crushing the rest — and their contribution is doing the scaling at a granularity fine enough that an outlier only degrades its own block.

**3. Accumulation precision and the hardware driver.**

They describe promoting accumulation to higher precision at intervals, rather than relying on the tensor cores' native FP8 accumulation.

**The hardware limitation:** the FP8 tensor-core accumulate path on that hardware accumulates at limited precision (effectively around 14 bits of mantissa rather than full FP32), which is not enough for long reductions — exactly the §8.4 part 4 problem. Their solution copies partial sums out to CUDA cores for FP32 accumulation at a fixed interval, trading some throughput for numerical correctness.

This is a good example of §20.11's point that kernel work at frontier labs is substantially about accommodating specific hardware behaviour.

**4. The quality claim and its validation.**

They report that FP8 training produced **no measurable degradation relative to BF16**, validated by running a smaller-scale comparison — training the same configuration in both precisions and comparing loss curves, with the relative error in the training loss reported as very small.

The honest caveat, which the report is reasonably clear about: the validation was at a smaller scale than the full run, because running a 671B model twice at 14.8T tokens in two precisions is not affordable. So the claim is "no degradation at the scale we could afford to test, and the loss curve at full scale looked normal" — which is the strongest claim anyone in this position can make, and worth recognizing as such rather than as a fully controlled result.

## 8.9 🧪 Break the scaling-law lab

**1. Fit on 4 sizes, extrapolate 10×.**

*Prediction to make first:* most people predict the extrapolation over-estimates the achievable loss reduction (i.e. predicts the model will be better than it is).

*What happens:* the extrapolation is typically **optimistic** — the fitted power law predicts a lower loss than the real model achieves. Two reasons: the irreducible-loss term is poorly constrained by small models and tends to be under-estimated, and real large-scale runs encounter optimization difficulties (instabilities, imperfect hyperparameter transfer) that the small runs did not.

The magnitude of the error is the useful thing to measure. A 10× extrapolation from a well-fit 4-point curve is usually within a few percent on loss — which sounds excellent until you remember that a few percent of loss is a large difference in capability.

**2. Fit on only the 2 smallest.**

Substantially worse, and — importantly — **the error is not symmetric in the fit quality.** A 3-parameter power law fit to 2 points is exactly determined with no residual, so the fit *looks perfect* and gives you no diagnostic that it is unreliable.

This is the lesson: **the apparent quality of a scaling-law fit tells you nothing about its extrapolation accuracy.** You need enough points to see the residuals, and you need them spread over enough orders of magnitude that the curvature is constrained. Four sizes over two orders of magnitude is a reasonable minimum; two adjacent sizes is not a scaling law, it is a line through two points.

**3. FP8 per-tensor scaling with one outlier.**

*Prediction:* small values lose some precision.

*What happens:* they are **destroyed, but only once the spread exceeds the
format's own dynamic range** — and that threshold is the part worth internalising.

E4M3 spans roughly 0.0156 (smallest normal) to 448, a range of ~29,000×, with
subnormals reaching down to 0.00195. So an outlier merely 1,000× the typical
element still *fits*: scale it to 448 and typical elements land near 0.45, which
E4M3 represents with ~6% relative precision. Degraded, not destroyed. The lab
measures no meaningful difference between per-tensor and per-block scaling at
that ratio.

Push the spread to ~100,000× and it collapses. Typical elements land in the
subnormal range where the only representable values are multiples of 0.00195, so
they are quantised to one of two steps, and anything below about $10^{-3}$ of the
scale flushes to exactly zero. The lab measures **18% of a tensor zeroed and a
15.9% median relative error** under per-tensor scaling, against 0.4% and 2.2%
per-block.

So the rule is not "per-block scaling is better". It is: **scaling granularity
matters exactly when the dynamic range within a scaling group exceeds what the
format can hold, and not before.** That makes an FP8 claim uninterpretable
without the activation statistics beside it — which is why the reports publish
both. Precision failures are threshold effects, not gradual ones, and that is why
they arrive as a cliff at step 40,000 rather than a drift you could have watched.

**4. BF16 accumulation over 8,192 elements.**

*Prediction:* a fraction of a percent.

*What happens:* **worse than that — the lab measures ~72% relative error at
$d = 8192$**, against $4.2 \times 10^{-6}$ for the same inputs with an FP32
accumulator. An error ratio of about 170,000×.

The failure is far worse than the naive $\sqrt{n}\epsilon$ estimate when the
values share a sign, because the accumulator grows monotonically until later
additions are smaller than its ulp and are rounded away entirely — *stagnation*.
Past that point the sum simply stops increasing.

Two things worth doing. Try values of alternating sign and the error drops
substantially: the error depends on the data, not only the format. And try
reducing **pairwise** instead of sequentially in the same low precision — the lab
measures three orders of magnitude better, because error grows as $\log n$ rather
than $n$. The reduction *order* matters as much as the accumulator's width, which
is why every real kernel reduces as a tree.

A trap for anyone checking this in PyTorch rather than in a kernel: `torch.mean`
and `torch.sum` already accumulate in FP32 internally even for a BF16 input, so
calling them twice with different input dtypes measures almost nothing. The
running total has to be held in the dtype under test.
