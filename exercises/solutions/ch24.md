## 24.1 🏗️ Decode three real JDs

A worked example on a composite posting, to show what the output looks like.

> *"Research Engineer, Post-Training. You will work on improving model capabilities through supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning. You will design and run experiments, build and maintain the data pipelines that support them, and collaborate with our evaluation team. Experience with distributed training and with modern preference-optimization methods expected. Familiarity with inference serving is a plus."*

**1. Chapters described.** 11 (SFT), 12 (reward modeling), 13–15 (RL), 22 (serving — the "plus"), 23 (evaluation), 6 (distributed training).

**2. Archetype.** B (Post-training Researcher), leaning toward Research Engineer — note "build and maintain the data pipelines," which is engineering work a pure researcher posting would not mention.

**3. What they are struggling with.**

> *"build and maintain the data pipelines that support them"*

A research posting that explicitly lists pipeline maintenance is telling you the team's researchers are currently spending their time on data plumbing. Either they lack dedicated post-training data engineers, or the pipelines are fragile.

Second signal:

> *"Familiarity with inference serving is a plus."*

Per §13.9, rollout generation is 60–80% of RLHF wall-clock. A team listing serving as a "plus" rather than a requirement has probably discovered this recently and is hoping to hire someone who already knows it.

**4. What they will test.**

- The SFT mixture design question ([Exercise 11.4](ch11.md)) and the masking-bug diagnosis ([Exercise 11.5](ch11.md)).
- Derive the DPO loss ([Exercise 14.1](ch14.md)); explain PPO vs GRPO and when each is right.
- Design an experiment to test whether a mixture change helped — and whether you volunteer confidence intervals ([Exercise 23.1](ch23.md)).
- Given the pipeline emphasis: how would you build a rejection-sampling loop, and what would break first.

**5. Honest percentage.** Yours to compute. §24.1's note: **roughly 60% is a reasonable application**, and this is more true at frontier labs than elsewhere because the roles are new enough that almost nobody has the full stack.

**The method to extract from this:** read the JD twice — once for what it asks for, once for what it *dwells on*. The second reading tells you what the team's current pain is, and naming that pain accurately in an interview is worth more than meeting one more requirement.

## 24.2 🏗️ The self-assessment

No solution — the answer is yours. Four notes on doing it honestly.

**On part 2 — scoring.** The common error is scoring by familiarity rather than by demonstrated capability. The test for ✅ is: *could I do this on a whiteboard, under mild pressure, without notes?* For anything you have only read about, the honest score is 🟡 at best.

**On part 3 — the categories.** Most people over-populate "requires a job that has one." From [Chapter 24 §24.7](../../book/part-5-the-job/24-translating-jds.md), the genuinely job-gated items are narrower than they look:

| Often assumed job-gated | Actually |
|---|---|
| "Experience with distributed training" | Learnable in six months — the arithmetic is Chapter 6, and multi-GPU practice is available on rented hardware |
| "Experience with large-scale data pipelines" | Learnable — the techniques are Chapter 3, and Common Crawl is public |
| "Experience with RLHF" | Learnable in six months — `trl` and Tülu 3 are open |
| "Experience training a frontier model" | **Genuinely job-gated.** Nobody has it before their first job at a place that has one |
| "Experience with 10,000-GPU clusters" | **Genuinely job-gated** |

Only the last two are truly gated, and §24.7 notes nobody expects them.

**On part 4 — substitutes.** For a genuinely job-gated item, the substitute is **demonstrated understanding of the model**, not a scaled-down version of the experience. You cannot run a 10,000-GPU job; you can size one on paper with the arithmetic shown ([Exercise 6.7](ch06.md)), and that is what the interview tests anyway.

**On part 5.** For most people the answer is **the arithmetic** ([Exercise 24.5](ch24.md)). It is upstream of pre-training, post-training, systems, and inference simultaneously, every archetype's interview contains it (§24.5), and it takes weeks rather than months.

## 24.3 🏗️ Answer the four interview questions

Worked examples of the *shape* of a good answer.

**1. The arithmetic question** — "How much memory does a 70B model need to train?"

A good answer decomposes before it computes:

> "Mixed precision with AdamW, so per parameter: 2 bytes for the BF16 working weights, 2 for BF16 gradients, and 12 for the FP32 master copy plus Adam's two moments — 16 bytes per parameter. For 70B that's 1,120 gigabytes of static state, so 14 H100s just for parameters, before any activations.
>
> And activations usually dominate: at 8K context and micro-batch 1 that's roughly 170 GB for a 70B model without recomputation, which is why the answer is never 'just get 14 GPUs.'"

What makes it good: the **breakdown** before the number, the **units carried**, and the **volunteered caveat** that activations dominate. The interviewer learns you have thought about it rather than memorized it.

**2. The design question** — "Train a 30B MoE on 512 H100s."

**Ask first.** From [Exercise 6.7](ch06.md) part 1:

> "Before I answer — what's the target context length at the end of training, and is there a long-context extension phase? What's the expert count and top-$k$? Is the interconnect InfiniBand or Ethernet, and is the allocation contiguous? Is FP8 validated on this stack, or is this the first run? And is the token budget fixed, or the wall-clock?"

Then the configuration, with the memory arithmetic shown, and **the sensitivity**: "if the interconnect is Ethernet rather than InfiniBand, I'd move traffic off DP onto PP and expect MFU to drop from ~35% to ~20%."

**Answering immediately scores worse than asking three questions first.** The incompleteness is deliberate.

**3. The debugging scenario** — "Throughput dropped 30% overnight, no code change."

Ordered by cost to test:

> 1. **Per-rank step-time histogram** (2 minutes). A synchronous collective runs at the pace of the slowest rank, so a 30% cluster-wide drop is far more likely to be one rank at 70% than all ranks at 70%.
> 2. If one rank: **`nvidia-smi -q` on that host** for clock throttling and throttle reasons (2 minutes). Thermal or power throttling is the most common cause.
> 3. **Link error and retransmit counters** on that node's NIC (5 minutes). A degraded optical link forces retransmits and slows every collective.
> 4. **Allocator reserved-vs-allocated over time** (5 minutes). Growing reserved with flat allocated is fragmentation, which builds over hours and matches 'overnight.'
> 5. **Data-loader latency per rank** (10 minutes). A filesystem degradation or a full disk shows up as ranks waiting for batches.
> 6. **Whether the scheduler moved the job** (5 minutes). A reallocation across a rack boundary costs exactly this kind of step change ([Exercise 7.2](ch07.md)).

What makes it good: **a procedure, ordered by cheapness**, with the reasoning for the ordering stated. [Chapter 7 §7.4](../../book/part-2-pretraining/07-cluster-reality.md), [Chapter 11 §11.12](../../book/part-3-post-training/11-sft-frontier-style.md), [Chapter 13 §13.7](../../book/part-3-post-training/13-ppo-rloo-frontier.md), and [Chapter 21 §21.11](../../book/part-4-infra/21-checkpointing-resumption.md) are effectively answer keys for this question type.

**4. The depth question.**

Pick the single thing you know best and write three levels:

> **L1:** "I implemented DPO from scratch and verified it against `trl`."
> **L2:** "Which parts had to match exactly?" → sum vs mean over token log-probabilities, prompt masking identical to SFT, and the off-by-one in the logit-to-token shift. Mine failed initially on the second.
> **L3:** "Why does DPO decrease the chosen response's log-probability?" → the loss constrains only the difference; pushing down on a concentrated target is easier than pushing up, so mass leaks to the rest of the distribution. Which is why you log `chosen_reward` and `rejected_reward` separately, not just the margin.

**Better to have one area with three levels than five areas with one.** "Familiar with" is dangerous phrasing precisely because it invites L2 and L3.

## 24.4 🏗️ Design a portfolio project

A worked example, for someone whose gap is "distributed training."

**1. Gap:** cannot demonstrate understanding of parallelism beyond having read about it.

**2. The project and its artifact.**

> **A parallelism memory-and-throughput model, validated against real measurements.**
>
> Build a calculator that takes a model config and a cluster config and predicts per-GPU memory and step time under any combination of TP/PP/DP/ZeRO/recomputation. Then **validate it**: rent 8 GPUs for a few hours, run a small model under six different configurations, and compare predictions to measurements.
>
> **Artifact:** a repo with the model, the validation script, and a write-up containing a table of predicted-versus-measured for each configuration, with the discrepancies explained.

Why this specific project: it produces the skill §24.5 says every interview tests (the arithmetic), it is *validated* rather than theoretical, and it costs perhaps $50 of rented compute.

**3. The expected honest failure.**

The predictions will be wrong, and specifically: **activation memory will be under-predicted** (real implementations keep more than the textbook accounting suggests), and **throughput will be over-predicted** (the model assumes perfect communication overlap, which does not happen).

That is the write-up's most valuable section: "my model predicted 42 GB and PyTorch used 51 GB; here is where the extra 9 GB went." Nobody can fake that paragraph.

**4. Where it goes.**

Repo plus a written post. The post is the artifact people read; the repo is what makes it credible.

**5. A concrete open-source contribution matching this gap.**

Look for issues labelled `good first issue` in **`torchtitan`** — it is small, readable, actively maintained, and directly about parallelism. Concretely useful contributions that recur there: adding a missing parallelism configuration to the memory estimator, extending documentation for a parallelism combination, or adding a test that catches a config that silently OOMs.

For other gaps: **`trl`** for post-training (DPO/GRPO variants, tests, documentation of loss variants), **`lm-evaluation-harness`** for evaluation (a new task, or fixing a harness inconsistency of exactly the kind [Exercise 23.2](ch23.md) is about), **vLLM** for serving (kernel work, scheduler behaviour, a benchmark), **FlashAttention** for kernels.

§24.6 ranks this highest because it is **directly comparable to the job and reviewable by the person hiring**. A merged PR in `trl` is stronger evidence of post-training competence than any project you host yourself.

## 24.5 🔢 The arithmetic drill set

**1. Memory to train a 70B dense model.**

16 bytes/param (2 BF16 weights + 2 BF16 grads + 4 FP32 master + 4 Adam $m$ + 4 Adam $v$):

$$70\times10^9 \times 16 = \mathbf{1{,}120 \text{ GB}}$$

**2. KV cache per token.**

$$2 \times 80 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 = \mathbf{327{,}680 \text{ bytes} = 320 \text{ KB/token}}$$

**3. RMSNorm arithmetic intensity.**

FLOPs $\approx 4Td = 4 \times 8192 \times 8192 = 2.68\times10^8$. Bytes: read + write $= 2 \times 8192 \times 8192 \times 2 = 2.68\times10^8$.

$$I = \mathbf{1.0 \text{ FLOP/byte}}$$

Against an H100 ridge point of ~295 → **severely memory-bound**, using about 0.3% of the machine's compute.

**4. Tokens/second for 15T tokens in 60 days.**

$$\frac{15\times10^{12}}{60 \times 86{,}400} = \frac{15\times10^{12}}{5.184\times10^6} = \mathbf{2.89\times10^6 \text{ tokens/s}}$$

**5. Time per output token, 70B BF16, batch 1, H100.**

$$\frac{70\times10^9 \times 2}{3.35\times10^{12}} = \mathbf{41.8 \text{ ms}}$$

Bounded by reading the weights, with the tensor cores 99.7% idle.

**6. Rejection-sampling GPU-hours.**

$$200{,}000 \times 8 \times 600 = 9.6\times10^8 \text{ tokens}$$
$$\frac{9.6\times10^8}{2{,}400} = 4\times10^5 \text{ GPU-s} = \mathbf{111 \text{ GPU-hours}}$$

(Plus ~5% for reward scoring → ~117.)

**7. Optimal checkpoint interval.**

$$\sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 10{,}800} = \sqrt{216{,}000} = 465 \text{ s} \approx \mathbf{7.8 \text{ minutes}}$$

**8. Chinchilla-optimal size for $10^{24}$ FLOPs.**

With $D = 20N$ and $C = 6ND = 120N^2$:

$$N = \sqrt{10^{24}/120} = \mathbf{9.1\times10^{10} \approx 91\text{B}}, \qquad D = \mathbf{1.82\text{T tokens}}$$

**If you got all eight without notes**, you are calibrated for §24.5's first interview question type across all four archetypes. If not, the ones you missed point at the chapter to reread — and doing them again in a week is worth more than reading that chapter again.

## 24.6 🏗️ The honest gap analysis

No solution. Four observations on the method.

**On part 1.** The question is deliberately "what would I be hired to do *tomorrow*," not "what am I interested in." The most common failure of a career plan is that it starts from the wrong place — someone who has shipped backend services and reads about MoE routing is a backend engineer who reads about MoE routing, and the plan should start there.

If your honest answer is not on [Chapter 2 §2.2](../../book/part-1-frontier/02-org-chart-and-roles.md)'s taxonomy at all, that is fine and informative: §25.3 says **lateral entry from distributed systems, HPC, or GPU programming is the most common successful path**, and none of those appear on the taxonomy either.

**On part 3.** The shortest credible path usually has an intermediate step, and people resist naming it. From §25.1's scarcity column, the intermediate is frequently one of the **hard-to-fill engineering roles** — data, systems, kernels, inference, evaluation, environments — rather than a direct move to the crowded research role.

**On part 4.** It should change your path, and this is §25.4's most actionable claim: **internal transitions are dramatically easier than external ones**, because colleagues have direct evidence of your judgement rather than a résumé. The implication is that "get into a lab in a scarce role, then move" beats "wait until qualified for the role I want."

**On part 5.** The most common honest answer is not a skill gap. It is **stopping** — the plan is 18 months long, nothing external enforces it, and month four has no deliverable.

The prevention, per [Chapter 25 §25.8](../../book/part-5-the-job/25-career-paths.md) and the learning-paths page: **ship the artifact.** Every path in this book ends with something you produce and publish, because completion rates for self-directed study with a public artifact are dramatically higher than without one — and the artifact is also the thing you show in the interview.
