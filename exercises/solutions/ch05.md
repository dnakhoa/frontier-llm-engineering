## 5.1 🔢 Count the parameters

$d = 8192$, 64 query heads × 128 = 8192 for Q; 8 KV heads × 128 = 1024 each for K and V.

**1. One attention block.**

| Matrix | Shape | Parameters |
|---|---|---|
| $W_Q$ | $8192 \times 8192$ | 67.1M |
| $W_K$ | $8192 \times 1024$ | 8.4M |
| $W_V$ | $8192 \times 1024$ | 8.4M |
| $W_O$ | $8192 \times 8192$ | 67.1M |
| **Total** | | **151.0M** |

Note how much GQA saves here beyond the cache: under MHA, $W_K$ and $W_V$ would be $8192 \times 8192$ each, making the block 268M — so GQA saves 44% of the attention parameters as well.

**2. One SwiGLU FFN.**

SwiGLU is $\text{SiLU}(xW_{\text{gate}}) \odot (xW_{\text{up}}) \cdot W_{\text{down}}$ — **three** matrices. The two-matrix assumption is the classic error here.

$$3 \times 8192 \times 28{,}672 = \mathbf{704.6\text{M}}$$

**3. All 80 layers.**

Per layer: $151.0 + 704.6 = 855.6$M, plus two RMSNorms at 8,192 parameters each (negligible, 16K).

$$80 \times 855.6\text{M} \approx \mathbf{68.4\text{B}}$$

**4. Plus embeddings.**

$$2 \times 128{,}256 \times 8192 = 2.10\text{B}$$
$$68.4 + 2.10 = \mathbf{70.5\text{B}}$$

Against the nominal 70B — close enough to confirm the arithmetic. (The small excess is rounding: "70B" is nominal. Llama 3 does **not** tie its embeddings — `tie_word_embeddings` is `false` — so both tables count.)

**5. Attention versus FFN.**

$$\text{attention} = \frac{151.0}{855.6} = \mathbf{17.6\%}, \qquad \text{FFN} = \mathbf{82.4\%}$$

**This is the number worth remembering.** In a modern transformer, roughly **80% of the parameters are in the FFN**, not attention. Attention gets all the conceptual attention; the FFN holds the model.

Two consequences that recur throughout the book: MoE replaces the *FFN* precisely because that is where the parameters are (§5.8); and tensor parallelism's communication cost is dominated by the FFN's two all-reduces per layer (§6.5).

## 5.2 🔢 KV cache across four variants

Per token: $2 \times L \times H_{kv} \times d_{\text{head}} \times 2$ bytes, except MLA which stores $2 d_c$ numbers per layer.

**1. Bytes per token.**

| Scheme | Calculation | Per token |
|---|---|---|
| MHA | $2 \times 80 \times 64 \times 128 \times 2$ | **2.62 MB** |
| GQA 8:1 | $2 \times 80 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2$ | **328 KB** |
| MQA | $2 \times 80 \times 1 \times 128 \times 2$ | **41 KB** |
| MLA | $80 \times 1024 \times 2$ | **164 KB** |

**2. One 128K sequence** (131,072 tokens):

| Scheme | Cache |
|---|---|
| MHA | **343 GB** |
| GQA 8:1 | **43 GB** |
| MQA | **5.4 GB** |
| MLA | **21 GB** |

**3. 8K sequences in 10 GB of free HBM per GPU.**

Cache per 8,192-token sequence, and how many fit in 10 GB:

| Scheme | Per sequence | Fit in 10 GB |
|---|---|---|
| MHA | 21.5 GB | **0** — one sequence does not fit |
| GQA 8:1 | 2.69 GB | **3** |
| MQA | 336 MB | **29** |
| MLA | 1.34 GB | **7** |

**4. Quality ranking, and why GQA won.**

Quality, best to worst: **MHA ≈ MLA > GQA > MQA.**

MHA is the reference. MQA — a single shared KV head — is a large reduction in the attention mechanism's expressiveness, and it measurably degrades quality, particularly on tasks requiring the heads to attend to different things.

**Why GQA rather than MQA:** GQA is the interpolation, and the empirical finding [\[20\]](../../book/appendix/b-references.md#20-gqa) is that most of MQA's memory saving arrives long before most of its quality cost. At 8:1 you get 8× the reduction with quality close to MHA; pushing to 64:1 (MQA) gets you another 8× on memory and pays for it in quality. The curve is sharply diminishing on one axis and sharply worsening on the other, and 8:1 sits near the knee.

**Why MLA is interesting:** it achieves better cache economy than GQA 8:1 (164 KB versus 328 KB per token) while — per the DeepSeek reports — retaining quality closer to MHA, because it compresses rather than discards. It is a genuinely different move: GQA *removes* heads, MLA *projects* them into a shared latent. The cost is a more complex attention implementation and a harder kernel story.

## 5.3 🔢 The MoE bargain

**1. One expert.** SwiGLU, three matrices, $d = 7168$, $d_{\text{ff}} = 2048$:

$$3 \times 7168 \times 2048 = \mathbf{44.0\text{M parameters}}$$

**2. All routed experts.**

$$256 \text{ experts} \times 44.0\text{M} \times 60 \text{ layers} = \mathbf{676\text{B}}$$

Which is already the whole model's nominal 671B — so essentially **100% of the parameter count is in the routed experts**, with attention, the shared expert, and embeddings accounting for the remainder within rounding. (The chapter's figures are approximate; the point stands.)

This is §5.1 part 5's finding taken to its conclusion: the FFN was 82% of a dense model, and MoE turns that 82% into ~100% by multiplying it 256-fold.

**3. Active expert parameters per token.**

Top-8 routing plus 1 shared expert = 9 experts per token per layer:

$$9 \times 44.0\text{M} \times 60 = \mathbf{23.8\text{B}}$$

Plus attention: 60 layers of MLA at roughly 100–150M each, call it ~7B, plus embeddings ~1.9B, plus the MTP head. That lands in the neighbourhood of **33–37B**, consistent with the reported 37B active. Good.

**4. Training FLOPs versus a dense 671B.**

$$\frac{6 \times 37\times10^9 \times D}{6 \times 671\times10^9 \times D} = \frac{37}{671} = \mathbf{5.5\%}$$

An **18× reduction in training compute** for the same parameter count.

**5. Training memory versus a dense 671B.**

Every parameter needs weights, gradients, and optimizer state, whether or not it was active this token. So:

$$\frac{671}{671} = \mathbf{1.0\times — identical}$$

Both need ~10.7 TB of static state (Ex 1.3).

**6. The MoE bargain in one sentence.**

**MoE buys you the quality of a very large model at the compute cost of a small one, and you pay for it in memory, communication, and complexity.**

The three costs are worth naming because they are where MoE actually hurts: the full parameter set must be resident (so expert parallelism, §6.8), routing requires an all-to-all on the critical path of every token (§6.11), and load imbalance turns a synchronous collective into a straggler problem (§5.7).

## 5.4 🔢 Fine-grained versus coarse

> **Corrected in v1.0.1.** Earlier editions gave Qwen3's expert width as 12,288 and concluded that Qwen3's experts were the largest of the three. The expert width is 1,536, and the conclusion reverses. See [errata E-050](../../book/appendix/d-errata.md#e-050--qwen3s-expert-width-and-exercise-54).

**1. Which width is the expert width.** Per layer, attention is $4096 \times (64 + 64 + 4 + 4) \times 128 \approx 71.3$M, and the untied embeddings are $2 \times 151{,}936 \times 4096 = 1.24$B.

| Candidate | Per expert ($3 d\, d_{\text{ff}}$) | Active per layer (top-8) | Active over 94 layers, plus attention and embeddings |
|---|---|---|---|
| `moe_intermediate_size` = 1,536 | $3 \times 4096 \times 1536 = 18.9$M | $8 \times 18.9 = 151$M | $94 \times (151 + 71.3)\text{M} + 1.24\text{B} = \mathbf{22.1\text{B}}$ |
| `intermediate_size` = 12,288 | $3 \times 4096 \times 12288 = 151$M | $8 \times 151 = 1{,}208$M | $94 \times (1208 + 71.3)\text{M} + 1.24\text{B} \approx 121\text{B}$ |

Only 1,536 reproduces the published 22B active (and, with all 128 experts, the 235B total). 12,288 would make "A22B" a 121B-active model. The field names were a trap: `intermediate_size` sizes a *dense* FFN layer, and since `mlp_only_layers` is empty and `decoder_sparse_step` is 1, this model has none ([fact sheet](../../book/appendix/fact-sheets/qwen3.md#architecture-qwen3-235b-a22b)). The general lesson: when a config has two plausible fields, check each against a published total before you build on it.

**2. Active expert parameters per token per layer.**

| Model | Experts | $d$ | $d_{\text{ff}}$ | top-$k$ | Per expert | Active |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DeepSeek-V3 | 256 (+1 shared) | 7,168 | 2,048 | 8 | 44.0M | $9 \times 44.0 = \mathbf{396\text{M}}$ |
| Qwen3-235B | 128 | 4,096 | 1,536 | 8 | 18.9M | $8 \times 18.9 = \mathbf{151\text{M}}$ |
| Mixtral 8×7B | 8 | 4,096 | 14,336 | 2 | 176M | $2 \times 176 = \mathbf{352\text{M}}$ |

**3. Distinct expert combinations.**

$$\binom{256}{8} \approx 4.1\times10^{14}, \qquad \binom{128}{8} \approx 1.4\times10^{12}, \qquad \binom{8}{2} = 28$$

DeepSeek-V3 has roughly **13 orders of magnitude more** routing configurations available per token than Mixtral, and Qwen3 roughly 11.

**4. Why Qwen3 is fine-grained at 1,536.** $8 \times 1536 = 12{,}288$, exactly the config's dense `intermediate_size`, and $12{,}288 = 3d$ (our arithmetic). The eight experts a token uses add up to one ordinary dense FFN. That is what fine-grained expert segmentation does, and the Qwen3 report says it follows it ([fact sheet](../../book/appendix/fact-sheets/qwen3.md#architecture-qwen3-235b-a22b)): cut each FFN into smaller experts and route to proportionally more of them. The active compute per token stays at about one dense FFN. What multiplies is the number of experts and so the number of combinations, from 28 for Mixtral to about $10^{12}$ for Qwen3.

Expert width as a fraction of $d$:

| Model | $d_{\text{ff}} / d$ |
|---|---|
| DeepSeek-V3 | $2048 / 7168 = 0.29$ |
| Qwen3-235B | $1536 / 4096 = 0.375$ |
| Mixtral 8×7B | $14336 / 4096 = 3.5$ |

From fine to coarse: DeepSeek-V3, Qwen3, Mixtral. Qwen3 sits next to V3, with experts about a ninth of Mixtral's relative size. It is not the coarse counterexample that the wrong width made it look like. The real contrast among the three is Mixtral against the other two.

**5. The argument for fine-grained.**

Combinatorial specialization. With 28 possible combinations, Mixtral's experts must each be a generalist over a large slice of the input distribution. With $4\times10^{14}$ (V3) or $1.4\times10^{12}$ (Qwen3), fine-grained experts can specialize narrowly and the *combination* provides the coverage. The DeepSeekMoE paper's argument is essentially that expert specialization is what MoE is for, and coarse experts cannot specialize.

Fine-grained also improves load balance statistically: with 256 experts, one over-subscribed expert absorbs a smaller share of the total, so the straggler effect is bounded lower.

**The argument against.**

Each expert is small ($d_{\text{ff}} = 2048$ in V3 and 1,536 in Qwen3, against Mixtral's 14,336), which means each expert's matmuls are small, which means **poor GPU utilization**. A $7168 \times 2048$ GEMM on a handful of tokens is far below the size where tensor cores are efficient. Fine-grained MoE turns one large efficient matmul into many small inefficient ones, and this is a real cost that the parameter arithmetic hides.

It also makes the routing metadata larger and the all-to-all pattern more scattered.

**6. Cost in the all-to-all.**

The all-to-all volume is driven by the *tokens dispatched*, which is $k$ per token regardless of expert count. So raw bytes are similar for equal $k$.

What changes is the **scatter**: with 256 experts spread across many devices, each token's 8 destinations are more likely to be on 8 *different* devices, so the communication pattern is finer-grained and less able to be coalesced into large contiguous transfers. Small scattered messages are exactly what interconnects handle worst.

This is precisely the problem DualPipe [\[50\]](../../book/appendix/b-references.md#50-dualpipe-deepseek-github) exists to hide — overlapping the all-to-all with computation so its latency does not sit on the critical path. Fine-grained MoE and communication-overlap engineering are the same design decision seen from two ends.

## 5.5 🔢 RoPE and the frequency budget

RoPE dimension pair $i$ (of $d/2$ pairs) rotates at frequency $\omega_i = \theta^{-2i/d}$, giving wavelength $\lambda_i = 2\pi / \omega_i = 2\pi \theta^{2i/d}$.

**1. Slowest pair, $d = 128$, $\theta = 10{,}000$.**

The slowest is the last pair, $2i/d \to 1$:

$$\lambda_{\max} = 2\pi \times 10{,}000 \approx \mathbf{62{,}832 \text{ tokens}}$$

**2. With $\theta = 500{,}000$.**

$$\lambda_{\max} = 2\pi \times 500{,}000 \approx \mathbf{3{,}141{,}593 \text{ tokens}}$$

**3. Why raising $\theta$ helps extrapolation.**

The model learns to interpret rotation *angles*. During training at context $L$, each dimension pair only ever experiences angles up to $2\pi L / \lambda_i$.

- For a **fast** pair (short wavelength), the angle wraps many times within $L$ — the model sees the full $[0, 2\pi)$ range and learns it properly.
- For a **slow** pair (long wavelength), the angle only reaches a small fraction of a full rotation within $L$. The model has never seen larger angles for that dimension.

At inference beyond $L$, the fast pairs are fine — they are just wrapping again, in-distribution. The slow pairs enter angles never observed, and that is where extrapolation fails.

Raising $\theta$ stretches all wavelengths, so **more of the angle range each dimension will ever encounter has been seen during training**. It buys extrapolation headroom by making the training-time angles more representative of inference-time angles.

**4. Which pairs are out of distribution at 32K, trained at 8K with $\theta = 10{,}000$?**

A pair is safely in-distribution if its wavelength is short enough that the full angle range was covered during training: $\lambda_i \lesssim L = 8{,}192$.

$$2\pi \times 10{,}000^{2i/128} \le 8{,}192 \;\Rightarrow\; 10{,}000^{2i/128} \le 1{,}304 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{2i}{128} \le \frac{\ln 1304}{\ln 10^4} = \frac{7.17}{9.21} = 0.779$$

So $i \le 49.8$ — roughly **the first 50 of 64 pairs are fine**, and **the slowest ~14 pairs are out of distribution** at 32K.

That is the concrete target for the extension methods in §9.10: Position Interpolation compresses positions so the slow pairs stay within their trained angular range; NTK-aware scaling adjusts $\theta$ to stretch the slow wavelengths while leaving fast ones alone; YaRN does this per-dimension with a smooth interpolation between the two regimes. All three are treating exactly these 14 dimensions.

## 5.6 🏗️ Design a 30B MoE

A defensible design, served at high volume at 32K.

**1. Total and active.** **30B total, ~3.5B active.** The brief says *high-volume serving*, which makes inference cost dominant. A high sparsity ratio (~8.5:1) gives the quality of a much larger model at the serving compute of a small one. The memory cost is 30B of weights, which fits comfortably on one 80 GB GPU in BF16 — deliberately, so a serving replica is one GPU and no tensor parallelism is needed for capacity.

**2. Layers and hidden dimension.** **40 layers, $d = 2560$.**

The aspect-ratio argument: $d / L = 64$, which is in the conventional range (Llama-3-70B is $8192/80 = 102$; Qwen3-235B is $4096/94 = 44$). Deeper-and-narrower generally trains slightly better per parameter, and costs more in pipeline depth and in kernel efficiency (narrow matmuls). At 30B, 40 layers keeps pipeline parallelism unnecessary for serving, which is the point.

**3. Attention variant.** **GQA, 20 query heads, 4 KV heads, head dim 128.**

The arithmetic for *this* config, at 32K:

$$2 \times 40 \times 4 \times 128 \times 2 = 81{,}920 \text{ bytes} = 80 \text{ KB/token}$$
$$80 \text{ KB} \times 32{,}768 = 2.6 \text{ GB per sequence}$$

With 30B of BF16 weights (60 GB) on an 80 GB card, ~18 GB remains for cache, so **~7 concurrent 32K sequences per GPU**. That is workable. Under MHA (20 KV heads) it would be 400 KB/token and 13 GB per sequence — one sequence per GPU, which is not a product.

MLA would be better still and costs a harder kernel story; at 30B with 32K context, GQA is sufficient and I would not spend the complexity budget there.

**4. Experts.** **64 routed experts, top-6, plus 1 shared expert. Expert $d_{\text{ff}} = 1408$.**

Leaning fine-grained per §5.4, but not to DeepSeek's extreme — with only 30B total, 256 experts would make each one tiny enough that GPU utilization suffers badly. 64 gives $\binom{64}{6} \approx 7\times10^7$ combinations, ample specialization, while keeping each expert's matmuls a usable size. The shared expert absorbs the common-case computation so the routed experts can specialize harder.

**5. Normalization, activation, position encoding.** RMSNorm (pre-norm), SwiGLU, RoPE with $\theta = 500{,}000$.

**Which of these deserves any thought: only the RoPE base**, and only because of §5.5 — at 32K native context, $\theta$ needs to be large enough that the slow dimension pairs are in distribution. The other two are settled: every competitive model since 2023 uses RMSNorm and SwiGLU, the ablations have been run, and spending time re-deriving that is spending time badly.

Being able to say "these three are settled, here is the one that is not" is the actual skill the question tests.

**6. The ablation.** **The sparsity ratio** — 30B/3.5B versus 30B/6B versus 30B/2B, at 3B scale, evaluated on quality *and* measured serving throughput.

Why this one: it is the decision with the largest effect on both quality and serving cost, the two things the brief cares about, and it is the one where my prior is weakest. Expert count and top-$k$ can be tuned later within a fixed active-parameter budget; the sparsity ratio is architectural.

## 5.7 🔢 Load balance

**1. Perfect balance.** 8,192 tokens × top-2 = 16,384 routed assignments across 64 experts:

$$\frac{16{,}384}{64} = \mathbf{256 \text{ tokens per expert}}$$

**2. Per GPU.** 8 experts per GPU × 256 = **2,048 tokens per GPU**.

**3. One expert at 12% instead of 1.6%.**

That expert receives $0.12 \times 16{,}384 = 1{,}966$ tokens instead of 256 — **7.7× its share**.

Its GPU now processes $1{,}966 + 7 \times 256 = 3{,}758$ tokens instead of 2,048, or **1.83× the balanced load**.

Because the all-to-all is a **synchronous collective, every other GPU waits for the slowest.** So the MoE layer's step time is set by that one GPU: roughly **83% longer** than the balanced case, for every layer, for every step.

That is the whole reason load balancing exists. A single hot expert does not cost you that expert's throughput — it costs the entire cluster's throughput, multiplied across all 60 layers.

**4. Auxiliary-loss-free balancing.**

**The mechanism:** maintain a per-expert **bias term** added to the router's logits before top-$k$ selection. After each step, decrease the bias of over-subscribed experts and increase the bias of under-subscribed ones, by a small fixed amount. The bias affects *which* experts are selected but is not part of the gradient path for the routing weights.

**What it avoids that an auxiliary loss does not:**

An auxiliary balance loss adds a term to the objective that is **not the thing you want the model to learn**. It fights the main loss: the router wants to send a token to the expert that handles it best, and the auxiliary loss pushes it elsewhere for the sake of balance. That is a direct tax on quality, and its weight is a hyperparameter with no principled setting — too low and you get imbalance, too high and you get quality loss, and the right value drifts during training.

The bias approach achieves balance **without touching the loss at all.** The gradient signal remains purely "route to the expert that helps," and balance is enforced by a separate control loop. DeepSeek's report is explicit that this improves quality relative to the auxiliary-loss approach at equal balance, and it is one of V3's genuinely novel contributions.

The general shape is worth noting: **when a constraint conflicts with your objective, prefer enforcing it outside the objective rather than adding a competing term.** The same instinct appears in §12.11's gating of safety versus helpfulness rather than summing them.

## 5.8 📄 Diff two configs

**1 & 2.** The meaningful differences and their category:

| Field | Llama-3-70B | Qwen3-235B-A22B | Category |
|---|---|---|---|
| Architecture | Dense | MoE (128 experts, top-8) | Training cost + serving cost |
| Layers | 80 | 94 | Quality (depth) |
| Hidden size | 8,192 | 4,096 | Quality + serving cost |
| KV heads | 8 | 4 | **Serving cost** |
| $d_{\text{ff}}$ | 28,672 | 1,536 per expert (`moe_intermediate_size`; see [5.4](#54--fine-grained-versus-coarse)) | Training cost |
| Vocabulary (`vocab_size`) | 128,256 | 151,936 ([tokenizer sheet](../../book/appendix/fact-sheets/tokenizers.md#qwen3)) | Quality (multilingual) + serving cost |
| RoPE $\theta$ | 500,000 | 1,000,000 | Quality (long context) |

**3. What each depth/width choice optimizes for.**

**Llama-3-70B (80 × 8192, ratio 102):** wide and comparatively shallow. Wide layers give large, efficient matmuls — better tensor-core utilization and better MFU. Fewer layers means less pipeline depth, so pipeline parallelism has fewer stages and smaller bubbles (§6.6). This is a configuration optimized for **training throughput and dense-serving simplicity.**

**Qwen3-235B (94 × 4096, ratio 44):** deep and narrow. More layers gives more sequential computation, which the literature broadly finds helps quality per parameter. Narrow layers are individually less efficient, but in an MoE the FFN work is spread across experts anyway, so the width penalty applies mainly to attention. This is a configuration optimized for **quality per active parameter** — which is what you want when active parameters set your serving cost.

Both are defensible; they are answers to different questions.

**4. Which to serve at 128K.**

**Qwen3-235B**, on the KV cache arithmetic. Per token:

- Llama-3-70B: $2 \times 80 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 = 328$ KB → **43 GB** at 128K
- Qwen3-235B: $2 \times 94 \times 4 \times 128 \times 2 = 193$ KB → **25 GB** at 128K

Qwen3's cache is 41% smaller despite having more layers, because it halved the KV heads. And its *active* parameters are 22B versus 70B, so the per-token decode compute is ~3× lower.

The catch, and it must be stated: Qwen3-235B needs **all 235B parameters resident** (470 GB in BF16), so a serving replica is at least 6–8 GPUs regardless of how few are active. Llama-3-70B fits on 2. So the honest answer is: **Qwen3 wins on marginal cost per token, Llama-3 wins on minimum viable deployment.** Which one you prefer depends entirely on your traffic volume — at high volume the marginal cost dominates and Qwen3 wins; at low volume you are paying for idle GPUs and Llama-3 wins.

This is the same total-versus-active trade from §5.3, appearing as a serving decision.

## 5.9 🧪 Break the MoE lab

**1. Auxiliary loss weight → 0.**

*Prediction people make:* balance degrades gradually.

*What happens:* imbalance develops steadily and **does not reach full collapse in
the lab** — a distinction the lab is explicit about, and worth keeping.

With no balancing the lab measures a Gini coefficient around 0.36 and a busiest
expert at ~2.7× its fair share. Real imbalance, and enough to produce the
straggler tax in §5.7. But the `dead` column stays at zero: **you do not see
experts receiving exactly nothing.**

The mechanism is a positive feedback loop, and it is real: an expert receiving
slightly more tokens gets more gradient, becomes slightly better, so the router
prefers it more. Nothing in the objective opposes it. At frontier scale that
loop runs to completion and produces the textbook picture — a few tall spikes and
a floor of permanently unselected experts, which are then dead weight.

What a CPU-scale lab cannot supply is the number of steps and the model capacity
for the loop to close. So read the lab as showing the *direction and the
mechanism*, not the endpoint. Overclaiming here would be easy and the lab
declines to: it says in its own header that it does not reach the fully-collapsed
state.

**2. Top-2 → top-1.**

*Prediction:* balance improves, since fewer assignments.

*What happens:* **balance gets worse and collapse gets faster.** With top-2, a token that would have gone to a collapsing expert still contributes gradient to a second expert, which keeps alternatives alive. Top-1 removes that hedge entirely — it is winner-take-all per token, which is exactly the dynamic that drives the feedback loop in part 1.

Quality also drops, because the model loses the ability to combine two experts' outputs. Switch Transformer used top-1 with careful capacity factors and auxiliary losses; the field largely moved to top-2 and above for these reasons.

**3. Bias-based balancing with auxiliary loss off.**

*Prediction:* partial recovery.

*What happens:* **balance recovers well** — the routing histogram flattens substantially. The control loop directly counteracts the feedback loop from part 1: an expert pulling ahead gets its bias decreased, which reduces its selection probability, which stops the runaway.

The instructive part is watching *how*: balance oscillates before settling, because the bias update is a proportional controller with no damping. The bias update rate is the tuning knob, and setting it too high produces visible oscillation in the load histogram.

**4. 8 → 64 experts, active parameters fixed.**

*Prediction people make:* load variance stays about the same, since it is the same routing mechanism.

*What happens:* the **relative** variance of expert load *increases* — each expert receives fewer tokens on average, so Poisson-like counting noise is proportionally larger ($\sigma/\mu \propto 1/\sqrt{\mu}$).

But the **impact of imbalance decreases**, which is the point of §5.4's argument for fine-grained experts. With 8 experts, one expert at 3× its share absorbs 37% of all tokens. With 64 experts, one expert at 3× its share absorbs only 4.7%. The straggler effect from §5.7 part 3 is bounded far lower.

So fine-grained routing is *noisier per expert* and *more robust in aggregate* — which is the correct and slightly counterintuitive conclusion, and the reason frontier MoE models trend toward more, smaller experts.

## 5.10 🧪 Verify the KV cache arithmetic

The two discrepancies most people find, and why:

**Measured memory is higher than calculated.** The cache is allocated in blocks or with padding to alignment boundaries, and PyTorch's caching allocator does not return memory to the OS. Measure the tensor sizes, not `nvidia-smi`.

**MLA's measured cache does not match the naive formula.** MLA stores the compressed latent *plus* a small decoupled RoPE component per token (in DeepSeek-V3, $d_c = 512$ latent plus $d_{\text{rope}} = 64$). If you computed $2 d_c$ and got a number below the measurement, the missing piece is the RoPE part — which cannot be compressed, because the rotation must be applied at the original head dimension.

That detail is a good illustration of why MLA is a harder kernel problem than GQA: the attention computation has to handle two differently-shaped pieces of cache per token.
