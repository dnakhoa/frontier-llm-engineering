## 12.1 🔢 The agreement ceiling

**1. Is 74% good?**

**Yes — it is essentially at the ceiling.** Human annotators agree with each other 75% of the time, which means 25% of the labelled pairs have no consistent "correct" answer. A model predicting the labels perfectly, in the sense of predicting the *modal* human judgement, would still be scored wrong on a large share of those disputed pairs.

74% against a 75% human-human baseline is a reward model that has extracted approximately all the consistent signal in the data.

**2. 84%: one good explanation and one bad.**

**Good:** the colleague improved the *data* — better annotation guidelines, better annotator training, filtering out low-agreement pairs, or collecting preference-strength ratings and dropping the ties. Any of these raises the ceiling itself by removing noise, and 84% against a raised ceiling is a genuine improvement.

**Bad:** the model learned an **artifact** that correlates with annotator behaviour but not with quality. The canonical one is length (§12.3) — if annotators mildly prefer longer responses and the reward model learns to be a length detector, it can exceed the *semantic* agreement rate by predicting a surface feature more reliably than humans predict each other's judgements.

**3. How to distinguish, with data you already have.**

Three checks, all cheap:

- **Stratify by preference strength** (§12.2). A genuine improvement raises accuracy most in the *middle* strata — the hard-but-decidable pairs. An artifact raises it uniformly, including in the "negligibly better" stratum where there is nothing real to learn.
- **Measure the length correlation.** If it rose alongside the accuracy, you have your answer.
- **Run best-of-$n$** (§12.4). A genuine improvement produces a curve that keeps rising; an artifact produces one that turns over.

**4. What a perfect reward model would score.**

**About 75%** — the human-human agreement rate.

The reasoning: if 25% of pairs are genuine coin-flips among humans, then whatever the "true" preference is, the recorded label disagrees with it roughly half the time on those pairs. A model that always predicts the true preference is scored wrong on ~12.5% of them.

More precisely: on the 75% of pairs where humans agree, a perfect model scores 100%. On the 25% where they do not, it scores ~50% by construction. Total: $0.75 + 0.25 \times 0.5 = \mathbf{87.5\%}$ if "agreement" means two random annotators agree.

The exact number depends on how the ceiling is defined, and the operationally important point stands regardless: **there is a ceiling well below 100%, it is set by your annotation process rather than your model, and an accuracy substantially above it is a warning sign rather than a triumph.**

## 12.2 🔢 Stratify the accuracy

**1. Overall accuracy.**

$$0.20 \times 0.91 + 0.30 \times 0.82 + 0.35 \times 0.68 + 0.15 \times 0.53 = 0.182 + 0.246 + 0.238 + 0.080 = \mathbf{74.6\%}$$

**2. Which stratum worries you.**

**"Significantly better" at 91% should worry you.** These are the pairs where the difference is large and obvious — where human annotators would agree at very high rates. A reward model getting 9% of them wrong is failing on the easy cases, and those failures are the ones that will produce visibly bad behaviour during RL: the policy will find responses that the RM scores well and a human would immediately reject.

**"Negligibly better" at 53% should not worry you.** That is chance, and chance is the *correct* answer — the annotator said the two responses were essentially equivalent. A model scoring 90% on this stratum would be learning something that is not preference.

This inversion of the naive reading is the point of the exercise. Look at the strata where there is a real signal and check performance there; the low-signal strata should look like noise.

**3. Which model is better: 74.6% stratified, or 78% uniform?**

**The stratified one (Model A), despite the lower overall score.**

A model at exactly 78% in every stratum is not distinguishing easy pairs from hard ones. That is the signature of a model predicting something *other than* preference strength — most likely a surface feature that is equally predictive regardless of how large the quality gap is. Length is the obvious candidate: if the chosen response is longer in 78% of pairs across all strata, a length detector scores exactly this pattern.

Model A's profile — high on clear pairs, chance on ties — is what a model that actually tracks quality looks like.

**The general principle: the *shape* of a reward model's accuracy across difficulty is more informative than its aggregate.** An aggregate can be reached many ways and only some of them are good.

**4. What the "negligibly better" row tells you about the ceiling.**

15% of your pairs carry **no signal at all**. They contribute noise to training and they drag the achievable overall accuracy down by construction — a perfect model scores ~50% on them, so they cap overall accuracy at $0.85 \times (\text{accuracy on the rest}) + 0.075$.

**The actionable consequence:** drop them, or down-weight them. Training on coin flips teaches the model that near-identical responses have a preference ordering, which is false, and it wastes 15% of your annotation budget on labels that mean nothing.

Better still, this is exactly what the **margin term** in Llama 2's loss ([Chapter 12 §12.3](../../book/part-3-post-training/12-reward-modeling.md)) is for: demand a large reward gap for "significantly better" pairs and essentially no gap for near-ties, so the loss stops pushing on pairs that carry no information.

## 12.3 🔢 Length bias, quantified

**1. Variance explained.**

Spearman $\rho = 0.62$; squaring gives $\rho^2 \approx \mathbf{0.38}$ — roughly **38% of the rank variance** in reward scores is accounted for by length alone.

(Strictly, $\rho^2$ for Spearman is the variance explained in the *ranks*, not the raw values; the distinction does not change the interpretation.)

**2. How much of the reward gain is length.**

Length grew from 280 to 520 tokens, an increase of 240. To convert that into reward units you need the slope of reward against length, which the correlation alone does not give — you need the standard deviations.

A working estimate: if held-out response lengths have $\sigma_{\text{len}} \approx 200$ tokens and reward has $\sigma_r = 1.0$, then the regression slope is $\rho \cdot \sigma_r / \sigma_{\text{len}} = 0.62 / 200 = 0.0031$ reward per token.

$$240 \text{ tokens} \times 0.0031 = \mathbf{0.74 \text{ reward units}}$$

Against a total gain of 0.8.

**Roughly 90% of the measured reward improvement is explained by the length increase alone.** That is the number to put in front of whoever is celebrating the reward curve.

The caveat worth stating: this assumes the length-reward relationship measured on held-out data holds on-policy during RL, and it may not — the policy is deliberately moving into the region where the RM scores highly. If anything that makes the estimate conservative.

**3. A control that isolates quality from length.**

**Length-matched pairwise evaluation.** Concretely:

- Sample responses from the pre-RL and post-RL policies on the same prompts.
- **Bin by response length** and compare only within bins — post-RL responses of 300–350 tokens against pre-RL responses of 300–350 tokens.
- Have an independent judge (human, or a model that is not the reward model) do the comparison.
- Report the win rate *per length bin* and the overall length-matched win rate.

If the post-RL model wins within every length bin, the improvement is real. If it wins overall but not within bins, you have measured length inflation.

A cheaper approximation: **cap generation length** at the pre-RL mean and re-evaluate. If the gain vanishes, it was length.

**4. Three mitigations, ranked by cost elsewhere.**

1. **Balance the training pairs on length** — cheapest. Ensure the chosen response is not systematically longer across your preference set. Costs you some data (you discard or reweight unbalanced pairs) and it addresses the *bias* in the RM without constraining the policy. **Do this first; it is nearly free.**

2. **Break ties toward shorter responses during rejection sampling** — cheap, and per [Exercise 11.4](ch11.md) part 5 it is the single highest-leverage intervention on length. Costs essentially nothing.

3. **Explicit length penalty in the reward** — most costly. It is blunt: it penalizes responses that are legitimately long (a detailed technical explanation, a long code block) exactly as much as padded ones. You are correcting a bias in the measurement by adding a bias to the objective, which trades one distortion for another. Use it when the first two are insufficient, and prefer a penalty that is conditional on task type over a global one.

## 12.4 🏗️ Best-of-$n$ as a diagnostic

**1. Which to ship.**

**RM-A**, clearly, despite RM-B being better at $n \le 16$.

**2. What is happening to RM-B.**

**Overoptimization** (§12.6), visible without running any RL.

At small $n$, both models rank a handful of typical samples and RM-B does it slightly better. As $n$ grows, you are sampling further into the tail of the policy's distribution — and the tail is where the reward model's exploitable artifacts live. Beyond $n \approx 16$, best-of-$n$ starts selecting responses that RM-B scores highly *because of an artifact*, and an independent judge sees them for what they are.

RM-A's curve keeps rising, which means its errors are not systematically exploitable by sampling harder.

**3. PPO prediction.**

**RM-B fails first, and dramatically.**

Best-of-$n$ is a very weak optimizer — it searches over $n$ independent samples from a fixed policy. PPO is a strong one: it moves the policy itself toward high-reward regions, over thousands of steps, with gradients pointing directly at whatever the reward model rewards.

If $n = 64$ is enough to find RM-B's artifacts, PPO will find them within a few hundred steps and then exploit them relentlessly. Expect: reward climbing smoothly, KL growing, independent evaluation flat or falling, and generations that look strange in a specific repeated way.

RM-A will also eventually overoptimize — every learned reward model does — but its peak is further out, so you have more KL budget before you reach it.

**4. Why this test is cheaper and more informative.**

**Cheaper:** best-of-$n$ requires only generation and scoring — no training, no value network, no four-model memory footprint (§13.5), no hyperparameters. Generating 256 samples for a few hundred prompts is a batch inference job of minutes to hours.

Running PPO to find the same information means a full RLHF setup, days of compute, and a run you then have to diagnose.

**More informative:** the best-of-$n$ curve gives you the *shape* — you learn not just that the RM overoptimizes but roughly *when*. A failed PPO run tells you it failed, and disentangling "the reward model was bad" from "the KL coefficient was wrong" from "the value function diverged" is genuinely hard (§13.7).

This is why §12.7 calls it the single most informative reward-model diagnostic. It should be run before any RL, on every reward model.

**5. Where to stop trusting RM-B, and the KL implication.**

RM-B's curve peaks between $n = 16$ and $n = 64$. **Stop trusting it beyond $n \approx 16$.**

The connection to KL: best-of-$n$ has a known relationship to KL divergence from the base policy — selecting the best of $n$ samples induces a KL of approximately $\log n - \frac{n-1}{n}$ nats. At $n = 16$:

$$D_{KL} \approx \log 16 - \frac{15}{16} = 2.77 - 0.94 \approx \mathbf{1.8 \text{ nats}}$$

So RM-B is trustworthy only up to roughly **1.8 nats** of divergence from the SFT policy. For RM-A, the curve is still rising at $n = 256$, giving $\log 256 - 1 \approx 4.5$ nats and probably more.

**That converts a cheap offline diagnostic directly into a KL budget for your RL run** (§13.6) — which is a genuinely useful trick, and it is how you set a KL target from evidence rather than by copying a number from a paper.

## 12.5 🏗️ Design the preference pipeline

**1. Where the pairs come from.**

**On-policy, from the current model**, as the default: sample two responses to the same prompt at temperature, from the checkpoint you intend to improve.

The argument (§12.4, and §14.5 makes the same point for DPO): the reward model will be evaluated during RL on responses drawn from the policy's distribution. A reward model trained on some other model's outputs is being asked to extrapolate, and extrapolation is exactly where its artifacts live.

**When off-policy is acceptable:** for bootstrapping a first reward model when you have no policy worth sampling from, and for injecting deliberately-diverse responses (a much weaker model's output, a deliberately-flawed response) to give the RM coverage of the low-quality region it would otherwise never see. Keep this a minority of the data.

**2. Who labels, by category.**

| Category | Labeller | Share of that category |
|---|---|---|
| Math with a checkable answer | **Verifier** | 100% |
| Code with tests | **Verifier** (execution) | 100% |
| Format/constraint compliance | **Verifier** (programmatic) | 100% |
| General helpfulness, tone, style | **Judge model** for volume, **humans** for a calibration subset | 85% / 15% |
| Safety and refusal boundaries | **Humans**, with a judge model for volume on clear cases | 60% / 40% |
| Culturally-specific or multilingual | **Native-speaker humans** | 100% |

The principle from §12.9: **use a verifier wherever one exists, a judge for volume where none does, and humans for the cases where the judge shares the policy's blind spots** (§17.8) — which is precisely safety and cultural judgement.

**3. What the interface collects beyond the binary choice.**

- **Preference strength** on a 4-point scale (significantly / better / slightly / negligibly). This drives the margin term and lets you stratify (§12.2).
- **A "both bad" and a "both good" option**, distinct from a tie. These identify prompts where your policy is uniformly failing — valuable for the SFT mixture even though they are useless as preference pairs.
- **A reason category** when a response is rejected (factually wrong / unsafe / off-format / unhelpful / too long). Cheap to collect and it lets you build category-specific reward models later.
- **Time spent.** A pair labelled in three seconds is worth less than one labelled in ninety.
- **Annotator ID**, so you can compute per-annotator agreement and detect drift.

**4. Measuring and improving agreement.**

**Measure:** overlap a fixed fraction of pairs — 5–10% — across multiple annotators, and compute pairwise agreement and Krippendorff's alpha continuously, not once at the start. Report it per annotator and per category.

**Improve**, in order of effect:

- **Sharpen the guidelines on the specific cases that disagree.** Pull the lowest-agreement pairs, look at them together, and write the rule that would have resolved them. This is the single highest-return activity and it is where §17.1's argument for *explicit written criteria* comes from — a guideline is a proto-constitution.
- **Calibration sessions**: annotators label the same set, then discuss disagreements.
- **Remove the coin flips.** Per §12.2 part 4, allowing "negligibly different" as an answer stops forcing noise into the data.
- **Retire or retrain annotators** persistently below the pool's agreement rate.

Recall §12.4's framing: annotator agreement sets the ceiling on everything downstream. Moving it from 65% to 78% is worth more than most modelling work, and it is achieved here.

**5. The refresh loop.**

```
1. Sample from the current policy on a fixed prompt distribution.
2. Score with the current reward model AND with a judge/human subset.
3. Measure RM accuracy on this fresh, on-policy data.
4. If accuracy has dropped materially below its value on training-distribution
   data -> the RM is stale.
5. Label a new batch on-policy, retrain the RM (one epoch, from the current
   SFT checkpoint), rerun the best-of-n diagnostic (12.4), resume RL.
```

**How you decide it is time — three triggers, any of which fires:**

- **RM accuracy on fresh on-policy samples** falls more than ~5 points below its held-out accuracy on training-distribution data (§12.7's distribution-shift check).
- **KL from the reference exceeds the budget** derived in §12.4 part 5.
- **Independent evaluation flattens** while reward keeps climbing — the §12.6 signature.

Llama 2 ran five such iterations, which is a reasonable expectation for a production cycle. The important structural point: **this loop is the mechanism, not an optimization.** A reward model is a snapshot of preferences on a distribution that RL is actively moving away from, so staleness is guaranteed by design and the only question is how often you refresh.

## 12.6 🔢 The last-token bug

**1. What it returns with right-padding.**

The hidden state at the **final padding position** — a position whose input token is `<pad>` and which, depending on the attention mask, may have attended to nothing meaningful. The score is read from a representation that has nothing to do with the response.

**2. Why the loss still looks plausible.**

Because the pad token's hidden state is not *random* — it is a deterministic function of the model's parameters and, under a causal mask without proper masking of pads, of the preceding real tokens. So it does carry *some* signal, and gradient descent will happily exploit whatever signal it carries.

The Bradley-Terry loss only needs $r(y_w) > r(y_l)$; if the pad-position representation correlates at all with response quality — and it will, weakly, via the sequence length and whatever leaked through — the loss decreases and accuracy climbs above 50%.

You get a model with a normal-looking loss curve and, say, 62% accuracy where it should have 74%. Nothing errors. Nobody notices unless they look.

**3. What you would notice at inference.**

- **Scores that depend on padding.** The same response scored alone versus in a batch with a longer sequence gets *different* rewards. This is the diagnostic tell: reward should be a function of $(x, y)$ only.
- **Compressed reward variance** — the scores cluster, because the pad representation varies less than a real final-token representation.
- **During RL: nonsense.** The policy optimizes against a signal that is partly about sequence length and padding, so it produces responses that are strange in a way that does not map to any human notion of quality.
- **A near-zero best-of-$n$ curve** (§12.4) — the model barely beats random selection.

**4. The correct version.**

```python
rewards = self.score(hidden).squeeze(-1)          # (B, T)
last_index = attention_mask.sum(dim=1) - 1        # index of the last real token
return rewards[torch.arange(rewards.size(0)), last_index]
```

With left-padding, `[:, -1]` happens to be correct — which is why this bug is intermittent across codebases and why it survives review.

**5. The one-minute test.**

**Score the same response twice, once alone and once padded to a longer length, and assert the results are equal.**

```python
r1 = reward_model(ids, mask)                        # unpadded
r2 = reward_model(pad_to(ids, 512), pad_mask(mask)) # same content, padded
assert torch.allclose(r1, r2, atol=1e-4)
```

This is the reward-model equivalent of [Chapter 11 §11.4](../../book/part-3-post-training/11-sft-frontier-style.md)'s "decode your labels" — a trivial invariant check that catches an entire class of silent failure. **Reward must be invariant to padding.** If it is not, you have this bug.

## 12.7 🏗️ Two objectives, one model

**1. The case for one reward model.**

- **Simplicity.** One model to train, evaluate, serve, and refresh. In the RL loop it is one forward pass rather than two.
- **It learns the trade-off from data.** Rather than you imposing an exchange rate between safety and helpfulness, the annotators' judgements on mixed pairs encode where the boundary sits in practice.
- **No combination rule to get wrong**, and no risk of the two models disagreeing in a way that produces incoherent gradients.
- **Fewer labels needed** in total, since every pair informs one model.

**2. The case for two.**

- **The objectives genuinely conflict**, and Bradley-Terry forces all preferences onto a single scalar axis (§12.2). Pushing "most helpful" and "safest" onto one axis means the annotators resolve the conflict individually and inconsistently, which shows up as *noise* — and noise lowers the agreement ceiling (§12.1).
- **Each model trains on internally coherent preferences**, so agreement within each labelling task is higher and the learned signal is cleaner.
- **The trade-off becomes explicit, tunable, and auditable** rather than emergent from your annotation pool's average disposition.
- **You can change the balance without recollecting preferences** — a large practical advantage when policy changes.
- **Different labellers.** Safety labelling wants trained specialists and possibly legal review; helpfulness labelling wants volume. Splitting the models lets you split the pipelines.

**3. The combination rule, and why a weighted sum is poor.**

**Why a weighted sum fails:**

$$r = w_h \cdot r_{\text{help}} + w_s \cdot r_{\text{safe}}$$

This lets a **very high helpfulness score buy off a safety violation.** A response that is extraordinarily useful and mildly unsafe scores the same as one that is moderately useful and perfectly safe. RL will find this — it is a linear trade and policy gradient is very good at finding linear trades — and the result is a model that becomes maximally helpful in exactly the regions where it is least safe.

The deeper problem: a weighted sum encodes safety as a **term to be traded**, when the design intent is that it is a **constraint**.

**A better rule — gating:**

$$r = \begin{cases} r_{\text{safe}} & \text{if } r_{\text{safe}} < \tau \\ r_{\text{help}} + \lambda \cdot \min(r_{\text{safe}} - \tau,\ 0) & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

Below the safety threshold, the reward is *only* the safety score — helpfulness cannot compensate at all. Above it, helpfulness drives the reward.

**4. Llama 2's argument, reproduced.**

Llama 2 used the safety reward model's score to decide *which* reward to apply: when the safety model indicates a problem, the safety score is the reward; otherwise the helpfulness score is.

The argument: **safety is a constraint on the feasible set, not a term in the objective.** You do not want a model that trades a little safety for a lot of helpfulness at the margin — you want a model that is helpful *subject to* being safe. Constrained optimization and penalized optimization give different answers, and only the first matches the intent.

The practical evidence in favour: under a weighted sum, the policy's most-rewarded responses cluster near the safety boundary, because that is where the helpfulness gradient is largest and still affordable. Under gating, there is no gradient pulling toward the boundary at all.

**5. "10% less cautious."**

**Under one reward model:** you cannot do it directly. The safety-helpfulness trade is baked into the learned scalar. Your options are to recollect preference data with different guidelines and retrain (weeks), or to adjust the KL coefficient and hope (which changes everything, not just caution). **This is the strongest practical argument for two models.**

**Under two reward models:** you change **$\tau$**, the safety threshold, and re-run RL — or, if you built it that way, change it at inference time without retraining at all. It is a one-line change to a number that someone can review, argue about, and revert.

And note what "10% less cautious" even means becomes answerable: you can measure the over-refusal rate as a function of $\tau$, show product the curve, and let them pick a point. Under one model there is no such curve to show.

## 12.8 📄 Read the overoptimization paper

**1. The setup.**

The core difficulty in studying overoptimization is that you need to know the *true* reward to see the proxy diverging from it, and the true reward is exactly what you do not have.

Their solution: **designate a large reward model as the "gold" reward by fiat.** Use it to generate synthetic preference labels. Train a *proxy* reward model on those synthetic labels. Then optimize a policy against the proxy while measuring the gold.

This makes the ground truth knowable by construction. It is a synthetic setting, and the price is that "gold" is itself a reward model rather than human judgement — which the authors are clear about, and which does not undermine the structural finding.

**2. Functional forms and the $\sqrt{D_{KL}}$ parameterization.**

They fit, with $d = \sqrt{D_{KL}}$:

$$R_{\text{bo}n}(d) = d(\alpha_{\text{bo}n} - \beta_{\text{bo}n} d), \qquad R_{\text{RL}}(d) = d(\alpha_{\text{RL}} - \beta_{\text{RL}} \log d)$$

**The significance of the $\sqrt{D_{KL}}$ axis:** it makes the relationship clean and roughly linear-plus-correction, which is what lets you *fit* and therefore *extrapolate*. Plotted against raw KL the curves are messier.

More practically: it says the gold reward degrades as a function of a *distance* measure rather than of training steps or of proxy-reward gain. Two runs that reach the same KL have spent the same amount of the budget, regardless of how many steps or what learning rate got them there. **KL is the currency.**

The difference in form between best-of-$n$ and RL matters too: best-of-$n$'s quadratic term makes it turn over more sharply, while RL's $d\log d$ decays more slowly — but RL reaches much higher $d$ in practice.

**3. How the peak moves.**

- **With reward-model size:** larger reward models overoptimize *later* and reach *higher* gold reward before turning. More parameters means a better approximation of the gold, so there is less exploitable gap. The improvement is smooth and predictable rather than a phase change.
- **With preference-data quantity:** more data likewise pushes the peak out. The proxy is a better estimate, so the divergence begins further along.

Both scale in a regular enough way that the paper fits scaling laws for them — which is the paper's title claim.

**4. Operational implications.**

- **Set a KL budget, do not chase reward.** The proxy reward will rise past the peak; it carries no information about whether you should stop.
- **Measure with something independent, periodically, during the run.** This is the only signal that can tell you where the peak is. It is why §12.13 and §13.12 both argue for organizational separation of evaluation.
- **Use the best-of-$n$ curve to estimate the budget in advance** (§12.4 part 5) — it is the cheap offline proxy for the expensive online question.
- **If you can afford a bigger reward model, buy peak position with it.** The paper says that is what the extra capacity purchases.
- **Do not interpret a plateau in gold reward as convergence.** Past the peak it is not a plateau, it is a decline you have not measured.

## 12.9 🧪 Break the reward-model lab

**1. Systematically longer chosen responses.**

*Prediction:* moderate correlation, maybe 0.3.

*What happens:* **very high — typically 0.7–0.9.** Length is an extremely easy feature to extract and it perfectly predicts the label in your training data, so gradient descent finds it almost immediately. Accuracy on the training distribution will look excellent.

The lesson: a reward model will learn the *easiest* feature that predicts the label, and length is nearly always the easiest available.

**2. Balance the pairs and retrain.**

*Prediction:* correlation goes to zero.

*What happens:* it drops substantially — perhaps to 0.2–0.35 — **and does not reach zero.**

Why not: because length is *genuinely* correlated with quality in the underlying data. A thorough answer really is often longer than a dismissive one. Balancing removes the *artifactual* correlation introduced by your sampling; it cannot remove the real one, and it should not.

This is the important nuance for §12.3 part 4: the goal is not zero length correlation, it is *no more length correlation than is warranted*. A reward model with $\rho = 0$ on data where length genuinely predicts quality has been over-corrected.

**3. Five epochs instead of one.**

*Prediction:* accuracy improves then plateaus.

*What happens — and the two metrics diverge, which is the point:*

- **Held-out accuracy** rises for the first epoch, then **flattens or declines**.
- **The reward gap** — mean $r(y_w) - r(y_l)$ on training data — **keeps growing**, steadily and impressively.

That divergence is the signature of overfitting in a reward model: it is becoming more *confident*, not more *correct*. The model has begun memorizing which specific responses were chosen rather than learning what makes a response better.

And an overfit reward model is worse than an undertrained one for RL, because its confident wrong regions are exactly what the policy will find.

**4. The best-of-$n$ curve.**

Generate it and find the turnover. On a small model trained on limited preference data, expect the turn somewhere between $n = 8$ and $n = 64$ — much earlier than a production reward model, because the artifacts are easier to find.

The thing to do with the number: convert it to a KL budget via $\log n - (n-1)/n$ (§12.4 part 5) and note how small it is. That is a visceral demonstration of why RLHF runs are short.

**5. Reward from the first token.**

*Prediction:* the loss stays flat at $\log 2 \approx 0.693$, since the first token carries no information about the response.

*What happens:* **it depends on which "first token" you take, and both answers are
instructive.**

Take the first token of the *response* and the loss decreases, with accuracy
rising above chance — often to 55–60%. Under a causal mask that position has seen
the prompt and the opening word, and the opening word correlates with style,
which correlates with the annotator's preference. Enough signal to train on,
nowhere near enough to be a reward model.

Take position 0 of the *sequence* — which is what the lab does — and the loss
never leaves $\log 2 = 0.6931$ and accuracy sits at exactly 50.0%. Position 0
attends only to itself, so there is nothing to read at all.

This is the same failure as §12.6's last-token bug, in a form that makes the mechanism obvious: **a reward model will train to a decreasing loss on almost any input signal, however inadequate.** A decreasing loss is not evidence that you are reading the right position, and the padding-invariance test in §12.6 part 5 is what actually checks it.
