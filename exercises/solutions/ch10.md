## 10.1 🏗️ Reconstruct the run

The reference version, against which to score yourself:

**1. Model.** 671B total parameters, 37B active per token. 61 layers (the first 3 dense), hidden dimension 7,168. **MLA** attention: KV latent $d_c = 512$, query latent $d_c' = 1536$, 128 heads of dimension 128, plus a decoupled RoPE component. **DeepSeekMoE**: 256 routed experts + 1 shared expert, top-8 routing, expert $d_{\text{ff}} = 2048$, with **auxiliary-loss-free** bias-based load balancing. **MTP**: depth 1, a sequential module of one Transformer block (14B parameters), loss weight 0.3 then 0.1. A small complementary sequence-wise balance loss accompanies the bias-based balancer.

**2. Data.** 14.8T tokens. Mixture heavily English and Chinese, with code, math, and multilingual. Custom deduplication and quality filtering, PDF extraction for academic content. Vocabulary ~129K.

**3. Parallelism.** 2,048 H800s. **16-way pipeline parallelism**, **64-way expert parallelism across 8 nodes**, ZeRO-1 data parallelism. **No tensor parallelism** — see §6.9 and the [fact sheet](../../book/appendix/fact-sheets/deepseek-v3.md#parallelism-layout). DualPipe schedule overlapping the MoE all-to-all with computation.

**4. Precision.** **FP8** for the bulk of the GEMMs: E4M3 for forward and weight gradients, E5M2 for activation gradients, with fine-grained (tile-wise and block-wise) scaling. **BF16** retained for embeddings, the output head, normalization, and other numerically sensitive operations. **FP32** master weights, optimizer moments, and promoted accumulation at intervals to work around the limited FP8 tensor-core accumulate precision.

**5. Optimizer and schedule.** AdamW. Warmup, then a multi-stage schedule with the LR decayed toward the end.

**6. Context.** Trained at **4K**, extended to **32K then 128K via YaRN** in a separate mid-training phase (Chapter 9's pattern).

**7. Cost.** 2,788K H800 GPU-hours, ~56.7 days on 2,048 GPUs, reported at ~$5.5M — covering **the final training run only**, explicitly excluding prior research, ablations, and data costs ([Exercise 1.7](../ch01.md)).

**Scoring.** If you produced 5 of 7, Part II worked. The two most commonly missed: the *absence* of tensor parallelism (item 3), and the fact that training was at 4K with context extended afterwards (item 6) — both of which are exactly the details that distinguish reading a report from understanding one.

## 10.2 🔢 Which choice bought the most

**1. Estimated training-cost multiple saved by each.**

**MoE (fine-grained, 256 experts, top-8): ~18×.**
From [Exercise 5.3](../ch05.md): $6ND$ with $N$ = 37B active versus 671B dense gives $37/671 = 5.5\%$. This is by far the largest single factor, and it is not close.

**FP8: ~1.6–2×.**
FP8 GEMMs run at roughly double BF16 throughput on this hardware, and halve the activation memory. In practice not the full 2× because only the bulk GEMMs are FP8 and the overhead of fine-grained scaling and promoted accumulation eats some of it. Call it 1.6× realized.

**DualPipe: ~1.2–1.5×.**
Hard to price precisely because it is a throughput-recovery mechanism rather than a work reduction. Without it, the §6.6 all-to-all (~900 GB/step/rank) sits exposed on the critical path. If that would otherwise stall the pipeline 20–35% of the time, DualPipe is worth that back.

**MLA: ~1.0× for training, large for serving.**
MLA slightly *reduces* attention compute versus MHA and slightly complicates the kernel. Its training-cost contribution is close to neutral. See part 3.

**2. Which two, if removed, change the story most.**

**MoE and FP8.** Remove MoE and the run costs ~18× more — the $5.5M figure becomes ~$100M and the entire "frontier model on a modest budget" narrative disappears. Remove FP8 and it roughly doubles.

Remove DualPipe and the run is meaningfully slower but the story survives. Remove MLA and training cost barely moves.

**3. Which is about serving, and how the ranking changes.**

**MLA is a serving decision** ([Exercise 1.4](../ch01.md)): it cuts the KV cache by ~93% versus MHA, which is what makes a 671B model economically servable at long context at all.

**How the ranking changes under lifetime cost:** dramatically. A model that is cheap to train and impossible to serve has no value. Using [Exercise 8.1](../ch08.md)'s framing — where inference FLOPs over a model's life can rival or exceed training FLOPs — and noting that KV cache is what limits *concurrency* rather than FLOPs:

- **MoE stays first**, because it reduces both training *and* per-token inference compute (37B active, not 671B).
- **MLA moves to second**, ahead of FP8, because without it the serving concurrency per GPU is roughly 15× worse and the model's deployment cost is dominated by cache-limited batch sizes.
- FP8 and DualPipe are training-side only.

The general point: **the four choices are not comparable on one axis.** Two of them (MoE, MLA) shape the model's whole economic life; two (FP8, DualPipe) are training-run optimizations.

**4. Least likely to transfer.**

**DualPipe**, and its associated custom all-to-all kernels. Per §6.9, these were shaped specifically by the H800's reduced inter-GPU bandwidth — the routing was constrained to a bounded number of nodes, and the SM budget was split between communication and computation to fit that network.

On hardware with full NVLink bandwidth, the all-to-all is less of a bottleneck, so the elaborate overlap machinery buys less. It is excellent engineering that is partly a workaround for a constraint most labs do not have.

FP8 is the most transferable of the four — the technique is hardware-general (any FP8-capable accelerator), and their fine-grained scaling insight applies everywhere.

## 10.3 🔢 Price the alternative

**1. Training FLOPs for a dense 400B on 14.8T tokens.**

$$6 \times 4\times10^{11} \times 1.48\times10^{13} = \mathbf{3.55\times10^{25} \text{ FLOPs}}$$

(Compare V3's $3.29\times10^{24}$ from [Exercise 1.2](../ch01.md) — about **11× more**.)

**2. GPU-hours and cost.**

At H100 BF16 990 TFLOP/s and 45% MFU (achievable for a well-tuned dense model):

$$\frac{3.55\times10^{25}}{990\times10^{12} \times 0.45} = 7.97\times10^{10} \text{ GPU-s} = \mathbf{22.1\text{M GPU-hours}}$$

At $2.50/H100-hour: **≈ $55M**.

**3. Ratio to V3.**

$$\frac{22.1\text{M}}{2.79\text{M}} = \mathbf{7.9\times \text{ the GPU-hours}}, \qquad \frac{\$55\text{M}}{\$5.5\text{M}} = \mathbf{10\times \text{ the cost}}$$

**4. How much is MoE versus FP8.**

- **MoE:** the 400B-dense-versus-37B-active compute ratio is $400/37 = 10.8\times$.
- **FP8:** worth ~1.6×, but note the dense baseline was computed at 45% MFU versus V3's effective ~33% at BF16-equivalent ([Exercise 1.2](../ch01.md) part 4) — MoE runs at lower MFU because of the all-to-all and small expert GEMMs.

Net: the 7.9× GPU-hour ratio decomposes roughly as **10.8× from sparsity, ×1.6 from FP8, ÷2.2 from the MFU penalty of running an MoE**. The MoE efficiency tax is real and it eats a meaningful share of the sparsity win — which is a number rarely stated and worth carrying.

**5. Is the resulting model better?**

**Unanswerable from this arithmetic, and that is the honest answer.**

What you would need to know:

- **The quality-per-active-parameter relationship for MoE.** A 671B/37B MoE is not equivalent to a 37B dense model — it is somewhere between 37B and 671B in capability, and where exactly is contested and task-dependent. If it behaves like a 150B dense model, V3 wins enormously. If it behaves like a 60B, the dense 400B is better.
- **Whether the dense 400B was trained Chinchilla-appropriately.** 14.8T tokens on 400B is 37 tokens/parameter — past Chinchilla, sensible for a served model.
- **The evaluation used**, and whether it is contaminated (§23.5).
- **Serving cost**, which per part 3 may dominate the comparison entirely.

The reason to insist on this: the tempting conclusion is "V3 got the same model for a tenth the price," and the report's benchmark comparisons support something like that. But benchmark parity is not capability parity, and a comparison across labs, architectures, and evaluation harnesses (§23.4) carries large error bars that the cost arithmetic does not.

## 10.4 🏗️ The auxiliary-loss-free scheme

**1. The standard auxiliary loss.**

For $E$ experts and a batch of $T$ tokens, let $f_i$ be the fraction of tokens routed to expert $i$ and $P_i$ the mean routing probability assigned to expert $i$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} = \alpha \cdot E \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{E} f_i P_i$$

Minimized when both are uniform. It is added to the language-modelling loss with weight $\alpha$.

**What it does to the objective:** it introduces a term that is **not the thing you want the model to learn.** The router wants to send each token to the expert that handles it best; the auxiliary loss pushes it elsewhere for balance. These are in direct tension, and $\alpha$ is the exchange rate between quality and balance — with no principled value, and a good value that drifts over training.

**2. The bias-based alternative, precisely.**

Maintain a per-expert bias vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^E$. At routing time, select the top-$k$ experts by the *biased* score:

$$\text{selected} = \text{top-}k\big(s_i + b_i\big)$$

but compute the **gating weights from the unbiased scores** $s_i$, so the bias affects *which* experts are chosen and not *how much* each contributes.

After each step, for each expert $i$, compare its observed load $f_i$ against the target $1/E$:

$$b_i \leftarrow b_i + \gamma \cdot \text{sign}\!\left(\frac{1}{E} - f_i\right)$$

with $\gamma$ a small fixed update rate. Over-loaded experts get their bias decreased; under-loaded experts get it increased.

**3. Why the bias is not part of the gradient.**

Because it is a **control mechanism, not a learned parameter**. Making it differentiable would put balance back into the objective and reintroduce exactly the tension in part 1.

Keeping it outside the gradient means the language-modelling loss remains pure — every gradient signal reaching the router says "route to the expert that lowers the loss," and nothing says "route somewhere else for fairness." Balance is enforced by a separate feedback loop operating on the *selection* rather than on the *objective*.

**4. What the bias approach fails to do.**

- **It does not balance within a step.** The auxiliary loss shapes the gradient so the model *learns* to route evenly. The bias only reacts to imbalance after observing it, so there is always a lag, and a rapidly-shifting routing distribution can outrun the controller.
- **It provides no smooth pressure toward balance in the learned function.** The router's underlying preferences can become arbitrarily skewed; the bias merely masks it at selection time. If you removed the bias at inference, the imbalance would reappear.
- **It has its own hyperparameter**, $\gamma$, and a poorly-chosen one oscillates (as [Exercise 5.9](../ch05.md) part 3 shows empirically).

So the claim is not "no hyperparameter" — it is "a hyperparameter that controls a *control loop* rather than the *objective*," which is a much more forgiving thing to mis-set.

**5. The experiment, and what would falsify it.**

**Design:** train three otherwise-identical MoE models at a scale where you can afford several runs (say 3B active / 30B total, 200B tokens):

- **A:** auxiliary loss, $\alpha$ tuned by a small sweep.
- **B:** bias-based, $\gamma$ tuned by a small sweep.
- **C:** no balancing at all (the control, to confirm balancing is needed).

**Measure:** final language-modelling loss on held-out data; expert load distribution (max/mean ratio) over training; downstream benchmarks; and — importantly — **loss at matched balance**, i.e. compare A and B at configurations where the max/mean ratios are equal.

That last measurement is the crux. The claim is "equal balance, better loss," so comparing A and B at *different* balance levels proves nothing.

**What would falsify it:** if at matched load balance the auxiliary-loss model achieves equal or lower held-out loss, the claim fails. Also falsifying: if the bias approach only achieves matched balance at a $\gamma$ that makes training unstable, or if its advantage vanishes at larger scale (which is the version worth worrying about, since the ablations affordable at 3B may not predict 671B).

## 10.5 🔢 Multi-token prediction

**1. Mechanically.**

In addition to predicting token $t+1$ from position $t$, the model also predicts token $t+2$ (and potentially further) via an MTP module that takes the main model's hidden state at position $t$ plus the embedding of the true token $t+1$, runs them through one Transformer block, and predicts with the shared output head.

The loss becomes

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{next-token}} + \lambda \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{MTP}}$$

with $\lambda = 0.3$ for V3's first 10T tokens and 0.1 for the rest. Crucially, V3's MTP predicts sequentially with a causal chain rather than predicting all future tokens independently from the same state, which preserves the causal structure.

**2. Overhead.**

The MTP module is one full V3 Transformer block, and V3's blocks are MoE blocks. The $12d^2$ rule of thumb is for a *dense* block and misses this. The block's 256 routed experts alone are $256 \times 3 \times 7168 \times 2048 \approx 11.3$B parameters; attention, the shared expert and the combining projection add a few hundred million. The released checkpoint settles it: **685B total = 671B main model + 14B MTP**, about 2% of the model ([fact sheet](../../book/appendix/fact-sheets/deepseek-v3.md#architecture)).

Compute overhead is small, because the MTP block is sparse like the others. Each position activates the attention plus 9 experts of one extra layer, roughly $1/61$ of the forward/backward FLOPs, plus the output projection, which is not free at a 129K vocabulary. Call it a few percent of step time. The parameters are large; the FLOPs are not.

**3. Two benefits.**

- **Training:** a **denser training signal**. Each position now contributes gradient about two future tokens rather than one, and predicting further ahead forces the representation at position $t$ to encode information relevant beyond the immediate next token. This is the claimed quality benefit and it is why $\lambda > 0$ at all.
- **Inference:** the MTP head is a **built-in draft model for speculative decoding** (§22.7). It predicts token $t+2$ cheaply, and the main model verifies. This gives speculative decoding without training or serving a separate draft model — the draft is already aligned with the target by construction.

**4. Why 0.3 rather than 1.0.**

Because next-token prediction is the actual objective and two-ahead prediction is a **regularizer/auxiliary signal**. At $\lambda = 1.0$ you would be spending equal optimization pressure on a task that is inherently harder (predicting two ahead is strictly more uncertain) and that is not what the model is evaluated on.

**Expected at $\lambda = 1.0$:** the model allocates capacity to the harder secondary task, and next-token perplexity degrades. The representation at each position becomes a compromise between "what predicts $t+1$ best" and "what predicts $t+2$ best," and since $t+2$ prediction has an irreducibly higher loss floor, its gradients are persistently larger — so an equal weight is effectively an over-weight.

The general pattern (and the same reasoning as §15.7's format-bonus warning): **an auxiliary objective should be weighted well below the primary one, because the primary one is what you are actually optimizing for.**

**5. When the MTP heads are kept.**

Under **speculative decoding** (§22.7). For ordinary autoregressive generation the module is discarded — you only need $t+1$.

Kept, the head drafts a candidate for $t+2$ which the main model verifies in the same forward pass that produces $t+1$'s successor, yielding an exact-distribution speedup at low batch size. The reason this is attractive relative to a separate draft model: the draft is trained jointly with the target on the same data, so its acceptance rate is high (DeepSeek report 85–90% for the second token, and 1.8× tokens per second), and it costs one 14B block rather than a separately trained draft model.

## 10.6 📄 Reading for absence

**1 & 2. Six things missing, and why.**

| Missing | Likely reason |
|---|---|
| **The exact data mixture proportions** — token counts per source and language | **Commercial.** This is the moat (§19.8). |
| **The quality filtering and dedup criteria** — thresholds, classifiers, training data for those classifiers | **Commercial.** Same as above, and arguably the single most valuable withheld item. |
| **Full hyperparameters** — every LR value, the exact schedule shape, batch ramp, $\gamma$ for the bias controller | **Practical + commercial.** Some is space; the balance-controller settings are genuinely useful to a competitor. |
| **The ablations** — what else was tried, at what scale, with what results | **Practical.** Reports have length limits, and negative results are rarely published. This is the biggest gap for a reproducer and the least likely to be deliberate secrecy. |
| **Failed runs and instabilities** — divergences, rollbacks, recipe changes mid-run | **Practical + reputational.** Almost nobody publishes this. R1's "unsuccessful attempts" section (§19.7) is a rare exception and it is exactly why that report is more valuable. |
| **Cost breakdown by phase** — how much went to pre-training versus mid-training versus post-training versus ablations | **Commercial + reputational**, given how much attention the $5.5M figure received. |

**3. Where the advantage lies.**

The pattern is unambiguous: **the architecture and systems work are published in detail; the data pipeline is not.** MLA, DeepSeekMoE, the bias controller, FP8 scaling, DualPipe — all described well enough to reimplement. The corpus, the filters, and the mixture — not at all.

That is the answer to "where is the moat": **the data.** Which is consistent with §19.8's finding for R1 and with §3.6's for Llama 3. Three independent reports, same pattern.

**4. Versus Llama 3.**

Llama 3 withholds a similar set — the mixture proportions are given at a coarse level, the quality classifier is described but not released, the ablations are partial.

**What is different:** Llama 3 releases **weights**, and it publishes considerably more about **evaluation and decontamination** (§3.6, §23.5). DeepSeek publishes more about **systems and architecture**.

**What that says about their positions:** Meta's differentiation is distribution and ecosystem — releasing weights is the strategy, so architecture secrecy buys little, and being trusted on evaluation matters. DeepSeek's differentiation at the time was demonstrating frontier capability at low cost, so the systems achievements *are* the story and publishing them establishes the claim.

**The transferable skill:** a lab publishes what supports its position and withholds what constitutes its advantage. Reading which is which tells you what the lab believes its advantage to be — often more reliably than anything the lab says directly.

## 10.7 🏗️ What would you change

A defensible plan.

**1. Change and keep.**

**Keep:** MoE with fine-grained experts (the 18× is not negotiable). MLA (serving economics, part 10.2.3). FP8. Bias-based balancing. No tensor parallelism.

**Change:**

- **Train at 8K natively rather than 4K.** V3 trained at 4K and extended. At 8K the §9.1 crossover is far away so attention is still cheap, and a longer native context gives the extension phase less work and better retention (§9.6 case 4).
- **More data, better filtered.** [Exercise 3.1](../ch03.md) showed a filtered crawl has ~10× more tokens than a 15T run needs. Spend more of the budget on the data pipeline and less on nothing.
- **Adopt WSD instead of a fixed schedule** ([Exercise 9.4](../ch09.md) part 5), specifically to enable annealing branches for specialist variants.
- **Higher MTP depth** (2 heads rather than 1), given the speculative-decoding payoff at serving time.
- **Budget explicitly for long-context data in the annealing mix**, so the extension phase is not fighting a model that has only ever seen short sequences.

**2. Expected gain and risk per change.**

| Change | Expected gain | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| 8K native | Better long-context retention; less extension work | ~10% more training compute at 8K vs 4K |
| Better data | The largest single quality lever available (§3.1) | Costs engineering time, not compute; delays the run start |
| WSD | Cheap specialist variants; no fixed-length commitment | Small quality gap vs a well-tuned cosine |
| MTP depth 2 | Higher speculative acceptance rate | More overhead; the second head's signal is weaker |
| Long-context anneal data | Better effective context (§9.5) | Displaces other annealing data |

**3. What I would not change despite suspecting suboptimality.**

**The DualPipe schedule and the custom all-to-all kernels.** I suspect a simpler schedule would be nearly as good on better hardware — but on H800s specifically, this machinery is load-bearing (§10.2 part 4), it is validated at this exact scale, and replacing it is weeks of kernel work with a real chance of a throughput regression discovered mid-run.

The general principle: **on a two-month, multi-million-dollar run, do not replace the validated component that is currently working in order to chase a suspected 10%.** Change the things with large upside and bounded downside; leave the load-bearing systems alone.

**4. Highest-variance decision.**

**The data pipeline changes.** Everything else has a bounded, predictable effect — 8K native costs a known 10% more compute, WSD costs a known small quality delta.

Data is different: better filtering could be worth more than every other change combined, or a filter bug could silently degrade the corpus in a way nobody notices until the model is finished (§3.5's filter false positives, §7.4's silent failures). It is the highest-upside and highest-downside decision in the plan.

**Mitigation:** validate the new pipeline by training a 3B model on both the old and new corpora before committing the full run. That is a few thousand GPU-hours against a 2.8M GPU-hour run — a 0.1% insurance premium on the riskiest decision.

## 10.8 🔢 Sanity-check the report

**1. Parameters versus architecture.**

From [Exercise 5.3](../ch05.md): with the first 3 of 61 layers dense, routed experts give $256 \times 44.0\text{M} \times 58 = 654$B, against a stated 671B total.

That leaves **~17B for everything else**: attention (~11.4B), the dense FFNs (~1.2B), the shared experts (~2.6B) and the embeddings (~1.9B). It adds up to within rounding. The check passes because you used the architecture the report actually describes. With 60 MoE layers you would get 676B, *more* than the whole model, and that is itself a sign an input is wrong.

*Correction in v1.0.1:* earlier editions used 60 MoE layers and explained away the 676B overshoot. See [errata](../../book/appendix/d-errata.md).

**2. GPU-hours versus cluster and duration.**

$$\frac{2{,}788{,}000}{2{,}048} = 1{,}361 \text{ hours} = 56.7 \text{ days}$$

Against the stated "approximately 2 months." **Consistent.**

**3. Implied MFU.**

From [Exercise 1.2](../ch01.md): $3.29\times10^{24}$ FLOPs against $9.93\times10^{24}$ available at BF16 peak gives **33%** — or ~17% measured against FP8 peak.

**Is that plausible for an MoE at this scale?** Yes, and it is arguably good. Dense models reach 40–50%; MoE runs lower because of the all-to-all on the critical path, the small expert GEMMs (§5.4), and load imbalance. 33% BF16-equivalent for a 256-expert MoE on bandwidth-constrained hardware is a strong result, and it is a large part of what DualPipe bought.

**4. Where numbers do not reconcile, the benign explanation.**

The recurring one across all three checks: **$6ND$ is an approximation that omits attention FLOPs and MoE routing overhead**, so any MFU computed from it *understates* real utilization. Combined with the ambiguity about which precision's peak is the denominator (§1.2 part 4), the plausible range for V3's true MFU spans roughly 17% to 40% depending on conventions.

**The transferable lesson:** when a published number does not reconcile, the first hypothesis should be a *convention* difference — what is counted, at what precision, over what boundary — not an error or a fabrication. Almost every apparent inconsistency in a technical report resolves to a definitional difference, and finding which one is the actual skill this exercise trains.
