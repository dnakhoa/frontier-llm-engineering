## 16.1 🔢 Correct by luck

**1. All 6 steps correct.** $0.85^6 = \mathbf{0.377}$.

**2. Exactly two errors.**

$$\binom{6}{2}(0.15)^2(0.85)^4 = 15 \times 0.0225 \times 0.5220 = \mathbf{0.176}$$

**3. Right answer with exactly two errors.** $0.176 \times 0.08 = \mathbf{0.0141}$.

**4. Fraction of correct answers with a flawed derivation.**

Correct answers come from clean derivations (0.377) plus lucky ones (0.0141):

$$\frac{0.0141}{0.377 + 0.0141} = \frac{0.0141}{0.391} = \mathbf{3.6\%}$$

About 1 in 28 correct answers reached the right number through invalid reasoning.

**5. What outcome-only GRPO does to them.**

They receive **full positive reward**, so their advantage is positive, so **every token in the flawed derivation gets its probability increased** — including both errors.

You are training the model to make compensating mistakes.

Two things make this less alarming than it first sounds, and one that makes it worse:

- *Less alarming:* at 3.6% they are a small minority of the positive signal, and the correct derivations reinforce correct steps far more strongly. The net gradient still points the right way.
- *Less alarming:* the specific compensating-error pattern is unlikely to reproduce, so what gets reinforced is largely the shared correct structure.
- **Worse:** the fraction grows as the model improves in *some* ways and not others. If step accuracy rises to 0.95, clean derivations become $0.95^6 = 0.735$ and two-error cases fall to $0.031$, so the lucky fraction drops to 0.3% — good. But if the model instead learns to produce *plausible-looking* derivations without improving step accuracy, the fraction rises. §16.7's concern is exactly this: outcome supervision cannot distinguish the two directions.

This is the motivating calculation for process supervision, and it is worth noting that at 3.6% it motivates *concern*, not necessarily a PRM — see §16.4.

## 16.2 🔢 The cost of automatic step labels

**1. Total generations.**

$$200{,}000 \text{ problems} \times 7 \text{ prefixes} \times 16 \text{ rollouts} = \mathbf{22.4\text{M generations}}$$

**2. Total tokens.**

$$22.4\times10^6 \times 400 = \mathbf{8.96\times10^9 = 8.96\text{B tokens}}$$

**3. GPU-hours.**

$$\frac{8.96\times10^9}{2{,}000 \times 64} = \frac{8.96\times10^9}{128{,}000} = 70{,}000 \text{ s} = 19.4 \text{ hours of wall clock}$$

$$19.4 \times 64 = \mathbf{1{,}244 \text{ GPU-hours}}$$

**4. Versus a GRPO run.**

[Exercise 13.7](ch13.md) gave 5.74B tokens generated across 500 iterations. This PRM labelling job generates **8.96B tokens — about 1.6× the entire RL run** it is meant to improve.

**5. The conclusion.**

**Building a PRM by Monte Carlo labelling costs more generation than the RL run it supports, before you have trained the PRM or run a single RL step.**

That is the practical reason PRMs are less common than the literature suggests, and it is independent of §16.7's hacking argument. Even if PRMs worked perfectly as RL objectives, the labelling cost would make them a second-order optimization for most teams.

Note what changes the calculation: fewer rollouts ($N=4$ cuts it 4×, at the cost of much noisier labels), fewer problems, or shorter completions. And note what does *not* change it: better hardware, since the same arithmetic applies to the RL run too.

## 16.3 🔢 Search-time scaling

**1. pass@$n$ = $1 - 0.6^n$.**

| $n$ | pass@$n$ |
|---|---|
| 4 | $1 - 0.1296 = \mathbf{87.0\%}$ |
| 16 | $1 - 0.6^{16} = 1 - 0.00028 = \mathbf{99.97\%}$ |
| 64 | $\approx \mathbf{100\%}$ |
| 256 | $\approx \mathbf{100\%}$ |

**2. Majority voting at $n = 16$.**

Each sample: correct with probability 0.40, or one of 8 wrong values with probability $0.60/8 = 0.075$ each.

Expected counts over 16 samples: correct ≈ 6.4; each wrong value ≈ 1.2.

The correct answer wins the plurality unless an unlucky draw puts some wrong value above it. With an expected margin of 6.4 versus 1.2 and binomial spreads of roughly $\sqrt{16 \times 0.4 \times 0.6} = 1.96$ and $\sqrt{16 \times 0.075 \times 0.925} = 1.05$, the correct answer wins the large majority of the time.

**Estimate: ~92–95%.** The exact value requires a multinomial computation, and the useful observation is the *shape*: majority voting converts a 40% single-sample accuracy into >90% with 16 samples, using no verifier at all. That is why §16.6 calls it a strong baseline.

The critical dependency: **wrong answers must be diverse.** If all 8 wrong values were instead a single systematically-wrong answer at 60%, majority voting would return the wrong answer essentially always. Majority voting works because errors are spread and correct reasoning converges — and it fails completely when the model is *confidently and consistently* wrong.

**3. Best-of-16 with an 80% verifier.**

A correct candidate is present with probability pass@16 = 0.9997. Given one is present, the verifier picks it 80% of the time:

$$0.9997 \times 0.80 \approx \mathbf{80\%}$$

**Which is worse than majority voting**, despite having a verifier and despite the correct answer being present in essentially every group.

That is the point of the exercise: **a mediocre verifier is worse than no verifier.** At $n=16$ the bottleneck is not finding a correct answer — pass@16 is 99.97% — it is *identifying* it, and an 80% verifier caps you at 80% no matter how large $n$ gets.

**4. Where independence breaks, and in which direction.**

Samples from one model on one prompt are **positively correlated**, not independent. The model has a consistent view of the problem; if it misunderstands the setup, it misunderstands it in most samples.

**Direction: pass@$n$ is substantially overstated by the independence assumption.** Real pass@16 for a model at 40% pass@1 is typically far below 99.97% — often 60–75%. The tail of "problems the model can sometimes solve" is much thinner than independence predicts, and there is a hard floor of problems it never solves at any $n$.

Two consequences:

- **The §15.1 "impossible" bucket is real and it does not shrink with $n$.** Correlated failure is why 20% of prompts stay at zero pass rate however many samples you draw.
- **Reported pass@$n$ curves flatten much earlier than the independence model predicts**, and a paper reporting pass@256 near 100% is usually reporting a benchmark where the model's failures are genuinely diverse rather than systematic.

## 16.4 🏗️ Build or skip

**1. Competition math, strong policy, answer key, large budget.**

**Skip — probably.** You have a perfect outcome verifier (the answer key), and §16.1 says only ~4% of correct answers are lucky. Spend the [Exercise 16.2](ch16.md) budget on more RL with outcome rewards instead. *Revisit if* you measure the correct-by-luck fraction directly and it is much higher than 4%, which is the check to run first.

**2. Code with a test suite.**

**Skip, and note you effectively already have a PRM.** A failing test localizes to a specific case, a compiler error localizes to a line, a type error localizes to an expression. That is **rule-based** step-level signal with none of §16.7's hacking risk — the best case in the whole chapter, and §16.10 notes it is underexploited. Use the localization you already have rather than learning one.

**3. Legal document analysis, no verifier, expensive experts.**

**Skip the PRM; the economics are hopeless.** With no automatic verifier you cannot use the Math-Shepherd rollout trick (a step is good if you can *finish correctly* from it — but you cannot check "correctly"). That leaves human step annotation at expert rates, for a domain where §16.7's "is this intermediate step correct" question is genuinely contested even among experts. Spend the annotation budget on outcome-level preference data instead.

**4. Formal theorem proving in Lean.**

**Build — and it is barely a PRM.** The proof checker *is* a step-level verifier: every tactic either applies or does not, and the proof state after each step is fully determined and checkable. This is the cleanest verifier that exists, it is rule-based, and it gives dense per-step signal for free. Use it as an RL objective without reservation.

**5. Open-ended creative writing.**

**Skip.** There is no meaningful notion of a "step," no verifier at any level, and the quality judgement is exactly the kind §12.9 says has no verifier. A PRM here would be a learned scorer of an ill-defined unit, which is the worst configuration available.

**6. Multi-step agentic tasks with per-call success/error.**

**Build — from rules, not learning.** Each tool call's success or failure is observable, which gives you rule-based milestone rewards ([Chapter 18 §18.7](../../book/part-3-post-training/18-tool-use-agentic.md)). The caveat: a successful tool call is not a *useful* one, so success alone is a weak signal and you must avoid rewarding an agent for making many trivially-successful calls. Combine per-call success with outcome reward rather than replacing it.

**The pattern across all six:** build when the step-level signal is **rule-based and already available**; skip when it would have to be **learned**. That is §16.10's decision rule and it resolves five of the six cases immediately.

## 16.5 🏗️ Safe as a ranker, dangerous as an objective

**1. Precisely stated.**

**As a ranker:** you have a *fixed* set of $n$ candidates, drawn from a policy that was not trained against the scorer. The scorer's job is to order them. Nothing is optimizing *against* the scorer — the candidates were produced without reference to it.

**As an objective:** the policy's parameters are updated by gradients that point directly at increasing the scorer's output. The policy is, mechanically, a search procedure for inputs the scorer rates highly — including inputs where the scorer is wrong.

**2. Why there is no adversary at inference.**

Because the candidate distribution is **fixed and independent of the scorer**. The scorer's errors are still there, but the candidates are not drawn preferentially from the error regions — they are drawn from the policy's natural distribution, in which scorer-error regions occupy whatever measure they happen to occupy.

The one qualification: at very large $n$ you *are* sampling into the tail, so best-of-$n$ becomes a weak adversary — which is exactly why the best-of-$n$ curve turns over ([Exercise 12.4](ch12.md)) and why that turnover is a useful diagnostic. Best-of-$n$ is a much weaker optimizer than gradient descent, so it finds the artifacts much later.

**3. A concrete PRM artifact.**

Suppose the PRM, trained on step labels where correct steps tended to be explicit and complete, learned that **steps containing an explicit numeric restatement of the previous result score higher.**

*In best-of-$n$:* among 16 candidate solutions produced normally, a few happen to restate results explicitly. The PRM prefers them slightly. They are otherwise ordinary solutions, and the effect on selection quality is small — you occasionally pick a slightly more verbose correct solution over a terse correct one.

*In RL:* the policy discovers this within a few hundred steps and begins restating every intermediate result, then restating the restatements. Steps become "We have x = 7. So x = 7. Therefore x equals 7." The PRM scores each of these highly. Reasoning quality does not improve and may degrade, while the PRM's reported score climbs steadily.

The asymmetry is the whole point: the same artifact is a rounding error in one use and the dominant behaviour in the other.

**4. Does it generalize? Two other cases in this book.**

Yes, and it is one of the more portable ideas in Part III.

- **Reward models** ([Chapter 12](../../book/part-3-post-training/12-reward-modeling.md)). A reward model is safe for offline evaluation and ranking; used as an RL objective it gets hacked (§12.6), and the best-of-$n$ curve is precisely the test of how much optimization it can withstand.
- **Generative judges** (§12.8, §16.9). Excellent for evaluation and for filtering generated data ([Chapter 19 §19.5](../../book/part-3-post-training/19-case-study-r1.md) uses one exactly this way — as a ranker on a fixed candidate set). Put one in an RL loop and the policy learns to write responses that read as authoritative to that judge.

A third, slightly different: **benchmarks** ([Chapter 23](../../book/part-4-infra/23-evaluation.md)). A benchmark is a fine measurement until someone optimizes against it, at which point it stops measuring. Same structure — the adversary is a human research process rather than gradient descent, but the failure is identical.

**5. When to use a PRM as an RL objective anyway.**

- **When it is rule-based** (§16.4 cases 4 and 6). A proof checker cannot be hacked because it is not an approximation of anything.
- **When you keep a KL budget and measure independently** — treat it exactly like a learned reward model in Chapter 13, with a KL target derived from a best-of-$n$ curve ([Exercise 12.4](ch12.md) part 5) and periodic evaluation against something the PRM did not train on.
- **When you combine it with an outcome verifier** so the PRM shapes the path but the outcome reward dominates the total. A PRM weighted at, say, 20% of the reward can improve credit assignment without being the thing the policy optimizes hardest.

**What to monitor:** the PRM's score versus an *independent* outcome measure. The moment PRM score rises while outcome accuracy is flat, you are in §16.5 part 3's failure and the KL budget is spent.

## 16.6 📄 Read R1's unsuccessful attempts

**1. Three reasons for abandoning PRMs, restated.**

- **Defining a step is hard.** In general reasoning — as opposed to numbered mathematical derivations — there is no clean, agreed decomposition of a trace into steps. The PRM's basic unit is ill-defined outside narrow domains.
- **Judging an intermediate step is hard.** Automatic annotation (Monte Carlo rollouts) gives unsatisfactory labels; human annotation does not scale. Both routes are noisy, and the noise is on the labels the whole method depends on.
- **A learned PRM reintroduces reward hacking.** Once a neural network is in the reward path, the policy can find its errors. Mitigating this requires retraining the PRM periodically, which adds cost and pipeline complexity — and gives up the property (an unhackable reward) that made their approach work.

**2. MCTS and the branching-factor argument.**

In games like Go, MCTS works because the action space at each node is bounded and modest — a few hundred legal moves. Tree search can meaningfully cover the promising branches.

In language generation, **every token is an action**, so the branching factor is the vocabulary size — on the order of $10^5$. A tree over token sequences is astronomically larger than anything MCTS was designed for. Constraining the branching (only expanding the top-$k$ tokens) makes search tractable and, they report, leads to local optima — the search commits early to a path and cannot recover.

They also note the difficulty of training a fine-grained value model to guide the search, which is the same problem as the PRM labelling difficulty in a different costume.

**3. Does the paper claim these cannot work?**

**No, and the hedge matters.** The section is framed as *unsuccessful attempts* — approaches that did not work *for them*, in *their* setting, with the effort they applied. The paper notes these approaches may still be valuable and that their failure here does not establish a general negative result.

Read it as evidence about difficulty and cost-effectiveness, not as a proof of impossibility. A team with a narrower domain (where steps *are* well-defined) or a rule-based step verifier is in a different situation entirely.

**4. Reconciling with "Let's Verify Step by Step."**

The two results are compatible and the reconciliation is §16.7's:

- **Lightman et al. used the PRM as a ranker** — best-of-$n$ selection over a fixed candidate set, on competition math, with human step labels. In that use, PRMs demonstrably beat outcome supervision, and the gap widens with $n$ because correct-by-luck grows as a fraction of what ORMs accept.
- **R1 wanted a PRM as an RL objective** — a very different demand, with gradient descent as an adversary.
- **Domain differs too.** PRM800K is competition math with numbered derivations, where "a step" is well-defined. R1's target was general reasoning, where it is not.
- **Label source differs.** Lightman et al. had 800,000 *human* step annotations. R1 would have needed automatic labels, with the [Exercise 16.2](ch16.md) cost and the noise that comes with them.

So: **PRMs work as rankers, in domains with well-defined steps, given good labels. R1 needed none of those three conditions to hold.** Neither result contradicts the other, and holding both is the correct state.

## 16.7 🔢 Reporting compute-dependent scores

**1. Headline versus deployment.**

**A paper headlines 81%** (best-of-64) — the largest number.

**A deployment cares about 62%** (pass@1), because that is what a single API call returns. Unless the product is explicitly built to sample 64 times and vote, in which case it cares about 79% *and* about the 64× cost.

The gap between 62% and 81% is 19 points of accuracy purchased with 64× the inference compute. Whether that is a good trade is a product question, and it is invisible in a table that reports only one number.

**2. Mean tokens per query.**

| Method | Samples | Tokens |
|---|---|---|
| pass@1 | 1 | **3,000** |
| majority-vote-64 | 64 | **192,000** |
| best-of-64 | 64 (+ verifier passes) | **192,000+** |

**3. Cost per query at $2/M output tokens.**

| Method | Cost |
|---|---|
| pass@1 | $0.006 |
| majority-vote-64 | **$0.384** |
| best-of-64 | **$0.384+** |

**64× the cost for 19 points**, or about $0.02 per additional point of accuracy per query. At scale that is a serious number: a million queries a day is $6,000/day at pass@1 and $384,000/day at 64 samples.

**4. A model-card result statement.**

> **MATH-500: 62.0% (pass@1, 1 sample, ~3.0k output tokens/query). With 64 samples: 79.4% by majority vote, 81.2% with verifier selection (~192k output tokens/query).** Evaluated with [harness + version], greedy decoding for pass@1, temperature 0.8 for sampled results.

The properties that make it a good statement: it gives the single-call number first, states the inference budget for every figure, quantifies the token cost so a reader can price it, and names the harness so the number is reproducible (§23.4).

What it deliberately avoids: quoting 81% without qualification, which is true and misleading.

## 16.8 🧪 Break the search lab

**1. Majority voting versus best-of-$n$.**

*Prediction people make:* the verifier wins everywhere, since it has more information.

*What happens:* **the crossover is in verifier quality, not in $n$.**

With a clean verifier, best-of-$n$ leads at every $n \ge 2$ — the lab measures 88.3%
against majority voting's 55.0% at $n = 4$, and best-of-$n$ tracks the oracle
while majority voting saturates at 92%. Majority voting does not win at small
$n$; it is behind from the start and stays behind.

What majority voting has is *robustness*, and that is where it takes over. Add
Gaussian noise to the verifier's scores and it overtakes once the noise reaches
about 1.0 — the size of the signal the verifier is trying to resolve (correct
scores 1, incorrect scores 0). Past that point self-consistency is both better
and cheaper, since it needs no verifier to train, host, or keep calibrated.

The saturation argument is still right and still matters: majority voting is
bounded by whether the *mode* is correct, and no amount of sampling moves the
mode. That is why it flattens at 92% while the oracle reaches 100%. But the
practical rule is the noise threshold — a verifier is worth using exactly while
it is more reliable than the model's own consensus.

**2. Adding noise to the verifier.**

Sweep the noise level and find the crossover. The instructive part is **how low the bar is**: a verifier only modestly better than random selection loses to majority voting, because majority voting is exploiting real structure in the error distribution while a noisy verifier is exploiting nothing.

Report the accuracy at which the crossover occurs. It is usually higher than people guess — often the verifier needs to be well above 60–70% selection accuracy to beat voting at moderate $n$.

**3. A length-rewarding flawed verifier.**

*Prediction:* the curve is worse than the good verifier's but still rises.

*What happens:* it **rises then turns over**, exactly like RM-B in [Exercise 12.4](ch12.md). At small $n$ the candidate pool contains few extreme-length solutions and the verifier's bias barely matters. As $n$ grows you sample further into the tail, extreme-length solutions appear, and the verifier reliably picks them — so accuracy falls.

Find the turnover $n$ and convert it to a KL budget ([Exercise 12.4](ch12.md) part 5). That is the same diagnostic, applied to a PRM instead of a reward model, and it is the concrete demonstration of §16.5's "safe as a ranker, dangerous as an objective" — the ranker is safe *up to a point*, and best-of-$n$ locates the point.

**4. PRM aggregators: minimum, product, mean.**

*Prediction:* mean is most robust.

*What happens:* **it depends on how noisy the PRM is, and the ordering reverses.**

With clean step scores, minimum wins as the semantics predict: the lab measures
99.9% fully-correct selection at $\sigma = 0.3$. A solution with one badly wrong
step is wrong regardless of the others, and minimum encodes exactly that.

With noisy step scores, **minimum is among the worst** — 59.7% at $\sigma = 3.0$,
below both product and mean. The reason is in the word: a minimum over $k$ noisy
estimates is decided by the *unluckiest of the $k$ draws*, so it does no
averaging at all and inherits the worst error in the score vector. One perfectly
good step that happens to draw a bad sample sinks the whole candidate.

The aggregators that **pool** evidence across steps — product and mean — degrade
more gracefully, because independent step noise partly cancels before the
comparison is made. Which of the two leads at the noisy end is not stable across
runs; that they both overtake minimum is.

So the principle needs both halves. Match the aggregator to the failure
semantics *and* to your PRM's calibration: take minimum if you trust the step
scores, take something that pools them if you do not. And note the trap — at low
noise all four aggregators look identical, so anyone comparing them on a clean
PRM will conclude they are interchangeable and ship whichever they wrote first.

One more row worth reading: **"last step only"** is near the best on
final-answer accuracy and clearly the worst on fully-correct selection. It is an
outcome reward model wearing a PRM's interface, and that gap is the entire
argument for process supervision — invisible if you only measure whether the
answer was right.
