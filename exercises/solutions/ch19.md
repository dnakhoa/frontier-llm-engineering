## 19.1 🏗️ Reconstruct the pipeline

The reference version.

**1. Starting model: DeepSeek-V3-Base.** Two properties that matter:

- **It is a base model, not instruction-tuned.** Everything R1 becomes is built on what pre-training already installed — which is what makes §19.4's latent-capability finding possible to observe.
- **It is a large MoE** (671B total, 37B active). Post-training compute per token is set by 37B, but *memory* is set by 671B, and RL needs the policy plus a reference plus a generation cluster. The architecture that made pre-training cheap makes post-training infrastructure harder.

**2. R1-Zero.** V3-Base → GRPO with rule-based rewards. No SFT, no demonstrations, no human reasoning traces. The reward had exactly two components: **accuracy** (does the final answer match the key / do the tests pass) and **format** (is the reasoning inside the required tags). AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from a reported 15.6% to 71.0%; 86.7% with majority voting at 64.

**3. What it established and broke.**

*Established:* reasoning was **latent** in the base model — RL surfaced it rather than installing it. SFT is **not required**. Extreme reward **sparsity works** — one bit at the end of thousands of tokens. Behaviours **emerge**: length growth, self-verification, backtracking, none of them in the reward.

*Broke:* **readability** and **language consistency** — the model mixed English and Chinese mid-trace. Both because nothing in the reward mentioned either.

**4. The four stages.**

| Stage | What | For |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Cold start | SFT on a few thousand curated long chain-of-thought examples | **Presentation**, not capability: readability, language consistency, stable format |
| 2 — Reasoning RL | GRPO + accuracy + format + a language-consistency reward | **Capability**: this is where the reasoning is built |
| 3 — Rejection sampling + SFT | ~600k reasoning + ~200k general samples, filtered; 2 epochs | **Generality**: turns a reasoning specialist back into a usable general model |
| 4 — Final RL | Hybrid reward: rules for reasoning, learned RMs for helpfulness and harmlessness | **Product**: makes it shippable |

**5. Distillation.** The ~800k Stage-3 samples were SFT'd into smaller open models (Qwen2.5, Llama, 1.5B–70B) with **no RL on the small models**. The distilled models substantially outperformed their bases, and — the interesting part — **distillation from the large model beat running RL directly on a small one**, at less compute.

**6. Abandoned.** **Process reward models** (steps ill-defined in general reasoning; intermediate correctness hard to judge; a learned PRM reintroduces reward hacking) and **MCTS** (the token-level branching factor is unmanageable; constraining it produced local optima; the guiding value model was itself hard to train).

**Scoring.** Items 3 and 6 are the ones most often missed. If you missed 3, reread §19.4 — it is the conceptual core. If you missed 6, reread §19.7, which is the most useful section in the chapter and the one that is unique to this report.

## 19.2 🔢 The two AIME numbers

**1. Independent pass@64 versus reported 86.7%.**

$$\text{pass@64} = 1 - (1 - 0.71)^{64} = 1 - 0.29^{64} \approx 1 - 10^{-34} \approx \mathbf{100\%}$$

The reported majority-vote figure of 86.7% is **enormously** below that.

**Why:** two separate reasons, and both matter.

- **Samples are not independent** ([Exercise 16.3](ch16.md) part 4). The model has a consistent view of a problem; if it misunderstands the setup it misunderstands it in most samples. There is a hard floor of problems it never solves at any sample count. So the true pass@64 is far below 100% — probably in the high 80s or low 90s.
- **Majority voting is not pass@$n$.** pass@$n$ asks whether a correct answer appears *anywhere* in the 64. Majority voting requires the correct answer to be the *most common*. If the model produces a consistent wrong answer 40 times and the correct one 24 times, pass@64 counts it as solved and majority voting returns the wrong answer.

**2. What the gap tells you.**

The gap between (true) pass@64 and majority-vote-64 measures **how often the model is confidently and consistently wrong** — cases where a correct answer was generated but was outvoted.

That gap is exactly the region where a **verifier** would help and voting does not ([Chapter 16 §16.6](../../book/part-3-post-training/16-process-reward-models.md)). If pass@64 is ~90% and majority-vote is 86.7%, roughly 3 points of accuracy are sitting there, recoverable by a good verifier and unrecoverable by voting.

**3. Token cost per query.**

| Metric | Samples | Tokens |
|---|---|---|
| pass@1 = 71.0% | 1 | **3,000** |
| majority-vote-64 = 86.7% | 64 | **192,000** |

**64× the tokens for 15.7 points.**

**4. The honest one-line statement.**

> **AIME 2024: 71.0% (pass@1, 1 sample, ~3k output tokens/query); 86.7% with majority voting over 64 samples (~192k output tokens/query).**

The properties that make it honest: the single-call number first, the inference budget stated for both, and the token cost given so a reader can price the difference. This is [Exercise 16.7](ch16.md) part 4's format, and §23.8 argues it should be the standard.

## 19.3 🏗️ What each stage is for

**1. Cold-start SFT.**

*Problem solved:* R1-Zero's readability and language-mixing failures. It puts the policy in a region where traces are legible and consistently in one language, so RL starts from and stays near legible behaviour.

*Without it:* R1-Zero. The capability is there; the output is hard to read and mixes languages.

*Mergeable?* Not with Stage 2 — the whole point is that it precedes RL. Arguably mergeable with Stage 3's SFT if you were willing to run RL twice, but that inverts the pipeline for no benefit.

**2. Reasoning RL.**

*Problem solved:* the model does not reason at length by default. This stage builds the capability.

*Without it:* you have a model that imitates a few thousand reasoning traces. Per §19.4, imitation of a small set does not produce the capability — R1-Zero showed RL is what surfaces it.

*Mergeable?* No. This is the irreducible stage.

**3. Rejection sampling + second SFT.**

*Problem solved:* Stage 2 optimized hard on math and code, and the model has drifted toward being a reasoning specialist. This stage generates a broad, filtered dataset (~600k reasoning + ~200k general) and retrains, restoring general capability while keeping the reasoning.

*Without it:* excellent at competition math, noticeably worse at writing, factual QA, translation, and conversation. This is [Chapter 11 §11.9](../../book/part-3-post-training/11-sft-frontier-style.md)'s capacity allocation appearing as a consequence of RL rather than of a mixture decision.

*Mergeable?* Partially — you could fold the general data into Stage 4's RL. But rejection sampling is *cheap and stable* relative to RL, so doing the bulk of the restoration with SFT and reserving RL for the final polish is the better ordering.

**4. Final RL.**

*Problem solved:* the model is capable and general but not yet a good assistant — helpfulness, tone, and safety are unaddressed. Hybrid reward: rules for reasoning, learned RMs for helpfulness and harmlessness.

*Without it:* a strong model that is unpleasant to use and has no safety behaviour. Not shippable.

*Mergeable?* You could attempt one RL stage with all reward types from the start. Two reasons not to: the learned RMs are hackable ([Chapter 12 §12.6](../../book/part-3-post-training/12-reward-modeling.md)) so you want to spend as little KL budget against them as possible, and mixing an unhackable reward with a hackable one from step 0 means the policy has thousands of steps to find the hackable one. Doing the long, hard optimization against rules first and the short, careful optimization against learned rewards last is deliberate.

**What R1-Zero's success tells you about the split.**

R1-Zero skipped 1, 3, and 4 and still produced the reasoning capability. So:

- **Stage 2 is about capability.** It is the only one that is.
- **Stages 1, 3, and 4 are about product** — legibility, generality, and assistant behaviour respectively.

That is a useful decomposition to carry: **one stage makes the model smart; three make it usable.** And the three cost far less compute than the one. It also explains why R1-Zero was worth publishing separately — it isolates the capability claim from the product engineering.

## 19.4 🔢 The language-consistency trade

**1. Why restricting to one language could reduce accuracy.**

The model's internal computation is not in any language; the *tokens* are. A concept may be more compactly or more precisely expressed by a token sequence from another language — a mathematical term with a shorter Chinese rendering, a construction the model has seen more often in one language's training data.

Forcing single-language output means the model must express intermediate reasoning in tokens that are, for that specific step, a slightly worse representation. Over a long trace, small representational losses compound.

Mechanically: the reward now has a term the policy must satisfy that is **orthogonal to correctness**, so some probability mass that would have gone to accuracy-improving continuations goes to language-conforming ones instead. That is the capability tax in its general form.

**2. Three other capability taxes in this book.**

- **Multilingual versus English** ([Chapter 11 §11.9](../../book/part-3-post-training/11-sft-frontier-style.md), [Exercise 3.4](ch03.md)). Capacity is finite; adding multilingual SFT data measurably costs English performance at fixed model size.
- **Safety versus helpfulness** ([Exercise 17.4](ch17.md)). Reducing harmful compliance raises over-refusal; the two move along a frontier and no single knob improves both.
- **Reasoning length versus latency** (§15.6, [Exercise 15.6](ch15.md)). Longer reasoning improves accuracy and costs tokens and time.

A fourth, at the pre-training layer: **long-context extension costs short-context quality** ([Exercise 9.3](ch09.md)) — Position Interpolation buys 128K by compressing the resolution the model used for local structure.

**3. Arguing to keep it at 1.5 points.**

Keep it, and the argument has three parts.

- **The cost is measured in a metric that does not capture the benefit.** AIME accuracy does not measure whether a user can read the trace. A model that is 1.5 points better and produces traces nobody can follow is worse *as a product*, and the evaluation is simply not measuring the axis that changed.
- **Reasoning traces are user-facing.** For reasoning models specifically, the trace is often shown, and it is a large part of what users trust. An unreadable trace undermines the thing the model is for.
- **1.5 points is within the range where benchmark noise and harness choices matter** ([Chapter 23 §23.4](../../book/part-4-infra/23-evaluation.md)). On AIME's small problem count, the confidence interval on a 1.5-point difference is substantial.

**4. At 8 points.**

**The answer changes.** 8 points is well outside noise and it is a large capability difference — the gap between a competitive model and a non-competitive one.

**What I would do instead**, in order of preference:

- **Move the constraint out of the RL objective.** Keep RL unconstrained on language, then apply a **post-hoc translation pass** on the trace, or train a small separate model to render the trace in the target language. The reasoning stays optimal; the presentation is fixed downstream. This is the general move: *when a constraint costs capability, enforce it outside the objective* — the same instinct as [Exercise 10.4](ch10.md)'s bias-based load balancing.
- **Strengthen the cold start instead.** A larger, better cold-start set on readable single-language traces shapes the starting policy so RL naturally stays in that region, without a reward term fighting the accuracy term. This is what §15.7 means by "an SFT cold start doing the anchoring instead."
- **Show the answer, hide the trace.** If the trace cannot be both optimal and readable, do not show it. Many deployed reasoning models take this route.
- **Accept mixed-language traces for internal use and translate for display.**

The general principle: **at 1.5 points, pay the tax; at 8 points, restructure so you do not have to.**

**5. What reporting it tells you about the report.**

That it is unusually honest.

A paper reporting only the final numbers would present the language-consistency reward as an unambiguous improvement, and nobody could tell otherwise. Reporting that a design choice **cost measured accuracy and was kept anyway** is a disclosure with no upside for the authors — it makes their headline number slightly harder to defend.

It also tells you the decision was **made deliberately rather than discovered**, which is the more informative fact. Someone measured the trade, weighed it, and chose. That is the kind of detail that makes a report useful for reproduction rather than merely for citation, and it is the same quality that makes §19.7's unsuccessful-attempts section valuable.

## 19.5 🔢 Distillation versus RL on a small model

**1. Option A — GRPO on the 7B directly.**

Using [Exercise 13.7](ch13.md)'s shape but at 7B: generation on a 7B model runs perhaps 6× faster than on a 70B, say ~11,000 tok/s/GPU.

A GRPO iteration with 1,024 prompts, $G=8$, 3,000 tokens: $1{,}024 \times 8 \times 3{,}000 = 24.6$M tokens. On 8 GPUs at 11,000 tok/s: $24.6\times10^6 / 88{,}000 = 280$ s of generation, plus roughly the same again for training and scoring — call it **600 s per iteration**, or 1.33 GPU-hours.

$$\frac{50{,}000 \text{ GPU-hours}}{1.33} \approx \mathbf{37{,}000 \text{ iterations}}$$

That is a very substantial RL run — far more than the few thousand iterations typical of published reasoning-RL work. **Compute is not the constraint for Option A.**

**2. Option B — SFT on 800k distilled traces.**

800k samples at ~3,000 tokens = 2.4B tokens. Two epochs = 4.8B tokens.

$$\text{FLOPs} = 6 \times 7\times10^9 \times 4.8\times10^9 = 2.02\times10^{20}$$

At 990 TFLOP/s × 40% MFU: $2.02\times10^{20} / (3.96\times10^{14}) = 510{,}000$ GPU-seconds = **142 GPU-hours.**

**Option B costs about 0.3% of the budget.** You could run it 350 times.

**3. Which R1's result favours, and by how much.**

**Option B, decisively.** The report states that distillation from the large model beat applying large-scale RL directly to a small model, at less compute — and this arithmetic shows *how much* less: two to three orders of magnitude.

The margin is not "distillation is somewhat better." It is "distillation is better *and* costs 0.3% as much."

**4. The mechanistic explanation.**

§19.4's finding: **RL surfaces latent capability rather than installing it.** GRPO increases the probability of token sequences that led to correct answers — but it can only reinforce sequences the policy *sometimes produces*. If a 7B base model essentially never produces a correct extended reasoning trace for a hard problem, there is nothing in the group to reinforce ([Exercise 15.1](ch15.md)'s zero-variance problem, at scale).

A 671B model *does* sometimes produce them, so RL has something to amplify. Its exploration — the expensive part — discovers the traces.

Distillation then **transfers the product of that exploration** through supervised imitation. The small model is not asked to discover extended reasoning; it is shown thousands of examples of it. SFT can install a behaviour the model would rarely generate on its own, which is precisely what RL cannot do.

The clean statement: **RL amplifies; SFT installs.** When the capability is absent from the base model, you need the second.

**5. When Option A wins.**

- **When no strong reasoner exists for your domain.** Distillation requires a teacher. For a specialized domain with no strong model, RL on the small model is the only option — and its ceiling is whatever is latent in that base.
- **When the base model is already close.** If the 7B produces correct extended traces 10–20% of the time, the zero-variance problem is manageable and RL has real signal to amplify.
- **When you want capability *beyond* the teacher.** Distillation caps you at the teacher's distribution. RL can, in principle, find solutions the teacher does not produce — though §15.11 lists whether it actually does as an open question.
- **When licensing or access forbids using the teacher's outputs.** A practical constraint that decides this more often than the technical arguments.

The best answer in practice is **both**: distil first to install the behaviour, then run RL on the distilled model to push beyond the teacher. That is cheap (142 GPU-hours) and it starts RL from a policy where the zero-variance problem is much smaller.

## 19.6 📄 Reading for absence, again

**1. Six missing items.**

| Missing | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **The RL prompt set** — how many problems, from where, at what difficulty distribution | Per [Exercise 15.1](ch15.md), difficulty calibration determines your effective batch size. This is arguably the most load-bearing unpublished choice. |
| **The cold-start data** — "a few thousand" examples, with collection approaches described but not the data, criteria, or exact count | Stage 1 fixes readability; without the data you cannot reproduce that fix |
| **GRPO hyperparameters** — $G$, $\beta$, learning rate, clip range, number of RL steps | Standard omission; recoverable by search, at cost |
| **Reward component weights** — accuracy versus format versus language consistency | §15.7 shows a too-large format bonus produces format collapse; the ratio matters |
| **Compute per stage** — GPU-hours for R1-Zero, each of the four stages, and the distillation | The number everyone wants and nobody publishes |
| **Failures within the successful path** — diverged runs, intermediate checkpoints, how the recipe evolved | The largest gap between reading the report and doing the work |

Also unpublished: the Stage-4 reward models (described by reference to V3's pipeline, which itself does not fully describe them), and the filtering criteria for Stage 3's 800k samples.

**2. The single largest barrier.**

**The RL prompt set**, specifically its difficulty calibration.

Everything else is recoverable with effort: hyperparameters by search, cold-start data by generating your own, compute by measurement. But §15.9 and the reproductions in §19.9 both establish that **prompt difficulty distribution matters more than the algorithm** — and a prompt set is not something you can search for. You either have a large supply of verifiable problems calibrated to your policy's ability, or you do not, and building one is a data-engineering project measured in months.

This is why §19.10 concludes the moat is the data.

**3. Two things the failures section tells you that a success-only paper could not.**

- **Which promising directions do not pay off at scale.** Process reward models and MCTS are the two most theoretically-motivated enhancements to outcome-only RL. Knowing that a well-resourced team tried both and abandoned them saves everyone else the same experiment. A success-only paper would simply not mention them, and readers would assume they were untried.
- **What the actual obstacles are, as opposed to the conceptual ones.** "Defining a step is hard in general reasoning" and "the branching factor in language generation is unmanageable" are practical findings you cannot derive from first principles. They tell you *why* the theory does not transfer, which is more useful than knowing that it does not.

A third, more general: it tells you the team's *reasoning*, not just their conclusion. The stated concern that a learned PRM reintroduces reward hacking is a design principle you can apply elsewhere ([Exercise 16.5](ch16.md)), independent of PRMs.

**4. R1 versus V3, same pattern?**

**Same pattern, and that is the point.**

Both publish the **method** in reproducible detail — architecture and systems for V3, the pipeline and reward structure for R1. Both withhold the **data**: mixture proportions, filtering criteria, prompt sets, and the ablations that produced the choices.

R1 goes further than V3 in one respect — it publishes negative results, which V3 does not — and that is a genuine difference in candour rather than a difference in what is considered proprietary.

**Two reports, one lab, the same disclosure boundary.** Combined with [Exercise 3.6](ch03.md)'s finding that Llama 3 withholds the same category, that is three independent data points for the §19.10 conclusion: **the algorithm is publishable, the data is the advantage.**

## 19.7 🏗️ Reproduce it at 1/100 scale

**1. Which stages you can run.**

| Stage | Feasible on 8 H100s / 2 weeks? |
|---|---|
| R1-Zero-style RL from a base model | **Yes** — this is the core and it is what reproductions did |
| Stage 1 cold start | **Yes** — a few thousand SFT examples is hours |
| Stage 2 reasoning RL | **Yes**, at reduced scale |
| Stage 3 rejection sampling + SFT | **Partially** — 800k samples of generation is the expensive part; scale to ~50k |
| Stage 4 final RL with learned RMs | **No** — you would need to train helpfulness and safety reward models first, which is its own project |

**What you cannot do, and why:** Stage 4, for the reason above, and anything requiring a 671B teacher. You also cannot reproduce the *scale-dependent* findings — whether the emergent behaviours appear the same way at 7B as at 671B is exactly what a small reproduction cannot tell you.

**2. Prompt set.**

- **Source:** public math datasets with verifiable answers — MATH, GSM8K's harder tail, AMC/AIME archives, plus synthetic problems generated with known answers. For code: problems with test suites.
- **Size:** 20,000–50,000 problems. More than you can use per iteration, so you can filter aggressively.
- **Difficulty calibration**, and this is the part reproductions found mattered most: sample $G=4$ from the base model on all problems purely to measure pass rates, then keep those in $[0.05, 0.85]$. Re-measure every ~300 steps ([Exercise 15.1](ch15.md) part 5). Hold the excluded hard problems and re-test them each round rather than discarding.
- **Contamination check** against your evaluation set before anything else (§23.5).

**3. Reward function.**

```python
def reward(response, ground_truth):
    r = 0.0
    if has_think_tags(response) and has_answer_tags(response):
        r += 0.05                       # small, per section 15.7
    ans = extract_answer(response)
    if ans is not None and answers_match(ans, ground_truth):
        r += 1.00
    return r
```

Deliberately minimal. No language-consistency term (single-language base model, single-language prompts), no length term initially — add one only if [Exercise 15.6](ch15.md) part 3's measurement shows accuracy has decoupled from length.

**4. What I expect to reproduce, and not.**

*Expect to reproduce* — these are robust across the published reproductions:
- Response length growing without a length reward.
- Emergence of self-verification and backtracking phrases.
- Substantial gains on the verifiable task.
- The zero-variance-group problem, and the benefit of filtering by pass rate.

*Expect **not** to reproduce:*
- **The magnitude of the gain.** 15.6% → 71.0% depended on V3-Base's latent capability. A 7B base has less to surface ([Exercise 19.5](ch19.md) part 4), and reproductions consistently found the base model matters enormously.
- **Transfer beyond the training domain.** Whether math RL improves general reasoning is contested at any scale and unmeasurable at this one.
- **Anything about the distillation result**, which requires a large teacher.

**5. The single measurement that tells you it worked.**

**Held-out accuracy on a decontaminated evaluation set, as a function of training step, against the base model's accuracy on the same set.**

Not reward — reward can rise from format compliance or from the [Exercise 15.3](ch15.md) biases. Not response length — length rises whether or not the model is improving. Not the qualitative appearance of "wait, let me reconsider," which is striking and is not evidence of capability.

Held-out accuracy is the one number that cannot be produced by any of the failure modes in §15.7.

**6. What to do differently, per §19.9's reproductions.**

- **Drop the std normalization** in the GRPO advantage, and drop the $1/|o_i|$ length normalization ([Exercise 15.3](ch15.md) part 5). Both are one-line changes and both were found to help.
- **Filter prompts by pass rate from the start**, rather than treating it as an optimization. Reproductions that did this outperformed those that did not, and it is free.
- **Consider plain REINFORCE with a group baseline** as a control. Several reproductions found it performs comparably, which — if true for you — removes a chunk of implementation surface.
- **Spend disproportionate effort on the base model choice.** §19.9's most consistent finding is that the same recipe on a weaker base produces much less. Testing three candidate base models with a short run each is a better use of the first three days than tuning hyperparameters.

## 19.8 🏗️ Where the moat is

**1. Evidence from three reports.**

- **DeepSeek-V3** ([Exercise 10.6](ch10.md)): publishes MLA, DeepSeekMoE, the bias-based balancer, FP8 scaling, and DualPipe in reimplementable detail. Withholds the mixture proportions, the quality-filtering criteria, and the classifier that implements them.
- **DeepSeek-R1** ([Exercise 19.6](ch19.md)): publishes the full pipeline, the reward structure, and even the failed approaches. Withholds the RL prompt set, the cold-start data, and the Stage-3 filtering criteria.
- **Llama 3** ([Exercise 3.6](ch03.md)): publishes the architecture, the training infrastructure, and the failure statistics. Gives mixture proportions only at a coarse level; withholds the quality classifier, its training data, and the scaling-law experiments that chose the mixture.

Three reports, two labs, one consistent boundary: **method published, data withheld.**

**2. Implication for which roles a lab is short of.**

If the advantage is data, the scarce roles are the ones that produce and validate it. [Chapter 25 §25.1](../../book/part-5-the-job/25-career-paths.md)'s scarcity column agrees:

- **Data Engineer (pre-training)** — very scarce
- **Data Engineer (post-training)** — very scarce
- **Environment Engineer** — very scarce, very new
- **Evaluation Engineer** — scarce and undervalued

Meanwhile **Pre-training Researcher** and **Alignment Researcher** — the roles that work on the *published* half — are the ones with the most applicants per seat.

The actionable version for a reader: **the competition is concentrated where the publications are, and the headcount is concentrated where the moat is.**

**3. Implication for open-source efforts.**

**Prioritize open data over open algorithms.**

The algorithms are already open — GRPO, DPO, FlashAttention, MoE routing, and every technique in this book are published and implemented in libraries. Reimplementing them again adds little.

What is scarce and would actually move the field:

- **Open, filtered, decontaminated pre-training corpora** at frontier scale, with the filtering criteria published. FineWeb is the model here.
- **Open verifiable-problem sets** with difficulty calibration — the [Exercise 19.7](ch19.md) part 2 artifact, at scale, which nobody has released.
- **Open environments** for agentic RL (§26.3), which is the emerging version of the same gap.
- **Open evaluation sets that are genuinely held out**, which is structurally hard since publishing one contaminates it.

Tülu 3 [\[53\]](../../book/appendix/b-references.md#53-tulu-3) is the existence proof that this is the high-value contribution: its value is the *data and recipe*, not a novel algorithm.

**4. The strongest counterargument.**

**DeepSeek-V3's own cost result.**

The 10× cost advantage over a comparable dense run ([Exercise 10.3](ch10.md)) came almost entirely from **algorithmic and systems choices** — MoE sparsity worth ~18×, FP8 worth ~1.6×, DualPipe recovering throughput on constrained hardware. Not from data.

A team with V3's exact corpus and a conventional dense BF16 architecture would have spent roughly $55M instead of $5.5M. So in that instance the algorithm was decisively the advantage, and it was published.

**The reconciliation:** algorithmic advantages are real and **temporary**. Publishing MLA and FP8 scaling meant everyone could adopt them, and within a year they were standard. Data advantages compound and do not transfer — a competitor who reads the V3 report can build an MoE with MLA, and cannot build DeepSeek's corpus.

So the honest form of the claim is: **algorithms produce large, short-lived advantages; data produces smaller, durable ones.** Labs publish the first and withhold the second, which is exactly what the disclosure pattern in part 1 shows them doing — and it is a rational strategy rather than a statement about which matters more.
