## 20.1 🔢 Arithmetic intensity, every operation

$T = 8192$ tokens, $d = 8192$, $d_{\text{ff}} = 28672$, BF16 (2 bytes).

**1. FFN first matmul** $(8192, 8192) \times (8192, 28672)$.

- FLOPs: $2 \times 8192 \times 8192 \times 28672 = 3.85\times10^{12}$
- Bytes: input $8192\times8192\times2 = 1.34\times10^8$; weights $8192\times28672\times2 = 4.70\times10^8$; output $8192\times28672\times2 = 4.70\times10^8$. Total $1.07\times10^9$.
- $I = 3.85\times10^{12}/1.07\times10^9 = \mathbf{3{,}598}$ → **compute-bound**, 12× past the ridge point.

**2. RMSNorm** on $(8192, 8192)$.

- FLOPs: square, sum, rsqrt, multiply by weight ≈ $4Td = 2.68\times10^8$
- Bytes: read $1.34\times10^8$ + write $1.34\times10^8$ = $2.68\times10^8$
- $I = \mathbf{1.0}$ → **memory-bound**, 295× below the ridge.

**3. SwiGLU elementwise product** on $(8192, 28672)$.

- FLOPs: SiLU (≈4 ops) plus the multiply ≈ $5 \times 8192 \times 28672 = 1.17\times10^9$
- Bytes: read two tensors, write one = $3 \times 4.70\times10^8 = 1.41\times10^9$
- $I = \mathbf{0.83}$ → **memory-bound**.

**4. Residual add** on $(8192, 8192)$.

- FLOPs: $Td = 6.71\times10^7$
- Bytes: read two, write one = $4.03\times10^8$
- $I = \mathbf{0.17}$ → **severely memory-bound**. Six bytes moved per FLOP.

**5. RoPE on Q and K.** With GQA 8:1, Q is $(8192, 8192)$ and K is $(8192, 1024)$.

- FLOPs: roughly 6 ops per element (two multiplies, one add, per element of a rotated pair) over $8192 \times 9216$ elements ≈ $4.53\times10^8$
- Bytes: read + write both tensors = $2 \times 2 \times 8192 \times 9216 \times 2 = 6.04\times10^8$ (plus the cos/sin tables, small)
- $I = \mathbf{0.75}$ → **memory-bound**.

**6. Ranked by fusion benefit.**

| Rank | Operation | $I$ | Bytes moved |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | SwiGLU product | 0.83 | $1.41\times10^9$ |
| 2 | RoPE | 0.75 | $6.04\times10^8$ |
| 3 | Residual add | 0.17 | $4.03\times10^8$ |
| 4 | RMSNorm | 1.0 | $2.68\times10^8$ |
| — | FFN matmul | 3,598 | (fusing helps only at the epilogue) |

Ranked by **bytes moved**, not by intensity — because fusion saves *traffic*, and the SwiGLU product moves 5× more bytes than RMSNorm despite similar intensity. A common mistake is to rank by arithmetic intensity and fuse the wrong things.

The FFN matmul is compute-bound and fusion cannot speed up its main loop; what fusion buys there is the **epilogue** — applying the activation to the matmul's output while it is still in registers, which eliminates operation 3 entirely.

## 20.2 🔢 The fusion win

**1. Separately.**

$$2.68\times10^8 + 1.41\times10^9 + 4.03\times10^8 + 6.04\times10^8 = \mathbf{2.69\times10^9 \text{ bytes}}$$

**2. Fused.**

An ideal fusion reads the layer input once and writes the layer output once. The intermediate $(8192, 28672)$ tensors never touch HBM.

Read $(8192,8192)$ + write $(8192,8192)$, plus the weights that must be read regardless:

$$1.34\times10^8 + 1.34\times10^8 = \mathbf{2.68\times10^8 \text{ bytes}}$$

**A 10× reduction in traffic** for these four operations.

(In practice, tile sizes and register pressure prevent a perfect single-pass fusion of an operation on a $28672$-wide tensor — realistically you fuse in groups and achieve perhaps 3–5×. The idealized number is the upper bound.)

**3. Time saved at 3.35 TB/s.**

$$\frac{2.69\times10^9 - 2.68\times10^8}{3.35\times10^{12}} = \frac{2.42\times10^9}{3.35\times10^{12}} = \mathbf{723 \text{ µs}}$$

**4. Over 80 layers, forward + backward.**

The backward pass moves roughly 2× the forward's traffic (gradients in addition to activations), so take 3× the forward total:

$$723 \text{ µs} \times 80 \times 3 = \mathbf{174 \text{ ms per step}}$$

**5. As a percentage of an 8-second step.**

$$\frac{0.174}{8} = \mathbf{2.2\%}$$

**Which is the honest and slightly deflating answer**, and it is why this exercise is worth doing.

Fusing the elementwise operations in a transformer layer is worth low single-digit percent on a *training* step, because training is dominated by the compute-bound matmuls. The 10× traffic reduction is real and it applies to a small share of the total time.

Where the same fusion is worth far more:

- **Decoding** ([Chapter 22 §22.2](../../book/part-4-infra/22-inference-serving.md)), where *everything* is memory-bound and there are no large compute-bound matmuls to hide behind. Here fusion can be worth tens of percent.
- **Small models or small batches**, where the matmuls are not large enough to be compute-bound.
- **Kernel-launch overhead**, which this calculation ignored: four kernels × 80 layers × 3 passes = 960 launches at ~5 µs = 4.8 ms, another 0.06%.

The transferable lesson: **compute the fraction of total step time before writing the kernel.** A 10× improvement on 2% of the runtime is a 1.8% speedup, and §20.1's intensity table tells you which operations are worth the effort only after you weight them by their share of the step.

## 20.3 🔢 FlashAttention memory

**1. The $N \times N$ matrix, 32 heads, BF16.**

$$32 \times N^2 \times 2 \text{ bytes}$$

| $N$ | Size |
|---|---|
| 8,192 | $32 \times 6.71\times10^7 \times 2 = \mathbf{4.29 \text{ GB}}$ |
| 32,768 | $\mathbf{68.7 \text{ GB}}$ |
| 131,072 | $\mathbf{1{,}100 \text{ GB}}$ |

**2. Across 80 layers at 128K.**

$$80 \times 1{,}100 = \mathbf{88 \text{ TB}}$$

For one sequence. This is not a memory-pressure problem; it is an impossibility, and it is why long context did not exist before 2022.

(In practice you would not hold all layers' attention matrices simultaneously — only the current layer's, plus whatever the backward pass needs. But even one layer's 1,100 GB against an 80 GB card settles it.)

**3. What FlashAttention stores instead.**

Per query, it retains only:
- The **output** $O$: $N \times d_{\text{head}}$ per head
- The **running max** $m$ and **running sum** $\ell$: two scalars per query per head, retained for the backward pass (usually combined into a single logsumexp value)

At $N = 131{,}072$, 32 heads, head dim 128, BF16:

- Output: $32 \times 131{,}072 \times 128 \times 2 = \mathbf{1.07 \text{ GB}}$
- Logsumexp: $32 \times 131{,}072 \times 4 = 16.8$ MB

**About 1.1 GB instead of 1,100 GB** — a 1,000× reduction, and it is exactly the $O(N^2) \to O(N)$ change.

**4. Why it is faster despite more arithmetic.**

The rescaling in the online-softmax recurrence is extra FLOPs — roughly 10–20% more than standard attention. But attention at these sizes is **memory-bound**, not compute-bound: the $N \times N$ matrix must be written to HBM and read back for the softmax and again for the $PV$ multiply.

FlashAttention keeps the tile in SRAM through all three operations. It trades a small amount of extra arithmetic (which was free, because the tensor cores were idle waiting on memory) for the removal of nearly all the HBM traffic.

This is [Chapter 20 §20.3](../../book/part-4-infra/20-custom-kernels.md)'s framing in its purest form: **when you are memory-bound, arithmetic is free.**

**5. Why the backward is relatively more expensive.**

Because storing the attention matrix $S$ for the backward pass would reintroduce the $O(N^2)$ memory that the forward pass just eliminated. So the backward **recomputes** $S$ from $Q$, $K$, $V$.

The backward therefore does the forward's attention work *again*, on top of its own gradient computation. Standard attention's backward reads a stored $S$; FlashAttention's regenerates it.

This is activation recomputation ([Chapter 6 §6.12](../../book/part-2-pretraining/06-distributed-training.md)) applied *inside a single operation* — the same memory-for-compute trade, at a much finer granularity, and it is a good example of the principle recurring at every level of the stack.

## 20.4 🏗️ Benchmark critique

**1. Four things wrong.**

- **No synchronization.** CUDA kernels launch asynchronously. `time.time()` measures how long it took the CPU to *enqueue* 1,000 launches, not how long the GPU took to run them. This alone can produce a fake 10× "speedup."
- **No warmup.** The first call includes JIT compilation (Triton compiles on first invocation), autotuning, and memory allocation. Amortized over 1,000 iterations it is diluted but not eliminated, and if the baseline was warmed and this was not, the comparison is meaningless.
- **One shape only.** $(4096, 4096)$ is a power of two that divides evenly into every plausible tile size. A kernel tuned for it may lose badly at $(4096, 5120)$ or $(4096, 6144)$.
- **L2 cache reuse.** The same 32 MB tensor is read 1,000 times in a tight loop. At $4096 \times 4096 \times 2 = 33.5$ MB it fits in an H100's 50 MB L2, so after the first iteration it is never read from HBM — which is not what happens in a real model where the tensor was just produced by another layer and the cache is full of other things.

A fifth, worth mentioning: **`torch.randn` produces well-conditioned data.** Real activations have outliers, and a kernel's behaviour on them may differ.

**2. Correct measurement.**

```python
import torch

def benchmark(fn, *args, warmup=25, iters=100, n_buffers=8):
    """Time a GPU function correctly.

    CUDA events measure on the GPU timeline, so the result excludes host-side
    Python overhead. Rotating through several input buffers defeats the L2
    reuse that a tight single-tensor loop would otherwise get.
    """
    # Rotate inputs so no buffer stays resident in L2 across iterations.
    buffers = [tuple(a.clone() if torch.is_tensor(a) else a for a in args)
               for _ in range(n_buffers)]

    for i in range(warmup):
        fn(*buffers[i % n_buffers])
    torch.cuda.synchronize()

    start = torch.cuda.Event(enable_timing=True)
    end = torch.cuda.Event(enable_timing=True)

    start.record()
    for i in range(iters):
        fn(*buffers[i % n_buffers])
    end.record()
    torch.cuda.synchronize()

    return start.elapsed_time(end) / iters   # milliseconds
```

**3. The right baseline.**

**`torch.compile` on the same operation**, not eager PyTorch.

Why eager PyTorch is the wrong baseline: eager mode runs each elementwise operation as a separate kernel with a separate HBM round trip. Beating it is trivial and proves nothing — `torch.compile` will beat it too, for free, in one line.

The question a kernel must answer is "**is this worth maintaining hand-written CUDA/Triton for?**", and the alternative to hand-writing is `torch.compile`, so that is the comparison. Per §20.10, most teams skip this step and hand-write kernels the compiler would have handled.

If a vendor library implements the operation (cuDNN, cuBLAS, a fused-norm from Apex or Transformer Engine), beat that too.

**4. What to report instead of a ratio.**

**Achieved memory bandwidth as a fraction of peak.**

$$\text{achieved GB/s} = \frac{\text{bytes read} + \text{bytes written}}{\text{time}}$$

For RMSNorm at $(4096, 4096)$ BF16: $2 \times 33.5$ MB $= 67$ MB per call. At, say, 0.025 ms, that is 2.68 TB/s — **80% of an H100's 3.35 TB/s peak.**

Why this is better than "4.2× faster":

- It is **absolute and comparable** across hardware, shapes, and baselines.
- It tells you **when to stop.** At 80% of peak there is at most 25% left; at 30% of peak there is a 3× opportunity. A speedup ratio tells you neither.
- It is **falsifiable.** Nobody can achieve 110% of peak bandwidth, so an implausible number is visibly implausible.

For compute-bound kernels, report achieved FLOP/s against peak instead.

**5. Shapes to sweep, and what to expect.**

Sweep the hidden dimension over powers of two **and** the awkward sizes in between: 512, 768, 1024, 1536, 2048, 3072, 4096, 5120, 6144, 8192, 11008, 16384. (11008 and 5120 are real Llama-family dimensions and neither is a power of two.)

Expected behaviour at the edges:

- **Small $d$ (≤ 1024):** the kernel loses or ties. The row fits in a few registers, the work per program is tiny, and **kernel-launch overhead (~5 µs) dominates**. `torch.compile` may win by fusing across operations in a way a single hand-written kernel cannot.
- **Large $d$ (≥ 8192):** the row may exceed what fits in SRAM at the chosen `BLOCK_SIZE`, forcing either a smaller block with multiple passes or register spilling. Expect a **visible cliff** at whatever size crosses the threshold.
- **Non-power-of-two $d$:** masking overhead and partially-filled tiles. Expect a **sawtooth** — good at 4096, worse at 5120, recovering at 8192.

Reporting the sweep, including the losses, is what §20.9 means by benchmarking honestly. A candidate who volunteers where their kernel loses is immediately more credible than one who reports a single number.

## 20.5 🔢 Numerics

**1. Summing 8,192 values.**

**BF16 accumulator:** $\epsilon = 2^{-8} \approx 3.9\times10^{-3}$. Random-walk error $\approx \sqrt{8192} \times 3.9\times10^{-3} \approx \mathbf{0.35}$ — a **35% relative error.**

Worse in the same-sign case, where the accumulator grows monotonically and small addends round away entirely (stagnation) — errors of 50%+ are common.

**FP32 accumulator:** $\epsilon = 2^{-24} \approx 6\times10^{-8}$. Error $\approx \sqrt{8192} \times 6\times10^{-8} \approx \mathbf{5\times10^{-6}}$ — negligible.

This is why every kernel in Chapter 20 accumulates in FP32 regardless of storage precision, and it is the first thing to check when a kernel's numerics are wrong.

**2. Softmax and the max subtraction.**

$e^x$ overflows FP32 when $x > \ln(3.4\times10^{38}) \approx \mathbf{88.7}$. In FP16, when $x > \ln(65504) \approx 11.1$.

Attention logits are $q \cdot k / \sqrt{d}$, and with unnormalized activations these routinely exceed 88 — especially late in training when weight norms have grown ([Exercise 8.5](ch08.md) pattern 4).

Subtracting the row maximum makes the largest exponent exactly $e^0 = 1$ and every other term $\le 1$, so overflow is impossible. It is mathematically exact — $\text{softmax}(x) = \text{softmax}(x - c)$ for any constant $c$ — so it costs nothing but a max reduction.

This is exactly what FlashAttention's running $m$ is: the same max subtraction, maintained incrementally because the full row is never materialized.

**3. Not matching bit-for-bit — bug or not?**

**Not necessarily a bug**, and expecting bit-exactness is the more common error.

Floating-point addition is not associative, so a parallel tree reduction and a sequential loop produce different results in the last bits. Both are correct implementations of the same mathematical operation.

**How to decide:**

- **Compare against a high-precision reference**, not against PyTorch. Compute the operation in FP64 on CPU; whichever of your kernel and PyTorch is *closer* to the FP64 answer is more accurate. Frequently that is your kernel, because PyTorch's eager implementation may accumulate in lower precision.
- **Check that the error is at the expected scale.** For BF16 storage with FP32 accumulation, relative error should be around $10^{-2}$ (set by the BF16 output rounding) and *not* growing with input size. Error that grows with $d$ means an accumulation problem.
- **Test with adversarial inputs**: large dynamic range, many same-sign values, values near the format's limits. A kernel correct on `randn` and wrong on real activations is a real bug that bit-comparison on `randn` would never find.

**4. Tolerance for BF16, and what indicates a problem.**

Test at roughly `rtol=1e-2, atol=1e-2` for BF16 output, since BF16's 7 mantissa bits give ~0.4% relative precision and a couple of operations compound it.

**What indicates a real problem:**

- **Error that grows with tensor size.** Signals BF16 accumulation (part 1).
- **Error concentrated in specific elements** rather than spread. Signals a masking or boundary bug — off-the-end elements, or a tile edge.
- **Any `NaN` or `Inf`** on finite input. Always a bug.
- **Error much larger for large-magnitude inputs.** Signals a missing max subtraction or an overflow.
- **Different results run to run** on identical input. Signals atomics or a race (part 5).

**5. Atomics: what they cost beyond performance.**

**Determinism.**

Atomic adds complete in nondeterministic order, so the reduction's floating-point associativity varies run to run. The same kernel on the same input produces slightly different results each time.

Consequences that matter:

- **Debugging becomes much harder.** You cannot bisect a loss spike ([Exercise 8.5](ch08.md)) if reruns diverge for reasons unrelated to the bug.
- **Bit-exact resumption is impossible** ([Chapter 21 §21.8](../../book/part-4-infra/21-checkpointing-resumption.md)). A run cannot be reproduced from a checkpoint.
- **`torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)` will refuse to run** the kernel, so anyone in your organization who needs determinism cannot use it.
- **Test flakiness.** A tolerance-based test that passes at $1.01\times$ tolerance and fails at $0.99\times$ becomes intermittent.

The alternatives: a tree reduction within a block followed by a deterministic cross-block combine, or a two-pass approach that writes partials and reduces them in a fixed order. Both cost some performance and both preserve determinism, and for a kernel destined for a production training run that is usually the right trade.

## 20.6 🏗️ When to write a kernel

| # | Operation | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Five elementwise ops on a large tensor | **`torch.compile`** | This is exactly what it does well — fuse the chain, one HBM round trip. Hand-writing gets you a few percent over the compiler for hours of work and a maintenance burden. §20.10's "most common waste of kernel-engineering time." |
| 2 | Fused RMSNorm + QKV projection | **Triton** | A norm fused into a GEMM prologue is beyond what `torch.compile` reliably does — it involves a reduction feeding a matmul, and the compiler generally will not restructure across that boundary. Triton's tile model fits, and you keep the normalized values in SRAM. |
| 3 | MoE dispatch: gather, ragged per-expert GEMMs, scatter | **CUDA** | Data-dependent indexing with variable-sized GEMMs is exactly what §20.7 says maps poorly onto Triton's tile model. You need explicit control of shared-memory layout, the gather pattern, and grouped-GEMM scheduling. This is one of the genuinely hard kernel problems (§20.11). |
| 4 | Attention with a novel masking pattern | **Triton** | FlashAttention-style kernels are well within Triton's expressiveness, and there are good reference implementations to adapt. Reach for CUDA only if you need Hopper-specific asynchrony (as FA-3 does). Consider first whether FlexAttention or a mask-modifier API already covers it. |
| 5 | FP8 GEMM with per-block scaling on Hopper | **CUDA** | Needs specific tensor-core instructions, the TMA engine, and careful control of the scaling factors' layout relative to the tiles. Triton's FP8 support has improved but does not expose everything, and this is a top-1% hot loop where the last 15% is worth weeks (§20.7). |
| 6 | Custom loss with an unusual reduction | **`torch.compile` first, then Triton** | Try the compiler; if the reduction pattern defeats it (data-dependent, multi-stage), Triton. Note the loss is computed once per step against 80 layers of everything else — check its share of step time before spending a day on it ([Exercise 20.2](ch20.md) part 5). |

**The meta-rule across all six:** escalate PyTorch → `torch.compile` → Triton → CUDA, and **only after profiling**. Items 1 and 6 are the ones people get wrong most often, in the direction of writing a kernel that was not needed.

## 20.7 📄 Read FlashAttention

**1. The online softmax recurrence.**

For query block $Q_i$, iterating over key/value blocks $j = 1, \dots, T_c$, maintaining running max $m$, running sum $\ell$, and running output $O$:

$$S_j = Q_i K_j^\top / \sqrt{d}$$
$$m^{(j)} = \max\big(m^{(j-1)},\ \text{rowmax}(S_j)\big)$$
$$P_j = \exp\big(S_j - m^{(j)}\big)$$
$$\ell^{(j)} = e^{m^{(j-1)} - m^{(j)}}\,\ell^{(j-1)} + \text{rowsum}(P_j)$$
$$O^{(j)} = e^{m^{(j-1)} - m^{(j)}}\,O^{(j-1)} + P_j V_j$$

and finally $O = O^{(T_c)} / \ell^{(T_c)}$.

**The rescaling factor $e^{m^{(j-1)} - m^{(j)}}$ is the whole trick.** When a new block contains a larger maximum, every previously-accumulated term was exponentiated relative to the old, smaller max — so it must be scaled down by exactly that factor to be consistent with the new one. Because the factor is applied to both $\ell$ and $O$ identically, the final division normalizes correctly.

Note that $m^{(j)} \ge m^{(j-1)}$ always, so the rescaling factor is $\le 1$ and cannot overflow.

**2. IO complexity.**

Standard attention: $\Theta(Nd + N^2)$ HBM accesses — the $N^2$ term is writing and re-reading the attention matrix.

FlashAttention: $\Theta(N^2 d^2 / M)$ where $M$ is SRAM size. Since $M \gg d^2$ in practice (SRAM is ~100KB+, $d^2$ for a head dim of 128 is 16K elements), this is **substantially less than $N^2$** — the paper's central claim, and it is an IO claim rather than a FLOP claim.

**3. The backward pass and recomputation.**

The paper states that storing $S$ and $P$ for the backward would require $O(N^2)$ memory, so instead the backward **recomputes** them from $Q$, $K$, $V$ and the stored $O$, $m$, $\ell$ (in practice, the combined logsumexp).

They note this is a favourable trade because the recomputation is done in SRAM, so the extra FLOPs cost little while the memory saving is the whole point. They also observe the backward is still faster than standard attention's backward despite the recomputation, for the same memory-bound reason as the forward.

**4. What FlashAttention-2 changed.**

Same algorithm, better mapping onto the hardware:

- **Reduced non-matmul FLOPs.** The original rescaled the output accumulator on every block; FA-2 defers the normalization and restructures the recurrence so fewer non-tensor-core operations sit between matmuls. Non-matmul FLOPs are far slower per unit than tensor-core FLOPs, so removing them matters disproportionately.
- **Parallelized over the sequence dimension**, not only over batch and heads. The original's parallelism was limited when batch × heads was small — exactly the long-context, small-batch case that matters most.
- **Better work partitioning between warps within a thread block**, reducing shared-memory traffic and synchronization between warps.

**The lesson §20.5 draws from this:** the algorithm was already right; two years of work on *how it maps to the machine* doubled it. Kernel performance is a hardware-mapping problem, and it is why FA-3 exists as well.

## 20.8 🧪 Break the kernel lab

**1. Numerical equivalence.**

If it fails, check in this order: FP32 accumulation for the sum of squares; the masking for rows whose length is not a multiple of `BLOCK_SIZE`; and whether `other=0.0` is set on the masked load (a garbage value in a masked lane still contributes to a sum).

The error signature tells you which: error growing with $d$ → accumulation; error only at specific row lengths → masking.

**2. Versus `torch.compile`.**

*Prediction people make:* the hand-written kernel wins comfortably.

*What happens:* **often a tie, or a narrow loss.** `torch.compile` generates Triton for exactly this kind of operation and its generated kernel is frequently as good as a straightforward hand-written one.

Where you do win: when you fuse *more* than the compiler chose to — norm plus the following projection's prologue, for instance — or when the compiler introduces a graph break.

This result is the point of the exercise, and it is §20.10's argument made personally: measure against the compiler before deciding to maintain a kernel.

**3. Sweeping $d$ from 512 to 16384.**

*Prediction:* monotone improvement with size.

*What happens:* a curve with **two losses at the edges.**

- At $d = 512$: the kernel loses, or ties. Work per program is tiny and the ~5 µs launch overhead is a large fraction of the total.
- Rising through $d = 2048$–$8192$: the kernel is at its best, achieving a high fraction of peak bandwidth.
- At $d = 16384$: a possible cliff, when `BLOCK_SIZE = next_power_of_2(16384)` exceeds what fits in SRAM with the chosen `num_warps`, forcing spills.

Also expect the sawtooth at non-power-of-two sizes ([Exercise 20.4](ch20.md) part 5).

**4. `num_warps` at 1 and 32.**

- **`num_warps=1`:** 32 threads per program. At small $d$ this is fine — there is little work to spread. At large $d$ it is badly under-parallelized; one warp must process an entire 16K-element row, and occupancy collapses.
- **`num_warps=32`:** 1,024 threads. At large $d$ this is good. At small $d$ most threads are masked off doing nothing, and the block still occupies scheduler resources — so it is slower than `num_warps=4`.

The observable: the optimal `num_warps` **increases with $d$**, which is why the lab's heuristic branches on block size, and why Triton's autotuner exists.

**5. Removing FP32 accumulation at $d = 8192$.**

*Prediction:* a small precision loss.

*What happens:* **relative error of order 10–35%** ([Exercise 20.5](ch20.md) part 1), which for a normalization layer means the entire activation is scaled by a substantially wrong factor.

The instructive part: **the kernel does not crash, produce `NaN`, or look obviously wrong.** It produces plausible numbers. A model trained with this kernel would converge to something worse, slowly, with no error message — which is the same class of silent failure as [Chapter 7](../../book/part-2-pretraining/07-cluster-reality.md)'s silent data corruption and [Chapter 11](../../book/part-3-post-training/11-sft-frontier-style.md)'s masking bugs.

That is the reason §20.8 puts numerics before performance: a fast kernel that is quietly wrong is worse than a slow one that is right.
