## 23.1 🔢 The confidence interval that invalidates the result

**1. Standard errors.** With $n = 164$:

$$\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}$$

- Yours: $\sqrt{0.671 \times 0.329 / 164} = \sqrt{0.001346} = \mathbf{3.67\%}$
- Competitor: $\sqrt{0.646 \times 0.354 / 164} = \sqrt{0.001394} = \mathbf{3.73\%}$

**2. 95% confidence intervals** ($\pm 1.96\,\text{SE}$):

- Yours: $67.1\% \pm 7.2\% = \mathbf{[59.9\%,\ 74.3\%]}$
- Competitor: $64.6\% \pm 7.3\% = \mathbf{[57.3\%,\ 71.9\%]}$

The intervals overlap over almost their entire range.

**3. SE of the difference, and significance.**

For independent estimates:

$$\text{SE}_{\text{diff}} = \sqrt{\text{SE}_1^2 + \text{SE}_2^2} = \sqrt{0.001346 + 0.001394} = \sqrt{0.00274} = \mathbf{5.23\%}$$

The observed difference is 2.5 points against a standard error of 5.23 points:

$$z = \frac{2.5}{5.23} = 0.48$$

**Not remotely significant** ($p \approx 0.63$). You cannot distinguish these two models on this benchmark.

(If the two models were evaluated on the *same* 164 problems — which they are — a paired test using McNemar's would have more power, since problems both models get right or both get wrong carry no information. That helps, and not enough to rescue a 0.48 $z$-score.)

**4. Problems needed for significance.**

Requiring $z \ge 1.96$ with a true 2.5-point gap around $p \approx 0.66$:

$$1.96 \le \frac{0.025}{\sqrt{2 \times 0.66 \times 0.34 / n}} \;\Rightarrow\; n \ge \frac{2 \times 0.2244 \times 1.96^2}{0.025^2} = \frac{1.7243}{0.000625} \approx \mathbf{2{,}760 \text{ problems}}$$

**Seventeen times HumanEval's size.** HumanEval is structurally incapable of resolving differences of this magnitude, and most reported HumanEval comparisons are therefore reporting noise.

**5. What each should have reported.**

> "HumanEval: 67.1% (110/164), 95% CI [59.9%, 74.3%], `lm-evaluation-harness` v0.4.2, 0-shot, pass@1 greedy."

And, when comparing: **"the difference from [competitor] is not statistically significant on this benchmark ($n = 164$)."**

The general practice this implies, and §23.9 states it: **report confidence intervals, and treat any difference smaller than the interval as absent.** A large fraction of published frontier comparisons would evaporate under this rule, which is precisely why it is worth applying to your own results before someone else applies it to them.

## 23.2 🔢 Prompt sensitivity

**1. The spread.**

$$78.9 - 71.2 = \mathbf{7.7 \text{ points}}$$

For comparison, the gaps reported between successive frontier models on MMLU are typically **1–4 points**. The harness-induced spread is **roughly two to eight times larger than the differences everyone is arguing about.**

That is the finding that should change how you read a leaderboard.

**2. Which variable matters most.**

**Scoring method — generation versus option log-probability — by a wide margin.**

They measure genuinely different things. Log-probability scoring asks "which of these four continuations does the model assign highest probability to," which the model can answer even if it could not produce the answer unprompted. Generation scoring asks the model to *emit* a letter, which additionally tests instruction-following and output formatting.

A model that is knowledgeable but poor at terse formatted output scores far better under log-probability. A model heavily tuned for instruction-following may score better under generation. The gap between the two methods for a single model is commonly 3–6 points.

**Shot count** is second: 0-shot versus 5-shot typically moves scores by 1–3 points, and the direction depends on how heavily the model was instruction-tuned (heavily-tuned models often lose less from 0-shot, sometimes gain).

**Option label format** is third and smallest — usually under a point — but non-zero, and it is the one that most obviously should not matter at all, which is what makes it a good demonstration.

**3. What a bare "78.9%" tells you.**

**That the model scored 78.9% under some configuration the authors chose.**

Given a 7.7-point achievable range, the number alone does not locate the model within that range, and it is not comparable to any other reported number produced by a different harness. It is, strictly, uninformative for comparison — which is the majority use of a benchmark score.

It does tell you something: the model is roughly in the "70s on MMLU" class rather than the 50s or the 90s. That is a real if coarse signal.

**4. The reporting standard.**

> **MMLU: 78.9%** — `lm-evaluation-harness` v0.4.2, task `mmlu`, 5-shot, answer scored by option log-probability with length normalization, option labels `A)`–`D)`, chat template applied, temperature 0. 95% CI [77.8%, 80.0%] over 14,042 questions.

Six things it pins down: **harness and version**, **shot count**, **scoring method**, **prompt format**, **template applied or not**, and **the confidence interval**.

Add one more rule for comparisons, and it is the one §23.4 emphasizes most: **evaluate every model in the comparison yourself, in your harness.** Never mix your numbers with numbers copied from another paper's table.

**5. Trying six formats and reporting the best.**

**You have measured your prompt search, not your model.**

Selecting the maximum over six configurations inflates the expected reported score. With a spread of 7.7 points and six draws, the expected maximum sits roughly 2–4 points above a randomly-chosen configuration, and the reported number carries a selection bias that no confidence interval on the final run reflects.

It is the same failure as reporting the best of six random seeds, and it is common enough to have a name in other fields (the garden of forking paths).

**The defence: fix the protocol before you run.** Choose the harness, the shot count, and the scoring method in advance, write them down, and report whatever that configuration gives. If you genuinely want to know the sensitivity, report **all six** — which is more informative than any one of them and is honest about the range.

## 23.3 🏗️ Detect contamination

**1. The n-gram check.**

**$n = 13$ tokens** (or 8–13 words), which is the conventional choice — long enough that a match is unlikely by chance in natural text, short enough to survive minor reformatting.

**What you compare:** for each benchmark item, take the *question* text (and separately, the *answer*), tokenize, extract all $n$-grams, and test each against an index of the training corpus's $n$-grams. Flag the item if any $n$-gram matches.

Practically: build a Bloom filter or a hash set of the corpus's 13-grams once (expensive, one pass), then query it per benchmark item (cheap). At 14T tokens the filter is the engineering problem — a Bloom filter with a 1% false-positive rate needs ~10 bits per element, so $14\times10^{12} \times 10/8 = 17.5$ TB. In practice you index a *sample* of the corpus, or shard the filter, and accept reduced recall.

Report both **item-level** contamination (fraction of benchmark items with any match) and **token-level** (fraction of benchmark n-grams matched), because a single incidental match is different from full reproduction.

**2. Concluding from 0.3%.**

**Can conclude:** roughly 0.3% of GSM8K items appear *verbatim, at 13-gram granularity* somewhere in the indexed portion of the corpus.

**Cannot conclude:** that the benchmark is uncontaminated. The check has a specific, narrow scope, and per part 3 it misses most realistic contamination routes.

The honest phrasing, and §23.5 insists on it: **"no verbatim copies found above threshold," never "not contaminated."** A 0% n-gram result is a floor on cleanliness, not a certificate.

**3. Four routes an n-gram check misses.**

- **Paraphrase.** A tutorial explaining a GSM8K problem in different words teaches the model the problem with zero matching 13-grams.
- **Translation.** The same problem discussed in Chinese or Spanish contaminates a multilingual model, and no English n-gram check sees it.
- **Reformatting.** The same numbers and entities in a table, a JSON blob, or a differently-punctuated rendering.
- **Synthetic laundering.** SFT data generated by a model that saw the benchmark — the contamination transfers with **no textual overlap at all** (part 5). §23.5 calls this the mechanism that makes contamination untraceable in a modern pipeline.

A fifth: **partial contamination**, where the *answers* appear without the questions (an answer key posted separately), which is enough to help a model that can reconstruct the mapping.

**4. Catching paraphrase.**

**Embedding-based near-duplicate detection.**

- Embed every benchmark item with a sentence encoder.
- Embed the corpus — or, realistically, index it with an approximate-nearest-neighbour structure over document or passage embeddings.
- For each benchmark item, retrieve the top-$k$ nearest corpus passages and flag those above a cosine-similarity threshold.
- **Verify flagged pairs with a judge model**: "does passage A contain the information needed to answer question B?" This is the step that controls false positives, and it is why the raw similarity threshold can be set generously.

**What it costs:**

- **Embedding 14T tokens.** At, say, 512 tokens per passage that is $2.7\times10^{10}$ passages. Even at 10,000 passages/s/GPU on a small encoder, that is 750 GPU-hours — real, and cheap relative to a training run.
- **An ANN index over $2.7\times10^{10}$ vectors.** At 384 dimensions in FP16 that is 20 TB of raw vectors before the index structure. This is the genuinely hard part and it usually forces sampling or hierarchical indexing.
- **Judge verification** on flagged pairs, which is cheap because the flagged set is small.
- **Tuning the threshold**, which is unavoidable: too low and you flag every math word problem, too high and paraphrase slips through.

**5. Catching synthetic laundering.**

The textual checks cannot help — there is no overlap to find. Three approaches, none complete:

- **Ask the generator directly.** Prompt the model that produced your SFT data with benchmark questions, few-shot, and see whether it reproduces canonical answers or — more tellingly — canonical *wording*. A model that regurgitates the exact phrasing of a GSM8K solution has memorized it.
- **Canary strings.** If the benchmark embeds one, test whether the generator can complete it.
- **Behavioural: ordering sensitivity.** Per §23.5, a contaminated model does much better with multiple-choice options in their *original* order than shuffled. Test the generator and, separately, your own trained model. A large order-sensitivity gap is strong evidence of memorization somewhere upstream.
- **Structural: prefer time-based splits.** Generate SFT data only from models whose cutoff precedes the benchmark's release, or evaluate only on benchmarks released after your generator's cutoff. This is the only *preventive* measure and it is the one worth building the pipeline around.

**6. Finding 40% overlap two weeks before launch.**

Retraining is not available. What is:

- **Do not report that benchmark.** Remove it from the model card and from any comparison. This costs a headline number and it is the only fully honest option.
- **If you must report it, disclose the contamination rate alongside**: "GSM8K 82.1%; note that 40% of GSM8K items appear in our training corpus, so this figure is not comparable to models without known contamination."
- **Report a decontaminated subset.** Score only the 60% of items with no detected overlap, state the subset size, and give the confidence interval — which will be wider ([Exercise 23.1](ch23.md)).
- **Substitute an uncontaminated benchmark** measuring the same capability — a benchmark released after your cutoff, or freshly-generated variants of the same problem structures (§23.5).
- **Fix the pipeline for next time.** A 40% contamination rate discovered two weeks before launch means decontamination is not a step in the data pipeline. Make it one, and make it run *before* training rather than before launch.

The wrong answers, both common: quietly reporting the number, and quietly dropping the benchmark without explanation. The first is misleading; the second invites the question later, when it is worse.

## 23.4 🔢 Correct a judge

**1. Three biases and directions.**

- **Position bias.** Judges prefer whichever response is shown first. If the new model was consistently placed first, the 61% is inflated — potentially by several points. Direction: **inflates whichever model was presented first.**
- **Length bias.** Judges prefer longer responses, often more strongly than humans do. The new model averages 620 tokens against 410. Direction: **inflates the new model.**
- **Self-preference.** If the judge shares a model family with the policy, it rates outputs stylistically similar to its own higher. Direction: **inflates whichever model is more similar to the judge** — usually the newer one, if the judge is newer.

A fourth worth naming: **style-over-substance** — confident, well-formatted, wrong answers score well. Direction: inflates whichever model is more heavily format-tuned.

**2. Position-bias correction.**

**Evaluate every pair in both orderings and average.**

```
For each prompt:
    score_1 = judge(prompt, A_first=new, B_second=old)
    score_2 = judge(prompt, A_first=old, B_second=new)
    result  = average of the two, with the ordering flipped back
```

If the judge is consistent, the two agree and the average is unchanged. If it is position-biased, the two disagree and the average cancels the bias to first order.

**What it costs: exactly 2×** the judge calls. On 2,000 prompts that is 4,000 calls instead of 2,000 — cheap in absolute terms, and it is the single most commonly skipped correction in published judge-based evaluations.

**Bonus diagnostic:** the *disagreement rate* between the two orderings is a direct measurement of the judge's position bias. If it is above ~10%, your judge is unreliable enough that you should consider a different one.

**3. Length: what to report, and how to isolate quality.**

**Must report:** the mean and distribution of response lengths for **both** models, alongside the win rate. A win rate quoted without the length distributions is not interpretable, because the reader cannot tell whether they are looking at a quality result or a verbosity result.

**The isolating measurement — length-matched comparison** ([Exercise 12.3](ch12.md) part 3):

- Bin responses by length (say, deciles).
- Compare new-model responses against old-model responses **within the same bin**.
- Report the win rate per bin and the aggregate over bins.

If the new model wins in every bin, the improvement is real and length is incidental. If it wins overall but ties or loses within bins, you have measured length inflation.

A cheaper approximation: instruct both models to a fixed length ("answer in at most 300 words") and re-run.

**4. Judge from the same model family.**

It **inflates the number in a way you cannot correct by averaging**, because the bias is not about presentation order — it is about the judge sharing the policy's notion of a good response.

Concretely: the judge and the policy have overlapping representations, so the judge rewards phrasings, structures, and reasoning styles the policy naturally produces. It also **shares the policy's blind spots** ([Exercise 17.3](ch17.md)) — an error category the family systematically makes is one the judge systematically fails to penalize.

**The fix:** use a judge from a **different model family** for any comparison that matters, and where possible use two judges from different families and report both. Disagreement between them is informative; agreement is much stronger evidence than either alone.

Never use your own model to judge itself against a competitor. It is the evaluation equivalent of grading your own exam, and it is common enough in published comparisons to be worth checking for whenever you read one.

**5. 53% after corrections — is the new model better?**

**First, the confidence interval.** On 2,000 prompts:

$$\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.53 \times 0.47}{2000}} = 1.12\%$$

95% CI: $[50.8\%,\ 55.2\%]$. So 53% is **marginally significant** — the interval excludes 50%, but only just, and this is before accounting for the residual biases the corrections do not fully remove.

**What I would do next**, in order:

- **Check the length-matched result** from part 3. If the new model does not win within bins, stop — the 53% is length.
- **Run a human comparison on a subsample** — 200–300 prompts, which is affordable — and check whether it agrees with the judge. If humans say 50%, the judge is measuring something humans do not care about.
- **Stratify by task category.** A 53% aggregate could be 65% on code and 45% on writing, which is a very different (and more actionable) result than a uniform 53%.
- **Check the capability regression suite** ([Chapter 11 §11.11](../../book/part-3-post-training/11-sft-frontier-style.md)). A model that wins 53% of pairwise comparisons and lost 3 points of MMLU is not an improvement.

**The honest interim answer: "marginally, and not enough to ship on this evidence alone."** A 3-point win rate over a previous checkpoint, after corrections, on a judge-based evaluation, is within the range that could be produced by residual bias. That is a fine thing to say out loud, and saying it is what §23.11 means by methodological rigour being the distinguishing skill.

## 23.5 🏗️ Build the suite

**1. The five layers.**

| Layer | What runs | Frequency | Runtime |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Smoke** | Does it generate? Stop at EOS? Follow a format instruction? No spurious role markers? Padding-invariant scoring? | Every checkpoint | < 1 min |
| **2. Regression** | Fixed capability benchmarks vs the **base model** and the previous release; safety spot-checks | Every checkpoint | 15–30 min |
| **3. Capability** | Full benchmark suite + held-out private sets + freshly-generated variants, all models evaluated in *our* harness | Every candidate | 4–8 h |
| **4. Preference** | Pairwise vs previous, both orderings, length-matched, judge for volume + human for decisions | Every candidate | 1–3 days |
| **5. Safety** | Harmful compliance and over-refusal **measured separately**, multilingual, multi-turn, plus red-team rotation | Every release | 3–5 days |

**2. The regression suite specifically.**

Contents, chosen for breadth and speed rather than for headline value:

- **Knowledge:** MMLU (a fixed 2,000-question subsample for speed), plus a private knowledge set.
- **Reasoning:** GSM8K (500-item subsample), a private math set.
- **Code:** HumanEval + MBPP, acknowledging [Exercise 23.1](ch23.md)'s power limits — used for detecting *drops*, not for ranking.
- **Long context:** a RULER-style aggregation task at the model's claimed length ([Exercise 9.5](ch09.md)).
- **Format compliance:** JSON schema adherence, explicit length instructions, tool-call syntax.
- **Safety spot-check:** 200 items, half harmful and half benign near-misses.
- **Behavioural:** stop-token rate, mean response length, refusal rate.

**Alert threshold: any benchmark down more than 2 standard errors from the previous checkpoint, or more than 3 points from the base model.** The first catches regressions; the second catches slow drift that per-step comparisons miss.

Note the deliberate framing: this suite exists to answer "**did we break something?**", not "is this model good?". Absolute values matter less than deltas.

**3. Keeping a held-out set held out for 12 months.**

The hard part, because entropy runs one way.

- **Never report per-item results** from it, only aggregates. Per-item feedback is how a team optimizes against a set without deciding to.
- **Rate-limit access.** The held-out set runs at fixed checkpoints — say, release candidates only — not on demand. If anyone can run it whenever they like, it becomes a training signal.
- **Owned by evaluation, not by the optimizing team** (part 4). The optimizing team receives a number, not the data.
- **Refresh a fraction periodically.** Retire 20% each quarter and replace with newly-authored items, so even gradual leakage has a bounded lifetime.
- **Never put it in any training pipeline**, including SFT, rejection sampling, and preference generation. This requires an explicit exclusion list checked in CI, not a convention.
- **Track how many times it has been run.** A set evaluated 200 times has been optimized against 200 times, regardless of anyone's intent.

**4. The organizational boundary.**

**Between the people who optimize and the people who measure**, with evaluation owning at minimum the held-out set and the safety evaluation.

**What it protects against, specifically:** not dishonesty — nobody decides to overfit an evaluation. It protects against the accumulation of individually-reasonable adjustments. "This prompt is ambiguous, let's rewrite it." "This grader is too strict on formatting." "This item is out of scope." Each is defensible; the sequence relocates the evaluation to wherever the model already performs.

Same structural argument as [Chapter 12 §12.13](../../book/part-3-post-training/12-reward-modeling.md) and [Chapter 13 §13.12](../../book/part-3-post-training/13-ppo-rloo-frontier.md), which is worth noticing: the book makes it three times in three contexts, because it is the same failure each time — **an optimizer will find the weaknesses of any target it can see, and a person optimizing is an optimizer.**

**5. Forty experiments, capacity to evaluate three.**

This is §23.1's argument made operational: **you are running three experiments a week, not forty**, and the other 37 are consuming compute to produce results nobody can act on.

What to do, in order of impact:

- **Build a cheap screening layer.** Not every experiment needs layers 3–5. A fast proxy — the regression suite plus a 200-prompt judge comparison, running in under an hour — can rank 40 candidates well enough to select the top 3–5 for full evaluation. The proxy will be wrong sometimes; it will be wrong far less often than picking arbitrarily.
- **Invest in evaluation throughput as the primary bottleneck.** Parallelize the harness, cache reference generations, batch judge calls, and shrink benchmark subsamples to the smallest size with adequate power ([Exercise 23.1](ch23.md) tells you what that is). Doubling evaluation throughput doubles the team's effective experiment rate; doubling training throughput does nothing.
- **Reduce the experiment count.** Forty experiments a week usually means the team is sweeping rather than hypothesizing. Ten well-designed experiments with a stated prediction each are worth more than forty exploratory ones you cannot evaluate.
- **Batch by decision.** Group experiments so that one evaluation cycle resolves one decision, rather than evaluating candidates one at a time.

The framing that makes this land with a team: **evaluation capacity is the team's actual throughput.** Everything upstream of it is speculative work.

## 23.6 🔢 Report a reasoning model

**1. Tokens and cost per query**, at 4,200 tokens/sample and $2/M output tokens:

| Setting | Samples | Tokens | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| pass@1 = 71% | 1 | 4,200 | **$0.0084** |
| maj@32 = 84% | 32 | 134,400 | **$0.269** |
| maj@256 = 87% | 256 | 1,075,200 | **$2.150** |

**2. The accuracy-versus-compute curve.**

| Tokens | Accuracy | Marginal points per 10× tokens |
|---|---|---|
| 4,200 | 71% | — |
| 134,400 (32×) | 84% | +13 points for 32× |
| 1,075,200 (256×) | 87% | +3 points for a further 8× |

**It flattens sharply after maj@32.** The first 32× buys 13 points; the next 8× buys 3. Marginal accuracy per token falls by roughly an order of magnitude across that step.

Cost per marginal point: $0.26/13 = \$0.020$ per point from 1→32; $1.88/3 = \$0.627$ per point from 32→256 — **31× more expensive per point.**

**maj@32 is the knee.** Anything beyond it is buying very expensive accuracy, and that is the operationally useful conclusion.

**3. The model card number.**

> **MATH-500: 71.0% (pass@1, 1 sample, ~4.2k output tokens/query). With 32 samples, 84.0% by majority vote (~134k tokens/query). With 256 samples, 87.0% (~1.08M tokens/query).** Evaluated with [harness+version], temperature 0.8 for sampled settings, greedy for pass@1. 95% CI on pass@1: [67.0%, 75.0%] over 500 problems.

Single-call number first, budget stated for every figure, token cost given so a reader can price it, harness named, confidence interval included. Same format as [Exercise 16.7](ch16.md) part 4 and [Exercise 19.2](ch19.md) part 4 — this is the third time the book asks for it, because it is the reporting habit most worth acquiring.

**4. A competitor reporting 86% with no budget.**

**Almost nothing can be concluded.** 86% could be:

- pass@1 from a genuinely stronger model — a large result.
- maj@256 from a model whose pass@1 is 68% — weaker than yours.
- best-of-$n$ with a verifier at some undisclosed $n$.
- A different harness, a different subset, or a contaminated evaluation.

Given your own curve, **a model with pass@1 well below yours could report 86% at sufficient budget.** The number is uninterpretable without the budget, and that is exactly why budgets get omitted.

The correct response: ask, publicly and politely, what the inference budget was. It is a reasonable question and the answer is informative either way.

**5. A field-wide reporting format.**

> For any benchmark result on a model capable of test-time compute scaling, report:
>
> 1. **pass@1** with greedy decoding — the single-call capability number, always first.
> 2. **The accuracy-versus-compute curve** at a standard ladder of budgets: 1, 4, 16, 64, 256 samples.
> 3. **Mean output tokens per sample**, so the curve can be converted to cost.
> 4. **The aggregation method** — majority vote, verifier-selected, best-of-$n$ — and for verifier-selected, what the verifier is.
> 5. **pass^k for $k = 4$** when any reliability claim is made ([Exercise 18.2](ch18.md)).
> 6. **Harness, version, temperature, and prompt format** (§23.4).
> 7. **Confidence intervals**, and problem count.
> 8. **Contamination status**: was decontamination run, by what method, and what did it find.

Points 1–3 are the ones that would change the most today. A field that reported the curve rather than a point would make an entire class of misleading comparison impossible, and it costs nothing beyond what the evaluation already computed.

## 23.7 🔢 Red teaming as coverage

**1. The core claim restated.**

**A passed red-team evaluation is evidence about the red team's coverage of the input space, not about the model's safety.** Because safety training modifies behaviour on the distribution it was trained on ([Exercise 17.7](ch17.md)), and adversarial training against a probe set teaches the model to handle *that probe set*, a high pass rate tells you what the probes covered — not what the model does elsewhere.

**2. The honest one-sentence summary.**

> "The model refused all 5,000 prompts in our automated adversarial set, which covers [N] harm categories in English, single-turn, generated by [method]; it has not been evaluated on multi-turn escalation, non-English prompts, or attack strategies outside that generator's distribution."

What makes it honest: it states what was covered and, explicitly, what was not. The sentence "the model refused all 5,000 adversarial prompts" is true and, standing alone, misleading — because a reader will hear it as a claim about the model.

**3. The coverage report.**

Dimensions to enumerate, with counts and dates for each cell:

| Dimension | Values enumerated |
|---|---|
| **Harm category** | Each policy category, individually |
| **Language** | Every deployment language, not just English |
| **Cultural context** | Regional framings, not just translations |
| **Turn structure** | Single-turn, multi-turn escalation, long-context burial |
| **Framing** | Direct, roleplay, hypothetical, professional-context, encoded/obfuscated |
| **Modality** | Text, tool output ([Exercise 18.8](ch18.md)), documents, images if applicable |
| **System prompt** | Default, empty, adversarial, customer-supplied |
| **Probe source** | Human, automated, external red team, user reports |
| **Recency** | When was each cell last probed |

The report's job is to make the **empty cells visible**. A grid where "Vietnamese × multi-turn × tool-output injection" has never been probed is a finding, and it is invisible in a pass rate.

**4. Why the probe set must rotate.**

Because a fixed probe set **becomes a training target**, whether or not anyone intends it to.

The mechanism is [Exercise 17.7](ch17.md) part 2's: the cheapest way for training to satisfy "do not fail these probes" is to learn to recognize the probes, not to remove the behaviour. Every round of adversarial training against a fixed set moves you further toward a model that is safe *on that set specifically*.

**If it does not rotate:** the pass rate climbs to 100% and stays there, the evaluation stops producing information, and the team's confidence rises while the underlying coverage does not change at all. The metric decouples from the thing it measures — Goodhart's law, with adversarial training as the optimizer.

Practically: hold out a fraction of probes that are **never** trained against, rotate the trained-against set each cycle, and treat any probe that has been used in training as permanently retired from evaluation.

**5. Preserving automated red teaming's scale despite shared blind spots.**

The problem: an automated red-teamer built from the same model family shares the target's concept boundaries, so harms the family cannot recognize are harms it cannot probe for ([Exercise 17.3](ch17.md)).

**Mitigations that keep the scale advantage:**

- **Use a different model family for the generator.** Two models trained on different corpora have less-correlated gaps. Cheap, and it directly attacks the correlation.
- **Seed the generator from human-authored probes** covering categories humans identified, then let it *expand* each seed into hundreds of variants. The human provides the coverage direction; the model provides the volume. This is the highest-value version and it preserves nearly all the cost advantage.
- **Generate in the target languages natively**, using generators that are strong in those languages, rather than translating English probes. Translated probes carry English framings and miss language-specific harms.
- **Mine real user reports** for attack patterns and expand them automatically. Deployment is an uncorrelated probe source that costs nothing to collect.
- **Reward novelty explicitly.** If the generator is itself optimized, reward probes that are *unlike* existing ones (low embedding similarity to the current set) rather than probes that succeed. Optimizing for success rate collapses onto one attack; optimizing for coverage expands.

The pattern across all five: **import an uncorrelated source of direction, then use automation for volume.** Automation cannot supply the direction, and humans cannot supply the volume.

## 23.8 🧪 Break the contamination lab

**1. Verbatim planted items, n-gram detector.**

*Prediction:* high recall, maybe 90%.

*What happens:* **near-100% recall** for items planted verbatim and longer than $n$ tokens. This is the case the detector is built for and it does it essentially perfectly.

The instructive part is the exception: items *shorter* than the n-gram length are undetectable, and short benchmark items (a one-line question) can slip through at $n = 13$. Try planting a 10-token question and watch it evade a 13-gram check entirely.

**2. Paraphrased items.**

*Prediction:* recall drops to maybe 50%.

*What happens:* **recall collapses to near zero** — typically under 10%, and often 0% for a thorough paraphrase.

This is more dramatic than people expect, and the reason is that a 13-gram match requires 13 *consecutive* identical tokens. Changing one word in every ten breaks every n-gram spanning it. A paraphrase that preserves all the information can share no 13-gram at all.

This single result is the argument for §23.5's "n-gram checks are a floor."

**3. Adding an embedding detector.**

*Prediction:* recovers most of the recall.

*What happens:* **recall recovers substantially** — typically 70–90% on paraphrased items — **and the false-positive rate becomes the problem.**

At a threshold loose enough to catch paraphrase, the detector also flags genuinely unrelated math word problems that happen to share structure ("a train leaves the station..."). On a corpus of math text, false positives can exceed true positives.

The fix, per [Exercise 23.3](ch23.md) part 4: use the embedding stage as a *recall* filter with a generous threshold, then verify flagged pairs with a judge. Measure the two stages separately — recall from the embedding stage, precision from the judge — and report both.

**4. Translated items.**

*Prediction:* the embedding detector catches them.

*What happens:* **depends entirely on whether the encoder is multilingual.**

- A monolingual English encoder: **both detectors find nothing.** Translation is invisible to both.
- A multilingual encoder with aligned cross-lingual embeddings: **the embedding detector finds them**, often with high recall, because cross-lingual sentence encoders map translations to nearby vectors by design.

This is a useful, concrete demonstration that **your contamination detector's coverage is bounded by your embedding model's coverage.** A team using an English-only encoder on a multilingual corpus has a detector that cannot see most of its own training data.

**5. False-positive rate on uncontaminated text.**

*Prediction:* near zero, since a 13-gram match is unlikely by chance.

*What happens:* **not zero, and what drives it is instructive.**

The false positives are dominated by:

- **Boilerplate and formulaic language.** "If you have any questions, please do not hesitate to" is a 13-gram that appears everywhere.
- **Common problem framings.** Math word problems share stock phrasings across textbooks that have nothing to do with the specific benchmark item.
- **Quotations and standard definitions** that appear identically in many documents.

Typical rates on natural text are low — well under 1% — but non-zero, and they cluster in exactly the domains you care about. Raising $n$ from 8 to 13 reduces them substantially; going to 20 nearly eliminates them and costs recall on reformatted content.

The practical consequence: **report the false-positive rate alongside the contamination rate**, measured on a corpus you know is clean. A "0.3% contamination" figure means something different if your detector's baseline false-positive rate is 0.25%.
